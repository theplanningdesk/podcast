Abstract 
Geopolitical risk has emerged as a major disruptive risk to global supply chains and the operations of 
multinational enterprises (MNEs) today. Drawing on a qualitative content analysis of the annual reports 
of 20 European MNEs across five industries, supported by a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) 
pipeline, this study examines how companies strategically manage geopolitical risk in their supply 
chains. We extend an established supply chain risk framework by conceptualizing geopolitical risk 
management (GRM) as a distinct strategic risk management dimension centered on reducing 
geopolitical exposure, in contrast to supply chain risk management (SCRM), which enhances 
operational resilience within existing supply chain structures. Applying this distinction, we identify 
seven key GRM strategies, including localization, decentralized procurement and R&D, direct sourcing, 
supplier diversification, geographical production diversification, circular economy initiatives, and 
political engagement. Furthermore, applying the Triple-P framework in a disclosure-based setting 
suggests that companies facing different forms of supply chain complexity across the partnership, 
product, and process dimensions disclose distinct portfolios of GRM strategies. The findings of this 
study provide cross-industry insights into the strategic orientation of large European MNEs and support 
managerial decision making to strengthen resilience in the face of supply chain and geopolitical risk. 
 
Keywords: geopolitical risk management; supply chain risk management; supply chain resilience; 
multinational enterprises; retrieval-augmented generation

1. Introduction 
Geopolitical risk has become a critical challenge for multinational enterprises (MNEs), exposing them 
to disruptions from conflicts, trade tensions, and regulatory shifts. These developments destabilize 
supply chains, restrict market access, disrupt the flow of goods and capital, and impact key supplier and 
customer relationships (S&P Global, 2025; Devesa et al., 2026; World Economic Forum, 2026). Recent 
geopolitical events illustrate the scale of these disruptions, particularly for European companies. 
Evolving trade tensions between European countries and their major trading partners, including the 
United States (U.S.) and China, are influencing global trade. For example, the Trump administration’s 
“Liberation Day” tariff measures have triggered a series of global tariff disputes aimed at reshaping the 
global trading order and encouraging domestic manufacturing in the United States, the long-term 
economic impact of which remains uncertain (Romm & Gamio, 2026). Similarly, the EU has imposed 
tariffs on electric vehicles imported from China to protect domestic markets (European Commission, 
2024). McKinsey reports that European companies are increasingly exposed to geopolitical pressures, 
facing a “double squeeze” of rising imports from China and restricted access to key export markets such

as the U.S., contributing to a $70 billion decline in the EU’s manufacturing trade surplus (Devesa et al., 
2026). These developments reflect a broader shift toward protectionism and geopolitical fragmentation, 
posing increasing challenges for globally integrated supply chains (S&P Global, 2024). 
Thus, the impact of geopolitical disruptions on supply chains is increasingly identified as a new 
subfield of research (Bednarski et al., 2023). A risk report by Allianz identifies that business interruption 
(including supply chain) risk is among the top three risks with only 3% of companies in the study rating 
their supply chains as very resilient, indicating the need for further strategic guidance and research 
insights (Allianz Commercial, 2026). Although internal operational resilience strategies and 
organizational enablers are well established in the literature (Sting & Huchzermeier, 2010; Blackhurst 
et al., 2011; Cohen et al., 2022a, 2022b), empirical evidence on how companies adapt operations and 
supply chains specifically in response to geopolitical risk remains scarce (Bednarski et al., 2023; Fan 
& Xiao, 2023; Zhu et al., 2024). Studies that do explicitly address geopolitical or political risk are 
largely quantitative, thus offer limited insight into company-level strategic decision-making, focus on 
isolated predefined responses (e.g., inventory buffers, location choices, supply chain concentration, or 
vertical integration), and largely overlook strategy cross-industry variation (Darby et al., 2020; 
Moradlou et al., 2021; Fan et al., 2022; Fan & Xiao, 2023; Belhadi et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2024; Tse et 
al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2025a). This paper aims to address this gap by answering the 
following research questions (RQs). 
RQ1: What supply chain resilience strategies do multinational enterprises disclose in their 
annual reports to manage supply chain risk? 
RQ2: To what extent are these strategies associated with managing geopolitical risk exposure? 
RQ3: How do the disclosed resilience strategies vary across industries? 
We argue that geopolitical risk management (GRM) constitutes a distinct strategic risk-
management dimension alongside conventional supply chain risk management (SCRM). Whereas 
GRM aims to reduce geopolitical risk exposure by addressing a company's dependencies on countries 
and regions, political and institutional systems, critical suppliers and intermediaries, and concentrated 
resources and technologies, SCRM focuses on enhancing operational resilience within existing supply 
chain structures. Accordingly, this study distinguishes resilience strategies according to whether they 
reduce geopolitical risk exposure or primarily strengthen operational resilience. 
To answer the research questions, we conduct a qualitative content analysis of the 2024 annual 
reports of 20 European public MNEs from France, Germany, the Netherlands, and the UK. While related 
to the work of Theile et al. (2026), who quantify companies’ risk-reduction efforts using public 
disclosures, our study takes a distinct qualitative perspective. We identify the specific strategic supply 
chain measures companies disclose to manage supply chain risk in line with country-level legislation 
mandating disclosure. Schlegel and Trent (2014) outline that around one third of key risks identified in 
annual reports have a supply chain connection or implication providing an additional foundation for

this analysis. Given that legal requirements for disclosures vary across countries, we focus our analysis 
on four European countries that require disclosures on how company risk is managed. 
Our study contributes to the fields of supply chain resilience (SCR) and GRM in five ways. 
First, the paper contributes to theory by extending Christopher and Peck's (2004) supply chain risk 
framework with a GRM dimension. In doing so, it conceptualizes GRM as the strategic management 
of geopolitical exposure and distinguishes it from conventional SCRM, which focuses on enhancing 
operational resilience within existing supply chain structures. Second, the paper develops a theoretical 
bridge between the SCR and GRM literatures by integrating established resilience concepts into the 
analysis of GRM. Third, we introduce a methodological approach that combines Retrieval-Augmented 
Generation (RAG) with qualitative content analysis to systematically retrieve and analyze company-
disclosed SCR strategies from annual reports in an exploratory setting (Arslan et al., 2024; Jackson et 
al., 2026). Fourth, the paper provides a structured overview of the organizational enablers and 
operational resilience strategies disclosed by European MNEs and applies the adapted risk framework 
to distinguish strategies that reduce geopolitical exposure from those that primarily enhance internal 
operational resilience. Lastly, by operationalizing the Triple-P framework of Cohen et al. (2022a) in a 
disclosure-based setting, the study provides exploratory evidence that companies with different supply 
chain structures appear to disclose distinct portfolios of SCR and GRM strategies. This suggests that 
the feasibility of GRM strategies may depend on the structural characteristics of a company’s supply 
chains rather than following a one-size-fits-all approach. 
The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses the theoretical background of 
geopolitical risk and SCR. Section 3 outlines the methodology of our analysis. Section 4 presents the 
results addressing the first research question, while Section 5 discusses the second and third research 
question. Finally, Section 6 summarizes our findings, offers managerial insights, outlines the study’s 
limitations, and suggests areas for further research. 
2. Theoretical Background 
2.1 The Role of Geopolitical Risk in Supply Chain Risk Management 
Prior SCRM frameworks typically incorporate geopolitical risk under environmental or external risk 
(Christopher & Peck, 2004; Schlegel & Trent, 2014; Ho et al., 2015). For instance, Christopher and 
Peck’s (2004) risk source framework distinguishes four core internal supply chain risk categories: 
supply risk (e.g., supplier failures or material shortages), process risk (e.g., production breakdowns or 
logistics disruptions), demand risk (e.g., sudden demand fluctuations or forecasting errors), and control 
risk (e.g., inadequate inventory policies or governance failures). These internal risks are impacted by 
environmental disruption risk, which captures macro-level disruptions such as sanctions, regulatory 
changes, political instability, and natural disasters (see Figure 1).

However, geopolitical disruptions differ fundamentally from other environmental risks. Unlike 
stochastic shocks such as natural disasters, pandemics, or climate related events which are non-actor 
driven, geopolitical disruption results from deliberate political actions, evolve through strategic 
interactions, and often persist over time while affecting entire regions (see Figure 1) (Schlegel & Trent, 
2014; Cohen et al., 2026). The European Systemic Risk Board (2026) outlines five dimensions of 
geopolitical risk including military conflict & war, infrastructure vulnerabilities, trade disruptions, 
capital and financial risk, and political and social factors. Caldara and Iacoviello (2022) define 
geopolitical risk as threats arising from conflicts and tensions among political actors, highlighting its 
systemic and actor-driven nature. As such, geopolitical risk is not fully captured by an event-based 
environmental risk lens. 
To address this limitation, we extend the risk-source framework by introducing the concept of 
geopolitical exposure, which captures the structural dependencies through which geopolitical risks 
affect companies (see Figure 1). Specifically, we conceptualize geopolitical exposure as a company's 
dependence on four dimensions of its external environment: (1) where it operates, referring to specific 
countries and regions in which it produces, sources, or serves markets; (2) whose political and 
institutional systems it depends on, including sanctions regimes, export controls, tariffs, and industrial 
Journal Pre-proof
5 
 
policies; (3) who it relies on within its supply network, namely critical suppliers and intermediaries; and 
(4) what critical resources and technologies underpin its operations, such as critical raw materials, 
semiconductors, software, and intellectual property. Such dependencies create vulnerabilities because 
geopolitical disruptions can impair access to critical inputs, markets, technologies, and capabilities. 
Building on Rezapour et al.'s (2018) distinction between strategic network design decisions and 
operational flow planning decisions, we conceptualize GRM as the strategic management of 
geopolitical exposure. Accordingly, GRM comprises strategic measures that reduce a company’s 
dependence on sourcing structures, production networks, supplier relationships, resource bases, and 
geographic footprints (see Figure 1) (Mao & Görg, 2020; Moradlou et al., 2021; Simchi-Levi & Haren, 
2022; Niu et al., 2025a). 
In contrast, SCRM focuses on reducing operational vulnerabilities and maintaining continuity 
within existing supply chain configurations through resilience-enhancing measures such as inventory 
buffers, demand forecasting, supplier audits, and business continuity routines (Christopher & Peck, 
2004; Schlegel & Trent, 2014; Rezapour et al., 2018). Accordingly, GRM and SCRM differ both in their 
object of management and their mechanism of risk reduction: whereas GRM seeks to reduce 
geopolitical exposure, SCRM seeks to mitigate the impact of disruptions through operational resilience 
measures. Importantly, while SCRM can reduce the consequences of geopolitical disruptions, it does 
not fundamentally alter the underlying geopolitical dependencies that give rise to such risks. 
Conceptualizing GRM as the strategic management of geopolitical exposure, rather than as a 
subset of environmental risk, extends conventional SCRM by incorporating the political and spatial 
embeddedness of global supply chains. Section 5.1 applies this perspective to structure the strategic 
measures in our analysis along the dimensions of SCRM and GRM. 
2.2 Building Supply Chain Resilience in the Context of Geopolitical Risk 
Recent research has begun to examine how companies adapt their supply chains in response to 
geopolitical risk. These studies investigate specific strategic responses to events such as Brexit, the 
U.S.–China trade war since 2018, the Covid-19 pandemic, and the Russia–Ukraine war (Mao & Görg, 
2020; Moradlou et al., 2021; Simchi-Levi & Haren, 2022; Niu et al., 2025a). The literature on 
geopolitical risk remains fragmented, as strategies are often analyzed in isolation without integration 
into a coherent resilience-based framework. To structure and synthesize this emerging body of research, 
the following section categorizes geopolitical supply chain adaptation strategies based on concepts 
associated with managing SCR, such as measures of agility (Wieland & Wallenburg, 2013; Fayezi et 
al., 2016), flexibility (Pettit et al., 2013; Fayezi et al., 2016), redundancy (Zsidisin & Wagner, 2010; 
Kamalahmadi et al., 2021), collaboration (Tukamuhabwa et al., 2015; Duong & Chong, 2020), and 
organizational enabling structures (Fayezi et al., 2016; Cohen et al., 2022b). We first review literature 
that conceptualizes these strategies in the context of general supply chain disruptions and then connect 
these insights to studies that explicitly examine company responses to geopolitical risk. Finally, we 
connect these insights to the identified research gaps that this study seeks to address.

Consistent with prior research, we view agility as an extension of flexibility (Swafford et al., 
2005; Hohenstein et al., 2015); whereas an agile supply chain necessarily incorporates flexibility, 
flexible supply chains need not be agile. In this study, however, we combine agility and flexibility into 
a single coding category, as annual report disclosures do not allow for a consistent empirical distinction 
between a company’s ability to adapt and the speed or efficiency with which such adaptation occurs. 
In general, strategies aimed at increasing agility refer to initiatives that accelerate the speed and 
responsiveness of supply chains in order to minimize the impact of disruptions (Wieland & Wallenburg, 
2013; Fayezi et al., 2016). These strategies include shorter set-up times for product changeovers, as 
well as predefined contingency plans enabling faster responses, and fewer errors during disruptions 
(Blackhurst et al., 2011; Wieland & Wallenburg, 2013; Fayezi et al., 2016). Flexibility is the built-in 
capacity to adapt to changes and disruptions efficiently, for instance, a manufacturing line that can 
convert to producing different product types without significant retooling (Ivanov et al., 2013; Fayezi 
et al., 2016). In the context of agility and flexibility in GRM, companies adapt by localizing production 
closer to key markets to mitigate tariffs, delays, and regulatory uncertainty, while also diversifying 
products and sales within existing structures (Moradlou et al., 2021; Fan & Xiao, 2023). Consequently, 
compared to conventional supply chain disruptions, agility and flexibility in GRM often require 
structural adaptations, such as localization or regionalization, rather than purely operational responses, 
such as manufacturing line flexibility. While these measures can reduce geopolitical exposure, they may 
also increase costs and reduce economies of scale. 
Strategies that focus on increasing redundancy address a supply chain’s buffers, such as 
inventory or production capacities, and the availability of backup suppliers (Sting & Huchzermeier, 
2010; Zsidisin & Wagner, 2010; Zhao & Huchzermeier, 2017; Kamalahmadi et al., 2021). In the context 
of GRM, Zhu et al. (2024) find that companies respond to higher geopolitical risk by diversifying their 
supplier and customer base, thereby reducing supply chain concentration. However, consistent with Fan 
and Xiao (2023), they find no evidence of geographical diversification; instead, companies tend to rely 
on geographically closer partners, as proximity facilitates coordination while limiting additional risks. 
While redundancy is effective in buffering temporary operational disruptions, its effectiveness may be 
more limited in geopolitical contexts, where disruptions can persist over extended periods or affect 
entire regions. Similarly, although supplier diversification is a viable risk mitigation strategy, 
maintaining excess inventory, multiple suppliers, or redundant capacity can impose substantial costs, 
requiring companies to balance resilience with efficiency. 
Collaboration strategies address whether and how supplier networks share information and 
how such sharing increases the transparency of supply chains (Pettit et al., 2013; Tukamuhabwa et al., 
2015; Duong & Chong, 2020). Examples include supplier relationship development, which aims to 
systematically evaluate, develop, and manage collaborative partnerships to reduce risk, as well as cross-
functional coordination and collaboration with non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and 
government agencies (Tang, 2006; Blackhurst et al., 2011; Sudusinghe & Seuring, 2022). In the context

of GRM, the introduction of global tariffs under Donald Trump, highlights how industry interactions 
with governments can serve as an extremely effective lever for managing geopolitical risk (Foulkes & 
Kirby, 2025). Recent research also highlights circular supply chains as a collaborative resilience 
approach, as implementing circular economy initiatives requires close coordination across multiple 
supply chain actors (Sudusinghe & Seuring, 2022). Together, these developments suggest that, in a 
geopolitical context, collaboration extends beyond traditional coordination with supply chain partners 
to include governments, regulators, and industry associations. However, the effectiveness of such efforts 
depends on the broader institutional and political environment. 
The term organizational enabler has been adopted by scholars studying the effective 
implementation of SCR strategies mentioned above (Cohen et al., 2022b). Organizational enablers refer 
to internal capabilities such as visibility, information systems, managerial readiness, and cross-
functional integration that allow companies to sense and interpret supply chain risks (Fayezi et al., 2016; 
Cohen et al., 2022b). They enhance resilience by enabling faster, coordinated responses, for example 
through supply chain digitization and dedicated SCRM functions (Cohen et al., 2022b). Consistent with 
Zheng et al. (2025), effectively managing geopolitical uncertainty requires organizational capabilities 
that enable companies to sense, interpret, and respond to emerging risks. Within this study, 
organizational enablers are conceptualized as internal capabilities that enable the effective 
implementation of both GRM and SCRM strategies by strengthening a company’s visibility, 
coordination, and risk management capabilities. 
The research introduced above provides important insights into how companies respond to 
geopolitical risk; however, three limitations remain. First, existing research is predominantly 
quantitative, limiting company-level insight into the strategic responses of companies (Darby et al., 
2020; Fan & Xiao, 2023; Fan et al., 2024; Tse et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). Second, most studies focus 
on individual resilience measures, such as localization (Moradlou et al., 2021), supplier diversification 
(Zhu et al., 2024), digital capabilities (Belhadi et al., 2024), or vertical integration (Fan & Xiao, 2023), 
providing only limited understanding of how companies combine multiple strategies into broader 
resilience portfolios. Third, although the feasibility of resilience strategies appears to depend on supply 
chain characteristics, existing studies rarely compare strategy portfolios across industries. Yet Cohen et 
al. (2022a) argue that companies require bespoke resilience strategies because supply chains differ 
substantially in product portfolio and network complexity. Our study addresses these shortcomings by 
providing a qualitative, cross-industry analysis of company-disclosed resilience strategies and their role 
in managing geopolitical exposure. 
3. Data and Methodology 
Our study applies a qualitative content analysis, following Mayring (2014), in combination with a RAG 
pipeline that leverages a Large Language Model (LLM) to examine how companies are addressing risks 
in their supply chains using data from the 2024 annual reports of selected European MNEs (Jackson et 
al., 2026; Niu et al., 2025b). All annual reports were published in early 2025, except for Infineon

Technologies and Siemens Energy. Due to their differing reporting cycles, the 2025 annual reports of 
these companies were already available in the final quarter of 2025 and were therefore included in the 
analysis. Using either the 2024 or the 2025 reports would have resulted in a temporal deviation of 
approximately five to eight months compared to the disclosure dates of the other annual reports which 
this study cannot control for. Consequently, the most recent reports available were selected. That said, 
the annual reports of Infineon Technologies and Siemens Energy are more recent and therefore reflect 
more recent risk developments and risk mitigation measures than the other reports included in the 
sample. Nevertheless, the reporting standards remained unchanged during this period, ensuring 
consistent data availability across all reports. Given the study’s aim to analyze supply chain strategy 
portfolios in the context of the most recent geopolitical risk environment, the latest available 
information was used. Figure 2 illustrates the research steps for this paper.

Step 1: Select European countries 
The first step was to identify European countries with regulatory requirements, mandating the disclosure 
of how risk is managed, ensuring comparability and availability of data for this examination. Based on 
our analysis, we focused on France, Germany, the Netherlands, and the UK, in line with the study’s 
European focus. The following national regulations govern the mandatory disclosure of how risk is 
Journal Pre-proof
9 
 
managed: Article L.232-1 of the Code de commerce [French Commercial Code] (2024), §315 of the 
Handelsgesetzbuch [German Commercial Code] (2024), Article 2:391(1) of the Burgerlijk Wetboek 
Boek 2 [Dutch Civil Code Book 2] (2024), and Section 414CB of the UK Companies Act 2006 (2006). 
These legal requirements are operationalized through the respective national accounting standards. 
Appendix A in the supplementary material provides an example of such disclosure using Henkel AG & 
Co. KGaA (2025a). In addition, France, Germany, and the Netherlands are subject to the European 
Union’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, which expands risk-related disclosures, 
particularly with regard to environmental, social, and supply-chain-related risks (European Union, 
2022). As companies are required to present a current view regarding material risks and corresponding 
strategic measures, annual reports constitute an adequate source of information for analyzing risk 
management practices. 
Step 2: Select companies 
The second step involved selecting industries and companies for analysis. Company selection was based 
on three main criteria: company size (large public companies with >€1 billion in revenue), industry 
sector, and the presence of global production (see Table 1). Focusing on large, listed, and global 
operating companies to reduce variation arising from company size and operational footprint, while 
also ensuring a consistent level of disclosure, which is often more limited for small and medium-sized 
enterprises. In addition, we followed a theoretical sampling approach aimed at maximizing variation 
across industries and replication logic to enhance analytical generalizability, in line with Eisenhardt 
(1989). Four companies were selected from each of the following industries: (i) automotive, (ii) 
chemicals and pharmaceuticals, (iii) consumer goods, (iv) industrial technology and engineering, and 
(v) semiconductors. These five globally active industries represent a diverse, and strategically important 
cross-section of the European economy and are exposed to supply chain and geopolitical risks, making 
them suitable for examining supply chain resilience strategies (Eurostat, 2021; Department for Business 
and Trade, 2025; Devesa et al., 2026). The 20 MNEs analyzed are listed in Table 1 by industry, along 
with country of the headquarters, revenue, number of employees, production sites, and countries of 
operation; the latter two indicators reflect the extent of each company’s international presence. All 
sampled companies operate global value chains, making them especially appropriate for our analysis of 
GRM. Eisenhardt (1989) suggests that 6-10 cases are ideal for analysis, however, more importantly, 
data collection should continue until theoretical saturation is reached, that is, the point at which 
additional cases yield only marginal insights. In this study, saturation was achieved at 20 cases. 
Saturation was assessed throughout the coding process. While early cases helped refine the coding 
framework and added incremental strategic insights, later cases largely confirmed previously identified 
patterns. As no substantively new strategies or categories emerged, additional reports were considered 
unlikely to alter the overall findings. Prior research in this area has relied on similar or smaller sample 
sizes, including Blackhurst et al. (2011) with seven cases, Cohen et al. (2022a) with 16 cases, Cohen et 
al. (2022b) with 12 cases, Cohen et al. (2026) with 13 cases.

Step 3 and 4: Review literature and derive initial coding guide 
To address the research questions, we employed a theory-informed qualitative content analysis with

higher-order SCR concepts to guide the analysis: agility and flexibility, redundancy, collaboration, and 
organizational enablers. These dimensions are well established in prior research and provided the initial 
analytical structure (see Section 2).

Step 7: Validate coding 
In Step 7, following Niu et al.’s (2025b) roadmap for LLM-based measurement construction, we 
complement manual coding, conducted by one coder, with an automated closed-list classification 
implemented in Python. Consistent with Mayring’s (2014) inter-coder approach, an LLM (GPT-5.4 via 
the OpenAI API, accessed 28 March 2026, temperature = 0) serves as a second coder, applying the 
predefined coding guide, strategic measure definitions, and coding units to assess classification 
consistency. Coding unit retrieval was conducted using GPT-5.2, while classification employed the 
more advanced GPT-5.4, reflecting our aim to leverage the most capable reasoning models available. 
The results show strong agreement (Krippendorff’s α = 0.85), indicating reliable consistency between 
manual and LLM-based coding (Krippendorff, 2004; Mayring, 2014). Remaining disagreement 
primarily concerned the assignment of individual coding units to specific resilience dimensions rather 
than disagreement about the presence of a resilience measure itself. All divergent cases were reviewed 
against the coding framework and resolved through manual assessment, with the human-coded

transparency and supports reproducibility of the coding process. Further details on this approach are

Step 8: Classify GRM and SCRM strategies 
In Step 8, to address RQ2, we apply the conceptual distinction developed in Section 2.1 to classify the 
identified strategic measures as either GRM or SCRM strategies. A strategy was classified as GRM 
when the disclosed measure addressed one or more dimensions of geopolitical exposure. Defining these 
dimensions is particularly important because annual report disclosures rarely refer explicitly to 
“geopolitical risk.” A strategy was coded as GRM if the disclosure indicated that the measure reduced 
dependencies related to where the company operates (specific countries and regions), whose political 
and institutional systems it depends on (e.g., sanctions regimes, export controls, tariffs, and industrial 
policies), who it relies on within its supply network (critical suppliers and intermediaries), or what 
critical resources and technologies underpin its operations (e.g., critical raw materials, semiconductors, 
software, and intellectual property). In contrast, strategies were classified as SCRM when the disclosed 
measure enhanced a company's ability to anticipate, absorb, respond to, or recover from disruptions 
while leaving these underlying geopolitical dependencies unchanged. Accordingly, the classification 
reflects whether a disclosed measure reduces geopolitical exposure or strengthens resilience within 
existing geopolitical dependencies. It does not imply that strategies exclusively serve GRM, nor that 
they cannot simultaneously contribute to operational resilience, sustainability, or efficiency objectives. 
Table D.1 in Appendix D of the supplementary material lists the conceptual rationale underlying the 
classification of all identified resilience strategies into GRM and SCRM. 
Step 9: Identify strategy archetypes 
In Step 9, to address RQ3, we perform a cluster analysis based on the "Triple-P" framework proposed 
by Cohen et al. (2022a). Developed from interview-based research with global supply chain executives, 
the framework identifies three empirically derived supply chain archetypes, partnership, product, and 
process complexity, which reflect different dominant sources of supply chain complexity and 
corresponding resilience strategies. We adopt this framework because it provides an established 
theoretical basis for interpreting differences in resilience strategy portfolios across companies. While 
Cohen et al. (2022a) derive the archetypes from interview-based assessments of supply chain 
complexity and subsequently relate them to resilience strategies, we reverse this perspective by 
assigning the resilience strategies identified in the previous coding step to the three archetypes 
according to their primary focus. Partnership strategies address interdependencies with external supply 
chain actors, product strategies relate to constraints arising from product design, technology, and 
production setup, and process strategies address complexity arising from product portfolios, 
organizational structures, and internal processes. Company-level variables are then constructed as the 
percentage share of strategies per archetype and subsequently standardized. Companies are thus 
represented by archetype-specific strategy intensities, forming a three-dimensional feature space which 
Journal Pre-proof
14 
 
is consistent with feature-based clustering approaches that rely on constructed summary variables 
(Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 1990; Kreienkamp et al., 2025). Clustering is performed using the k-means 
algorithm (Hartigan & Wong, 1979). The optimal number of clusters is determined using the elbow and 
silhouette method (Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 1990), both indicating a three-cluster solution. The clusters 
comprise 10, 6, and 4 companies. Detailed information on the clustering is reported in Appendix E in 
the supplementary material.

5. Analytical Insights and Interpretation of Results 
5.1 Managing Geopolitical Risk 
In addressing RQ2, we identify a range of resilience strategies through which companies manage 
geopolitical exposure. Building on the conceptual distinction developed in Section 2.1 and the 
classification criteria introduced in Step 8, we distinguish between SCRM and GRM. Within the 
introduced risk framework of this study, strategy categories are classified according to their role in 
managing geopolitical exposure. Figure 4 illustrates the classification of resilience strategy categories 
into GRM and SCRM. The following discussion focuses on the identified GRM strategies. Table D.1 
in Appendix D of the supplementary material summarizes the conceptual rationale for classifying all 
identified resilience strategies as either GRM or SCRM. This paper conceptualizes organizational 
enablers as strategic measures that facilitate and support the implementation of both GRM and SCRM.

Across the identified categories, GRM strategies reduce geopolitical exposure by addressing 
different dimensions of a company’s external dependencies. Within agility and flexibility, localization 
strategies and decentralized procurement and R&D functions primarily reduce dependence on where 
companies operate by geographically reconfiguring production, sourcing, and innovation activities 
while increasing responsiveness to regional institutional environments. Within redundancy, supplier 
diversification and direct sourcing reduce dependence on who companies rely on within their supply 
networks, whereas geographical production diversification reduces dependence on where production is 
concentrated. In addition, direct sourcing strengthens access to critical resources and technologies 
(what) while reducing dependence on critical suppliers and intermediaries (who). Finally, within 
collaboration, circular economy initiatives reduce dependence on what critical resources and 
technologies companies require, while political involvement and interaction with NGOs address 
dependence on whose political and institutional systems shape the business environment.

Cluster 1 in Figure 5 represents the partnership complexity archetype, comprising companies 
in automotive, industrial technology, and chemicals and pharmaceuticals. These companies operate 
complex, multi-tier supply chains and rely heavily on strategic partnerships within increasingly 
regionalized structures. To manage this complexity, they emphasize upstream digitalization and 
integrated data platforms to enhance multi-tier visibility and coordination. In addition, they seek greater 
control over critical resources through upstream integration of critical capabilities and resources. 
Companies combine standardization, decentralized procurement and R&D functions, and localization 
to enable flexible production across sites while reducing exposure to disruptions. Redundancy is 
primarily geared toward supply continuity, with inventory buffers, direct sourcing of critical inputs, and 
supply chain financing. Collaboration plays a central role as strategic partnerships and circular economy 
initiatives are leveraged to manage technological and capital constraints and secure access to critical 
materials. Overall, this archetype reflects a model in which resilience is achieved through coordination, 
control, and deep integration across complex supply networks. 
The prevalence of localization, decentralized procurement, direct sourcing, supplier 
diversification, and circular economy initiatives suggests that companies in this archetype manage 
geopolitical exposure through a broad portfolio of GRM strategies. This likely reflects their dependence 
on extensive multi-tier supplier networks, globally dispersed production systems, and externally 
sourced technologies, which expose companies simultaneously across multiple dimensions of 
geopolitical exposure. Companies combine complementary strategies that address where they operate, 
who they rely on within their supply networks, and what critical resources and technologies underpin 
their operations. Compared with the product-complexity archetype, opportunities to strategically 
reconfigure these dependencies appear less constrained by technological specialization and capital 
intensity, thereby enabling a broader portfolio of GRM strategies. 
Cluster 2 represents the product complexity archetype, including companies in semiconductors 
and industrial technology. These industries are characterized by capital-intensive production and high 
technological complexity. As a result, resilience is achieved less through structural reconfiguration and 
more through capacity management, technological control, and long-term supply and demand 
coordination. Companies emphasize upstream digitalization to manage complex production networks, 
Journal Pre-proof
26 
 
while production standardization enables scaling and load shifting across facilities. Localization plays 
a comparatively limited role, which may reflect high fixed investments, specialized labor requirements, 
and concentrated production capabilities commonly associated with these industries. Whereas 
localization emphasizes aligning production with regional markets, companies instead rely more 
strongly on geographic production diversification by spreading production capabilities across multiple 
locations, alongside strategic partnerships and long-term contractual arrangements, to secure access to 
critical technologies and manufacturing capabilities while stabilizing supply and demand. 
This archetype therefore pursues a more selective approach to GRM than the other clusters. 
The limited disclosure of localization and decentralized procurement suggests that opportunities to 
reduce geopolitical exposure by reconfiguring where companies operate are comparatively limited. 
Consequently, companies rely on a relatively narrow set of GRM strategies, primarily geographic 
production diversification, while complementing them with SCRM measures such as production 
standardization, partnerships, and long-term contractual arrangements. Rather than broadly reducing 
geopolitical exposure, resilience in this archetype appears to focus on maintaining reliable access to 
critical technologies, capabilities, and production assets within existing supply chain structures. 
Cluster 3 captures the process complexity archetype, consisting of consumer goods companies 
with broad product portfolios and strong cost pressures. These companies typically operate less asset-
intensive and more fragmented supply chains, where resilience is shaped by the need to balance 
flexibility and efficiency. Accordingly, companies focus on simplification and externalization. Product 
standardization and alternative bills of materials reduce internal complexity; while outsourcing and 
external manufacturing capacity provide operational flexibility without requiring significant capital 
investment. Organizational enablers are oriented toward downstream digitalization, improving demand 
visibility and enabling rapid replenishment in high-turnover markets. Localization strategies further 
support responsiveness to regional demand patterns. In contrast to other clusters, redundancy appears 
to be managed with a strong emphasis on cost efficiency, with companies relying more on external 
capacity than on extensive inventory buffers. Collaboration is primarily framed through circular 
economy initiatives and supplier coordination, often with a stronger focus on sustainability and resource 
efficiency than on securing critical inputs. 
Accordingly, GRM in this archetype relies less on control-oriented strategies such as vertical 
integration, direct sourcing, or extensive supplier governance and more on the ability to reconfigure 
where companies operate and who they rely on within their supply networks. Compared with the other 
archetypes, companies therefore appear to possess greater flexibility to reduce geopolitical exposure 
through supply chain reconfiguration rather than by primarily strengthening resilience within existing 
network structures. 
6. Conclusion 
Using data from the annual reports of 20 European MNEs, this study examines how companies can 
manage geopolitical exposure risk in their global supply chains. Our results identify a range of 
Journal Pre-proof
27 
 
organizational enablers and operational resilience strategies disclosed by European MNEs. Applying an 
adapted supply chain risk framework, which builds on the distinction between strategic and operational 
risk mitigation (Rezapour et al., 2018), the study differentiates strategic GRM from operational SCRM 
and identifies seven key strategies for managing geopolitical exposure. These strategies include 
localization of the value chain, decentralized procurement and R&D functions, direct sourcing, supplier 
diversification, geographic production diversification, the transition to a circular economy, and political 
involvement and engagement with NGOs. 
Clustering the identified strategies into the three supply chain archetypes of partnership, 
product, and process complexity (Cohen et al., 2022a) suggests that GRM is shaped by the structural 
characteristics of a company’s supply chain. Partnership-complexity companies appear to disclose the 
broadest portfolio of GRM strategies, whereas product-complexity companies disclose a more selective 
portfolio due to structural constraints. Process-complexity companies primarily reduce geopolitical 
exposure through localization and operational flexibility, which appears consistent with their 
comparatively lower asset intensity. Overall, the findings suggest that the feasibility of GRM strategies 
depends on the structural characteristics of the underlying supply chain, highlighting that effective 
GRM requires supply chain-specific rather than one-size-fits-all resilience strategies. 
This paper offers several practical implications for managers. First, it provides a structured risk 
framework that distinguishes between SCRM and GRM, enabling managers to systematically assess 
whether their current resilience strategies primarily strengthen operational resilience or actively reduce 
geopolitical exposure. Second, the identified organizational enablers and resilience strategies provide a 
benchmark of current practices disclosed by leading European MNEs across five industries, supporting 
managers in evaluating and extending their own resilience portfolios. Third, the proposed risk 
framework enables managers to operationalize GRM by systematically assessing geopolitical exposure 
across four dimensions. Based on this assessment, managers can prioritize GRM strategies that target 
their most significant dimensions of geopolitical exposure while accounting for the structural 
characteristics and reconfiguration potential of their supply chains (Cohen et al., 2022a). Companies 
operating complex multi-tier supply networks (partnership complexity) may need to combine multiple 
exposure-reduction strategies, such as localization, supplier diversification, and direct sourcing, to 
address dependencies across several exposure dimensions. Companies in capital-intensive industries 
(product complexity) may face greater constraints on structural reconfiguration, consistent with 
evidence that industries with high capital requirements exhibit more limited supply chain restructuring 
(Niu et al., 2025a), and may therefore need to focus on a more selective portfolio of GRM strategies. 
Conversely, companies with less asset-intensive supply chains (process complexity) may place greater 
emphasis on localization and operational flexibility to reduce geopolitical exposure through supply 
chain reconfiguration. These findings provide managers with a practical basis for prioritizing GRM 
strategies according to their supply chain structure, industry characteristics, and geopolitical exposure 
profile.

28 
 
As with any study, these findings are subject to limitations. The analysis is based on disclosures 
in annual reports of 20 large European MNEs and may not generalize to smaller or less globally active 
companies. Moreover, the data captures strategic intent rather than actual implementation. In addition, 
despite mandatory risk management disclosures, companies may withhold strategically sensitive 
information on geopolitical risk, limiting the completeness of the analysis. The binary coding enables 
systematic comparison but neglects variation in the potential impact of individual resilience strategies. 
Furthermore, the classification of strategies into SCRM and GRM is based solely on whether a strategy 
addresses one or more of the defined dimensions of geopolitical exposure. As many resilience strategies 
inherently serve multiple organizational objectives, this approach does not account for overlapping 
contributions to sustainability, efficiency, or broader operational resilience. Finally, the RAG-based 
extraction depends on the retrieval query and LLM used, potentially underrepresenting implicitly 
framed disclosures, although this is mitigated through a broad retrieval strategy and manual validation. 
Future research could extend this analysis by examining a broader set of companies, industries, 
and institutional contexts beyond Europe. Longitudinal studies could further assess how GRM and 
SCRM strategy portfolios evolve over time and how companies adapt their approaches in response to 
changing geopolitical conditions. In addition, future research could investigate how structural 
characteristics, such as asset intensity, technological specialization, and supply chain complexity, 
influence a company’s ability to reduce geopolitical exposure and the resulting trade-offs between 
resilience, efficiency, and supply chain reconfiguration. Furthermore, future research could combine 
annual report data with interviews, surveys, or internal company data to examine how disclosed GRM 
practices translate into actual implementation and managerial decision-making (Cohen et al., 2026). 
More broadly, future work may build on this study by further exploring the use of annual report data 
and LLM-assisted methods for systematic supply chain analysis (Niu et al., 2025b; Jackson et al., 2026).
