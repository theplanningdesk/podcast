Abstract
The National Hurricane Center routinely releases Tropical Cyclone Discussions that offer insights into
its tropical cyclone forecasts. This study examines the added value of these discussions for intensity
forecasting beyond a set of numerical predictors. For 637 Atlantic and eastern North Pacific tropical
cyclones from the 2002–2023 hurricane seasons, we analyze the discussion text and its combination
with 13 hurricane environmental predictors from SHIPS and ERA5 10-meter wind fields, for forecasting
intensity changes at lead times of 24 to 120 hours using three modeling methods. In one of the
approaches, we employ an end-to-end deep learning framework for modeling the text, which shows
that the discussions alone offer comparable value for 24-hour intensity forecasts to the SHIPS predictors.
Adding discussion information to the SHIPS predictors in an ensemble of five such models reduces
the mean absolute error of 24-hour maximum sustained wind forecasts for 2022–2023 cyclones from
9.89 to 8.90 kt, with a confidence interval that excludes zero when whole storms are resampled. The
ERA5 wind fields have a minor effect on mean absolute error in wind predictions, and model rankings
differ across predicted variables, lead times and model types. We also provide prediction intervals for
these forecasts, whose coverage falls below the 90% target during rapid intensification. Analysis of
the text shows that words related to intensity and intensity change (such as intensify, weaken, eyewall
and category) are important for the model predictions. This retrospective study using reanalysis data
shows the added value of the forecasters’ discussions and suggests that multi-modal learning can help
understand the predictive contribution of expert human judgment. The reanalysis data differ from
real-time analyses, and the discussions are issued at least three hours after the valid time of the other
inputs, so these results should not be interpreted operationally.
Keywords Tropical cyclones, Intensity prediction, Forecast discussions, Multi-modal learning
1 Introduction
Tropical cyclone (TC) intensity forecasting remains one of the most challenging problems in operational
meteorology due to the complex processes across many scales that govern intensification and the
nonlinear interactions between storms and their environment. Traditional approaches have provided
valuable foundations for intensity prediction. Statistical-dynamical schemes like the Statistical Hurricane
Intensity Prediction Scheme (SHIPS) use regression on environmental predictors, including sea surface
temperature and wind shear, to forecast intensity change (DeMaria et al., 2005). The GFDL hurricane
model has improved track and intensity forecasts through changes to initialization, model physics and
ocean coupling (Bender et al., 2007; 1993). Discussions written by weather forecasters for individual
storms provide a complementary source of information, combining observations, model guidance and
expert interpretation. Such discussions inform critical decisions and assist threat communication for
key National Weather Service partners (Morss et al., 2022).
In recent years, machine learning (ML) has offered new ways to extract predictive patterns from
large climate datasets. Deep learning models trained on satellite imagery can help locate cyclone centers
and classify current intensity (Do et al., 2025). Advances in large language models (LLMs) also open

• Forecaster discussion text as a predictive modality. We quantify the contribution of storm-

• Comparison across forecast leads. We report fixed modality configurations, reference baselines,

• Analysis of the text signal. We examine the signal in discussion text through integrated
gradients attribution, a TF-IDF lexical baseline, and a set of text perturbations. These analyses

are correlated within storm tracks. We compare a quantile based on pooled cases with bootstrap
calibration that resamples whole storms, Adaptive Conformal Inference with delayed feedback

Coverage is assessed empirically, without a finite-sample guarantee under storm dependence.
2 Related Work
The work most relevant to this study falls into four areas, covering established intensity forecasting,
machine learning with numerical and image inputs, meteorological text models, and uncertainty
quantification. We review these areas to identify what storm-specific forecast discussions may add to
the numerical inputs used here.
3
2.1 Traditional TC Intensity Prediction Models
Early intensity forecasting relied on statistical and statistical-dynamical approaches. For example,
SHIPS employs multiple regression with environmental predictors such as sea surface temperature,
vertical wind shear, and storm structure parameters to forecast intensity changes (DeMaria et al.,
2005). Successive versions incorporating new predictors and updated datasets showed significant skill
out to 72 hours in the Atlantic compared to climatology and persistence baselines, while dynamical
forecast systems based on numerical weather prediction models have evolved substantially over recent
decades. The GFDL hurricane model lineage, beginning with pure atmospheric dynamical cores and
progressing through ocean coupling, has demonstrated the value of improved physics parameterizations
and increased resolution (Bender et al., 2007; 1993). Modern operational systems like HWRF and HAFS
continue this evolution, incorporating advanced vortex initialization schemes and high-resolution
nested grids. Together, these traditional methods provide essential baselines and remain operationally
important, but the substantial cost of running these models motivates exploration of data-driven models
that can more flexibly incorporate diverse observations, akin to the use of data assimilation in dynamical
forecast systems.
2.2 Machine Learning for TC Forecasting
Machine learning studies differ in whether they use a single data source or combine complementary
modalities. The distinction matters here because our comparisons isolate the contribution of discussion
text within a shared evaluation design.
2.2.1 Uni-modal Approaches
Machine learning approaches are applied to TC forecasting using tabular or image data. Gradient
boosting machines (e.g., XGBoost) trained on environmental variables can capture nonlinear relation-
ships and reduce intensity forecast errors in retrospective tests (Jin et al., 2019). Convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) trained directly on satellite, infrared or microwave images of cyclones have achieved
intensity estimates with error comparable to or better than traditional methods (B.-F. Chen et al., 2019;
Combinido et al., 2018). Do et al. (2025) use geostationary satellite images to jointly locate cyclone
centers and classify current intensity. Their task concerns storm monitoring. A separate CNN approach
uses reanalysis fields for intensity forecasting (X.-Y. Xu et al., 2022).
2.2.2 Multi-modal Approaches
Recent TC studies combine different numerical or spatial inputs. D. Gupta & Arthur (2025) study
ensembles with heterogeneous datasets, while S. Zhang et al. (2025) introduce a multi-modal diffusion
model. HurriCast (Boussioux et al., 2022) encodes atmospheric reanalysis maps and combines the
learned representation with tabular predictors in a gradient-boosted tree. These studies motivate testing
whether a separately produced source of information, the NHC discussion, adds value to a numerical
model.
Other models combine images and environmental data directly. MSCAR uses causal autoregressive
attention to forecast intensity from satellite and reanalysis inputs (Wang et al., 2025). TropiCycloneNet
introduces a multi-modal dataset and a generative forecast model that combines images with environ-
mental and temporal features (C. Huang et al., 2025b). TCP-Diffusion instead predicts cyclone-associated
rainfall from past precipitation and environmental fields (C. Huang et al., 2025a). MMSTN jointly fore-
casts track and intensity (C. Huang et al., 2022), whereas MT-GN estimates current intensity through
classification and regression from satellite imagery (Zhao et al., 2024). The hybrid model of You et al.
(2025) combines rainfall images with SHIPS predictors for intensity forecasting.
4
2.3 Text Modality
While most forecasting models focus on numerical or image data, there is increasing exploration of
textual information in weather prediction. This includes not only numerical weather bulletins and
structured meteorological text, but also expert discussions, news, and public reports. Text enters weather
research as a benchmark target, a source of predictive features, or a direct input to a language model.
Our focus is the information carried by forecasters’ storm-specific prose.
2.3.1 Weather Language Models and Benchmarks
Text often enters indirectly, as a benchmark target paired with weather data. Ma et al. (2024) pair plots of
mesoscale analyses, surface observations and radar reflectivity with Storm Prediction Center discussions,
and document limitations of the tested vision-language models through benchmark tasks and expert
review. Shinde et al. (2026) present WxC-Bench, a multi-modal benchmark spanning six downstream
tasks including both TC monitoring and natural language forecast generation from NOAA Storm
Prediction Center reports. J. Chen et al. (2025) construct ClimateIQA, a meteorological visual question
answering dataset comprising 762,120 instruction samples paired with reanalysis-derived heatmaps
for extreme weather anomaly analysis. Similarly, CLLMate (H. Li et al., 2025) aligns environmental
news articles with reanalysis data and has been used to evaluate 32 multi-modal LLMs on weather and
climate event forecasting, revealing both strengths and persistent gaps.
Text is also used directly in some weather-related research tasks. Aquilon (Varambally et al., 2025)
combines language queries with encoded weather fields to study multi-modal weather reasoning.
Knowledge-guided prompt learning has also been studied for TC intensity estimation (W. Li et al.,
2025). Natural language processing has also been used to extract structured information from weather
text. For example, Lai et al. (2022) geocode historical storms, floods, and adaptation measures from
newspaper reports. However, these tasks differ substantially from the focus of this study, which uses
multi-modal learning to extract potential human insights from NHC forecaster discussions.
2.3.2 LLMs as Regressors
Recent studies have demonstrated that LLMs can serve as effective regressors through in-context
learning, eliminating the need for task-specific fine-tuning. In-context learning for numeric prediction
is when pretrained LLMs are prompted with examples to regress outputs. These approaches view
the LLM itself as a text-to-text regression engine (Nafar et al., 2025). Vacareanu et al. (2024) show
that LLMs can equal or outperform traditional regression models on various prediction tasks when
provided with appropriate in-context examples. Tang et al. (2025) study LLM embeddings as features
for regression, finding that larger models are not always better. The OmniPred framework extends
this concept (X. Song et al., 2024), training language models as universal regressors capable of precise
numerical prediction across diverse domains using textual representations of mathematical parameters.
A known limitation of LLM regressors is that discrete number tokenization and cross entropy over
tokens do not directly encode numerical distance. Continuous numerical representations (Golkar et al.,
2024) and losses that account for numerical distance (Zausinger et al., 2025) address these limitations,
and therefore performance depends on the model, representation and task.
2.3.3 Interpreting LLMs
We review interpretation methods because a central question here is which parts of a discussion the text
models respond to. Interpreting which input features drive predictions in transformer-based models
remains an active research challenge, with methods broadly falling into three categories: attention-based,
gradient-based, and perturbation-based approaches. Attention weights are often used to inspect which
tokens a model attends to. Whether they provide a faithful explanation depends on the question being
asked and on checks that connect the weights to model behavior (Bastings & Filippova, 2020; Serrano
& Smith, 2019; Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019). Integrated gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017) accumulate
5
gradients along a path from a baseline to the input. The method was developed to satisfy sensitivity
and implementation invariance, and its attributions sum to the output change from that baseline.
Perturbation-based approaches measure how the output changes when parts of the input are masked or
removed. A complementary line of work, mechanistic interpretability, aims to reverse-engineer the
internal computations of transformer models by identifying interpretable circuits, features in residual
streams, and neuron-level roles (Rai et al., 2025). While these techniques offer deeper insight into how
models process information internally, they remain nascent for domain-specific regression tasks such
as meteorological prediction.
2.4 Uncertainty Quantification
Intensity forecasts also require an account of predictive uncertainty. We distinguish general interval
methods from approaches evaluated specifically for tropical cyclones.
2.4.1 General Approaches
Split conformal prediction provides marginal coverage under exchangeability of calibration and test
scores (Vovk et al., 2005). For models with several input types, Azizi (2026a) studies calibration based
on disagreement among modality predictors or on which inputs are available. This is relevant when
discussions are missing, although its exchangeability assumptions do not directly cover dependent
cases within storms. Ensemble Batch Prediction Intervals (EnbPI) use bootstrap ensemble residuals to
form sequential intervals under assumptions on the time series error process (C. Xu & Xie, 2021). Those
results do not directly apply to our residual proxy based on a fixed ensemble. Functional Generative
Networks use learned model perturbations to generate joint ensemble weather forecasts while training
against marginal continuous ranked probability scores (Alet et al., 2025). CLEAR (Azizi et al., 2026)
calibrates regression intervals that combine aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, and Azizi (2026b)
applies a related separation to open-ended answers of language models, with conformal sets calibrated
for groups of questions identified before generation. These methods address different prediction and
dependence settings.
2.4.2 Uncertainty Quantification for TC Forecasting
For TC uncertainty quantification, researchers have studied dynamical ensembles, neural distribution
models, and conformal methods. The reliability of dynamical ensemble guidance varies by case and
verification setting (Bachmann & Torn, 2021). MTCIE predicts both a mean and a standard deviation for
intensity from satellite imagery (T. Song et al., 2024), while Fernandez et al. (2025) estimate uncertainty
in track forecasts with a neural network. Conformal methods have been applied to TC track (Meng &
T. Song, 2024) and intensity (X. Chen et al., 2025). Dependence within storms and shifts between seasons
make the exchangeability required for split-conformal coverage uncertain here, which motivates the
empirical storm-level analysis in Section 6.3.
3 Data
Our regional dataset uses TCBench storm identifiers and forecast structure (Gomez et al., 2026), with
the 13 selected predictors and initial intensity read from the SHIPS developmental data at the initial
time. These data are compiled after each season from the NHC best track and model analyses. We add
ERA5 reanalysis winds and NHC discussions for this study. The discussions are not part of the cited
TCBench release. The dataset covers the Atlantic and eastern North Pacific basins from 2002 to 2023.
It contains 637 storms (282 Atlantic and 355 eastern North Pacific) and 13,885 cases at 6 h intervals,
one per storm and synoptic time. The selected SHIPS predictors and ERA5 wind frames cover all these
storms. Section 3.2 describes how discussions are matched to cases and how many cases have a match.

3.1 Modalities
Tabular Data (SHIPS) The SHIPS dataset provides 13 meteorological variables derived for each
case and summarized in Table 2. These predictors are grouped into four categories, comprising storm
characteristics (e.g., VMAX, MSLP), environmental fields (e.g., CSST, Z850), thermodynamic variables
(e.g., EPOS, RHMD), and dynamic variables (e.g., D200, SHGC). Together, these 13 predictors capture both
the internal state of the storm and the environmental conditions influencing its intensity evolution. The
pairwise Pearson correlations among these variables are shown in subsubsection C.1.1 (Figure 11). As
expected, Vmax and MSLP are strongly anti-correlated, while most environmental predictors are only
weakly correlated with one another.

3.2 Curation
Temporal Alignment Since different modalities are recorded at different times, we specify their
timing explicitly. For each case at synoptic time t, the matching pipeline first searches for storm-specific
discussions whose issue times lie within 1 h of t+6 h. Issue times are read from the header printed in
each discussion. Routine discussions for synoptic time t are issued near t+3 h, so this window can
contain only special discussions. The pipeline prefers a discussion later than t+6 h within this window
and otherwise takes the closest one. If this search has no candidate, it chooses the closest discussion in
the archive, taking the earlier one when two are equally close. Most matches arise from this step and are
routine discussions issued near t+3 h. The pipeline then discards selections outside [t,t+7h], without
trying another candidate. Consequently, a case can remain unmatched even when another discussion
would satisfy the final time window. This matching is held fixed across the model comparisons. In
the matched data, 98% of issue times are exactly t+3 h and all offsets lie between t+3 h and t+7 h. The
matches later than t+3 h are special discussions. The discussion therefore has a later information set
than the time-t numerical inputs. This is a retrospective comparison, not an operational forecast using

from the matched-discussion population when comparing models, and assess the missingness channel
separately.
Text Processing We remove the terminal FORECAST POSITIONS AND MAX WINDS section from
storm-specific discussions. This excludes the structured official forecast table, except in two training
discussions (Appendix A), but the remaining narrative may still contain numerical intensities and
forecasts. Table removal alone does not establish the absence of information leakage. The end-to-end
neural model loader trims the bulletin header to the first datetime line, normalizes whitespace, converts
text to lowercase, and tokenizes it with the RoBERTa tokenizer. We retain up to the first 510 content
tokens of each cleaned discussion, plus two special tokens. An exploratory inference check using
length-weighted averaging of embeddings from chunks covering the full discussion showed no clear
improvement. The hybrid and temporal pipelines use the Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B sentence encoder
with its own text-cleaning procedure and the same forecast-section cutoff (subsection 4.2, subsection 4.4).
Both paths use the bounded discussion matching described above.
Normalization Numerical tabular predictors are standardized using feature statistics from the train-
ing set, with the family-specific missing-value handling described in Appendix C. ERA5 images are
normalized independently per channel (u10, v10), using the means and standard deviations of nonzero
frames from training storms. Frames that are entirely zero remain zero. Validation and test inputs
reuse these statistics. The neural models standardize each intensity-change target using statistics from
the training set for the multi-output MSE loss, and predictions are transformed back to knots or hPa
for evaluation. The hybrid XGBoost and Ridge regressors fit the intensity-change targets directly

in physical units. For fixed predictions, inversion of a common affine target transformation leaves
target-specific R2 unchanged. Scaling targets during neural training nevertheless changes the relative
weighting of the two losses.
4 Methodology
This section describes our approach to multi-modal tropical cyclone intensity prediction. We begin
by formalizing the prediction task, then present three complementary modeling frameworks. These
comprise a hybrid architecture combining deep learning encoders with gradient boosting, an end-to-end
neural network with learnable fusion, and a temporal extension that models storm evolution over
consecutive timesteps. We conclude with training strategies common to all approaches.
4.1 Problem Formulation
For each case at synoptic time t, we use the available tabular (T), image (I) and discussion text (D)
features to predict the change in storm intensity after h hours, where h∈{24,48,72,96,120} and cases
occur at 6 h intervals. For the full multi-modal ablation we evaluate all uni-modal (T, I, D), bi-modal
(T+I, T+D, I+D), and tri-modal (T+I+D) combinations. The prediction targets are
ΔVmax,h(t)=Vmax(t+h)−Vmax(t), ΔMSLPh(t)=MSLP(t+h)−MSLP(t),
Here Vmax is maximum sustained wind and MSLP is minimum sea-level pressure. Models are trained
and scored on these changes. Absolute future intensity is reconstructed by adding the predicted change
to the dataset intensity at time t. Models containing tabular inputs also receive Vmax(t) and MSLP(t)
among their numerical predictors. We emphasize 24 h results because longer leads have fewer valid
future intensities. A forecast initialized when the discussion was issued would require a new timing
and verification protocol.
4.2 Hybrid Approach
Discussion text, reanalysis fields, and tabular predictors are not jointly available for every case. The
hybrid approach in Figure 5 trains or supplies separate encoders, then fits a regressor to their fixed-
length outputs. Because the encoders are fixed, each combination of inputs only requires refitting the
regressor. Raw XGBoost feature importance scores over 13 tabular variables and much larger image and
text embedding blocks are not comparable measures of modality contribution. Correlated predictors
can also distort feature importance (Nicodemus et al., 2010). We therefore assess added predictive value
by fitting the model with and without a modality and comparing errors on the test seasons, not by raw
feature ranks. With correlated modalities this measures the value added beyond the other inputs, not
the total information in a modality.
Inspired by Boussioux et al. (2022), we pretrain a CNN encoder on ERA5 wind fields with a self-
supervised objective combining next-frame embedding prediction and frame reconstruction (subsec-
tion C.5), and use the trained encoder to produce 128-dimensional image embeddings. We repeat
the full hybrid training process with five random seeds. In each run, we first train a CNN, then use
it to extract image features for the regressor without further updating the CNN. We also explored
DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024), pretrained on natural images, but did not retain it for the reported experi-
ments (subsection C.4). For text encoding we use the pretrained Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Y. Zhang
et al., 2025) sentence encoder without fine-tuning, retaining the first 768 dimensions of its cached
output. An XGBoost gradient boosting model then fuses the raw 13 tabular SHIPS predictors, the image
embeddings, and the text embeddings into a single intensity prediction.

4.3 Deep Learning Approach
We also propose an end-to-end neural network approach in Figure 6, optimizing the trainable modality
branches and fusion jointly through backpropagation while using an externally supplied, frozen text
backbone. Each modality is encoded into a shared 64-dimensional embedding space ℝ64 before fusion.
The tabular encoder processes the 13 SHIPS predictors through a three-layer MLP (13 →128 →64 →
64) with LeakyReLU activations, layer normalization, and dropout (p=0.3). The image encoder extracts
spatial features from the ERA5 wind fields (𝐱img ∈ ℝ2×41×41) using a CNN with three convolutional
blocks (batch normalization, ReLU, max/adaptive-average pooling) of increasing channel depth (32 →
64 →128), followed by fully connected layers that compress the spatial representation to 64 dimensions.
The text encoder consumes the embedding of the [CLS] classification token from a RoBERTa-base
backbone (Liu et al., 2019) (ℝ768), used as a frozen feature extractor, and projects it to ℝ64 through a
two-layer network with GELU activation. We compare generic pretrained RoBERTa with forecast-tuned
RoBERTa. The forecast-tuned backbone is first trained in the discussion-only (D) model for the same
seed and lead time, then frozen during fusion training. Both regimes train the text projection and fusion
head. The forecast-tuned regime therefore uses a two-stage procedure. Predictions depend on both the
fusion model and its frozen text backbone. Unless stated otherwise, the neural text-fusion models (T+D,
I+D and T+I+D) use the forecast-tuned backbone.
We investigate two fusion strategies. Concatenation fusion stacks the normalized modality embed-
dings into a vector of dimension 64M, where M is the number of configured modalities. Attention-based
fusion projects each embedding to 256 dimensions, adds a learnable [FUSION] token and modality-
position embeddings, and applies a single pre-LN Transformer layer with four attention heads. Layer
normalization precedes the attention and feed-forward sublayers. The [FUSION] output supplies the
fused representation. This architecture can learn interactions between modalities, but the fitted com-
parisons do not identify a specific weighting mechanism. A linear head produces 2H intensity-change
predictions, whereH is the number of lead times. Full specifications are given in Appendix D.
4.4 Temporal Approach
In a standard setting, both the hybrid and end-to-end approaches treat each case independently. To
capture the temporal evolution of storm intensity, we extend the HurriCast framework (Boussioux et al.,
2022) with multi-modal inputs (Figure 7). A shared CNN encoder processes ERA5 wind fields at each of 8
consecutive 6-hourly timesteps (t−42 h to t), producing 128-dimensional image embeddings followed by
layer normalization. At every timestep, these embeddings are concatenated with the 13 tabular SHIPS
predictors and, optionally, the 768 retained dimensions of the pretrained Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Y.
Zhang et al., 2025) sentence embedding, projected to 64 dimensions by a trainable linear layer with
GELU and layer normalization. The full configuration forms a sequence of shape 8×205. At each time
step, the temporal model uses the discussion matched to the case at the ERA5 frame timestamp. Missing
discussions receive zero embeddings. The matched discussion can have a later issue time than that
timestamp, following the rule in Section 3.2.
A 2-layer GRU decoder models the temporal evolution of this sequence, producing 128-dimensional
hidden representations that are flattened and projected through fully connected layers. The resulting
128-d embeddings can either be used directly for prediction via a linear head, or serve as features for an
XGBoost ensemble, providing a second hybrid pathway complementary to the approach in Section 4.2.

4.5.2 Training Details
We train the neural models with AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), using learning rates specific
to each modality, batch sizes and early stopping based on validation loss. The primary 24 h runs use
patience 7 and epoch caps of 200 for T, I and T+I and 500 for D and the models using text fusion. The
configuration details and their selection limits are given in Appendix E.2. Learning rates decrease on a
validation plateau (factor 0.5, patience 3), and gradient norms are clipped at 1.0. In the 24 h multi-modal
ablations that include text, linear weights are reset with Kaiming normal initialization (Appendix E.3).
Our temporal split uses 2002–2019 for training, 2020–2021 for validation, and 2022–2023 for testing. We
retain rows with at least one valid 24 h target. The split avoids train–test storm overlap, but it does not
remove the later discussion timing or the retrospective nature of ERA5. Numerical inputs and targets
are standardized as described in Section 3.2.
4.6 Baselines and Evaluation
Tables of fitted models report means across five training seeds, with sample standard deviations where
shown, unless stated otherwise. In comparative tables, bold and underlined values indicate the best
and second-best displayed scores. Paired comparisons use identical cases, whereas Table 7 summarizes
scores from models evaluated on different sets of cases. Ties at the reported precision share emphasis.
A higher ranking alone does not establish a statistically significant difference. For paired comparisons
of forecast errors, we obtain 95% confidence intervals by resampling whole storms (Appendix N.4).
We compare our multi-modal approaches against baselines of increasing complexity (Table 3).
We use three simple reference baselines. Persistence predicts zero intensity change. Trend persistence
extrapolates the observed past change over the same lead time, Δ̂yt,h =yt −yt−h. Climatology predicts
the mean change in the training set for each target and lead time. The trend baseline requires an exact
timestamp match within the same storm at t−h, rather than a fixed number of preceding rows, and falls
back to zero change otherwise. It performs worse than persistence and climatology here (Table 15). This
result applies to the extrapolation rule and does not show that past intensity changes are uninformative.
We then add Ridge regression and XGBoost in three modality configurations, comprising tabular only (T,
the 13 SHIPS predictors), discussion only (D, the 768-d Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B embedding truncated
from the native 1024-d output), and T+D. A tabular-only neural network additionally provides the neural
reference. The multi-modal TC studies reviewed above use different datasets, targets and evaluation
periods, so their reported scores are not direct baselines for our experiment.

comparisons with D need a common subset. For 24 h Vmax directional accuracy (DAcc), a forecast is

steady only when it is exactly zero. Observed classes come from the original targets in physical units,

subsection J.1 gives the ensemble confusion matrices under this rule and a supplemental convention
that treats predictions within a fixed±2.5 kt band as steady.
5 Results
5.1 Modality Ablation
At 24, 48 and 72 h, Table 4 reports R2 and Table 5 reports MAE. In the primary 24 h comparison, adding
forecaster discussions to the tabular neural model gives a clear gain. Average MAE for wind change
falls from 10.13 to 9.46 kt, a 6.6% reduction on the same 1,150 cases. Wind R2 rises from 0.44 to 0.53.
These scores are averages over five training runs, and Table 11 gives their variation.
The wind fields carry predictive information on their own, with mean R2 =0.22, but adding them
to tabular inputs in the neural model changes the mean score only slightly. Adding wind fields to the
model using tabular inputs with discussions changes mean wind MAE from 9.46 to 9.45 kt, less than the
variation across training runs. This limited contribution is in line with the findings of Boussioux et al.
(2022), whose image-based configurations provided smaller improvements for 24 h intensity forecasts
than for track forecasts in the same basins. Our result concerns the two near-surface wind fields and
training configurations used here. Richer images, a different image representation or further tuning on
the validation set could still help. The ordering also varies across targets and forecast leads, as the full
tables show.
5.2 Temporal Model Results
Table 6 presents temporal configurations on 834 cases from 55 test storms that meet the eight-frame
sequence requirement. In the modality ablation, XGBoost over T+I+D embeddings has mean wind MAE
9.71 kt versus 10.56 kt for T+I. The corresponding paired ensemble gain is 0.59 kt, with a whole-storm
95% interval of [−0.11,1.33] kt that includes zero, as does the pressure interval. These results do not
establish a clear improvement from adding discussions to the temporal model. The full temporal model
has two prediction variants, labeled T+I+D (GRU) and T+I+D (GRU+XGB) in the table. The first predicts
directly from the temporal embedding. The second fits XGBoost to that embedding together with
tabular and discussion features from the last time step. In contrast, the modality ablation rows use only
the temporal embeddings as XGBoost inputs. Neither comparison isolates the causal contribution of
spatial wind structure.
5.3 Overall Performance
Table 7 places the fitted approaches side by side. In the primary comparison, the neural networks using
tabular inputs with discussions have the lowest mean wind and pressure MAE, with or without wind
fields. These two models differ by less than the variation across training runs, so the scores do not
separate them.

6 Model Analysis
6.1 Detecting Subjectivity
To describe explicit markers of personal judgment in the discussions, we classify each NHC discussion
into three rhetorical categories. An opinionated discussion makes an explicit first-person subjective
statement (“I think”, “it was hard for me to ignore”). A balanced discussion has no first-person
language but sustains interpretive reasoning across physical mechanisms or competing numerical
weather prediction models. A factual discussion has neither. We labeled a stratified benchmark
with Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026b) as LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al., 2023). Claude Opus 4.6
(Anthropic, 2026a) then labeled a subset using the same rubric without seeing the Sonnet labels. On
the benchmark, a regular expression (regex) that detects first-person language reproduces the judge’s
opinionated label, so we use it as a proxy for explicit first-person framing in the full corpus. The
two judges disagree more often on the balanced/factual boundary, where reasoning depth varies
continuously. An exploratory hybrid classifier combines the regex with a few-shot (Brown et al., 2020)
gemma-4-E2B-it model (Gemma Team, 2026). Its agreement with one LLM judge is descriptive, not
independent human validation. Appendix L gives the counts, rubric, regex patterns and audit.
We use these labels only to compare performance across groups of discussions, as the forecasting
model takes the cleaned text through its encoder (Section 4.3) rather than the assigned classes. On
cases with matched discussions, adding text to the tabular ensemble reduces wind MAE by 1.07 kt, with
an improvement also seen in the larger group without first-person statements detected by the regex.
The gain is therefore not confined to explicit first-person language, although the small group flagged
by the regex has negative point gains, with wind and pressure confidence intervals that both include
zero (subsection N.4). These comparisons describe performance within each group without testing
differences between groups or isolating the causal contribution of factual content, interpretation, or
judgment.
6.2 Interpretability
To inspect how individual trained models respond to the discussion text, we apply Integrated Gradients
(IG, Sundararajan et al., 2017) via the Captum library (Kokhlikyan et al., 2020) to the trained T+D and
discussion-only (D) models. IG attributes the model’s prediction to individual input embeddings by
integrating the gradient of the output with respect to the embedding along a straight-line path from
a baseline (here, the pad-token embedding) to the actual token embedding. Token-level attributions
are summed within tokenizer-defined word spans and sentences so that attributions can be reported
for words or sentences that have a meteorological interpretation. To measure where text is associated
with improved predictions, we compare absolute error for each case between the tabular-only and
T+D ensembles on the test set (Appendix N.4) and use IG only to characterize a fixed set of illustrative
predictions from individual models. Appendix M details the attribution pipeline, the IG completeness
check, and worked examples on a discussion-only rapid intensification case (Tropical Storm Ian,
Discussion 10), a T+D case where discussions improve the prediction and include explicit forecaster
judgment (Hurricane Agatha, Discussion 9), and a T+D case where discussions worsen the prediction
despite cautious forecaster wording (Tropical Storm Madeline, Discussion 7).
6.3 Uncertainty Quantification
Point forecasts do not convey forecast uncertainty, so we construct 90% prediction intervals without
retraining the forecasting models. Cases within a track are dependent, so each split holds out whole
storms. For the primary T+D ensemble, we normalize residuals by the ensemble spread and calibrate the
intervals by resampling whole storms. Across the paired splits used for comparison with CLEAR (Azizi
et al., 2026), wind coverage is 0.917 and pressure coverage is 0.916. The median wind width is 39.46 kt.
The wind interval score is 57.34, versus 62.30 for the tabular model under the same construction, where
18
lower is better. These are empirical coverage and score measurements, not guarantees. Appendix I
reports the online methods, conditional results, and a separate analysis across forecast leads.
Marginal coverage is not sufficient for every storm or intensity regime. On the observed rapid
intensification subset, every evaluated interval method falls below the 90% nominal level (Figure 15).
On Otis, the T+D interval misses the largest changes despite covering most of that storm’s other cases
(Appendix I). The full method comparison, per-lead results, storm panels and conditional analysis are
reported in the appendix.
7 Discussion
7.1 Comparing Approaches
The three model families use different encoders and are not always evaluated on the same cases. On all
1,150 valid 24 h test cases, adding discussions to hybrid XGBoost reduces mean wind MAE from 9.88 to
9.49 kt, compared with9.46 kt for the neural T+D model.
The temporal model is evaluated on the 834 cases that meet its sequence requirements, so a fair
comparison requires evaluating the other models on this same subset. On these cases, neural T+D has
mean wind MAE 9.47 kt, compared with 9.68 kt for the full temporal model with XGBoost. subsection J.3
reports the corresponding pressure and matched-discussion scores, distinguishing variation across
training runs from uncertainty in paired ensemble comparisons. Where a confidence interval for the
difference includes zero, the comparison remains inconclusive and does not establish that the models
are equivalent.
These approaches support different research questions. The hybrid approach permits rapid regressor
and modality comparisons, neural fusion jointly optimizes its trainable branches, and temporal models
condition on a sequence of past storm states.
7.2 Impact of Text Sources
The text results concern storm-specific discussions, which contain observations, guidance summaries
and forecaster assessments tailored to a cyclone. On its discussion-matched test population, D reaches
a mean wind R2 of 0.50 over five training runs (Table 11). A lexical TF-IDF model also predicts intensity
change, with wind R2 =0.41 (Table 33). Across five trained D models, masking numeric tokens slightly
lowers mean wind R2 (ΔR2 = −0.014), while removing sentences matched to intensity vocabulary
produces a much larger decrease (ΔR2 =−0.364) (Table 34). The latter mask overlaps with an outlook-
sentence mask, changes the text length and can expose later tokens after truncation. These probes
show sensitivity to the specified wording and quantify variation across five training seeds, but do not
isolate a semantic mechanism or rule out the later timing of discussions. A separate check on discussion
availability is reported in subsection J.2.
7.3 Fusion Strategy
Prior work motivates studying modality competition and gradient imbalance (Y. Huang et al., 2022;
Peng et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022), but these experiments do not isolate the effect of initialization or
show whether the modalities learn complementary representations during training.
The primary fusion operator is specified for each modality combination and forecast lead. At 24 h,
T+I, T+D and T+I+D use concatenation, while I+D uses multi-head attention. At 48–120 h, T+I and I+D
use attention, while T+D and T+I+D use concatenation. Joint multi-lead models use concatenation.
These choices are held fixed when comparing generic and forecast-tuned text backbones, and forecast
tuning does not provide a uniform improvement (Table 27). Additional configurations assess sensitivity
to the fusion operator (Tables 27–29).

7.4 Error Analysis
Rapid intensification remains difficult in the evaluated cases. In the discussion-only Ian example
(subsection M.4), the attributed checkpoint predicts +26.1 kt against an observed +30 kt change over
24 h. Other large changes, including the Otis case, remain underpredicted. A successful local example
therefore cannot establish reliable performance for rapid intensification, as the conditional interval
results in Appendix I also show.
7.5 Limitations
Scope. We study storms in the NHC area of responsibility, the Atlantic and eastern North Pacific
basins (2002–2023), at 6 h resolution, which may miss short rapid intensification episodes and limits
generalization to other basins or earlier periods. We address intensity, not track, and do not incorporate
the Public Advisory or Forecast/Advisory products as additional text sources. Since ERA5 reanalysis
fields and temporally matched forecaster discussions are used as inputs, the study is best read as a
retrospective study of predictive information rather than a directly deployable operational system.
Operational readiness. The forecast errors remain substantial for rapid intensification and landfall
decisions. The ATCF comparison (Appendix H) includes the official NHC forecast, which remains
more accurate than T+D on the same forecasts (MAE 7.69 vs. 8.97 kt), and HWRF, HAFS, LGEM and
decay-SHIPS guidance on each aid’s available rows. It is not a complete verification of the operational
guidance suite, which also includes consensus aids such as IVCN and HCCA. Our reanalysis inputs, best-
track-based initial intensity and discussions issued after the synoptic time differ from the information
available to those systems. The comparison provides retrospective context and does not establish
operational equivalence.
Model comparisons. Adding a modality does not uniformly improve prediction under the training
configurations. The image, tabular and text configurations use different learning rates and batch sizes,
and the fusion choices inherited from generic text features need not be optimal for the forecast-tuned
backbone. These comparisons therefore assess specified models, not the maximum information available
in each modality. Checkpoint and fusion configuration selection used validation results. Additional
baseline methods were included after the initial test evaluation, so the set of comparisons was not
fixed in advance. The storm-bootstrap intervals condition on the fitted models and do not include
uncertainty from tuning or model selection. Evaluation on later seasons with the modeling choices
fixed beforehand would provide a further test of generalization. The temporal model also requires a
sequence of valid past ERA5 frames, which changes its evaluation population. Comparisons on identical
cases (subsection J.3) address that population difference without removing every training difference.
Robustness of the text signal. Storm-specific discussions align to most but not all cases. A
missingness-indicator baseline and pre-landfall stratification (subsection J.2) assess sensitivity to discus-
sion availability without excluding interactions between missingness and other inputs. The post-landfall
point gain is larger but less precise, and the selected SHIPS predictors include latitude and longitude
but no explicit variable for distance to land. The newer Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B encoder may have
encountered hurricane reporting from the test period during pretraining. The original RoBERTa-base
predates those test seasons. The TF-IDF baseline (subsection N.1) shows that lexical features have
predictive value without pretraining a language model. The perturbation probes instead measure the
sensitivity of five pretrained discussion-only checkpoints. Neither analysis quantifies the effect of
possible Qwen pretraining exposure. The retained narrative can state the official intensity forecast
and refer to statistical or consensus guidance, so part of the text signal may be the official forecast
itself. Only a small number of test discussions are flagged opinionated by the first-person regex, so a
differential effect of explicit forecaster judgment cannot be isolated at the present sample size.

8 Conclusion
MultiCyclone evaluates NHC discussions alongside tabular meteorological inputs and ERA5 wind
fields for retrospective prediction of tropical cyclone intensity. On the 2022–2023 storms held out
from training, neural networks using tabular inputs with discussions reduce average 24 h wind MAE
from 10.13 to 9.46 kt across five runs. Averaging the predictions of those runs gives an ensemble MAE
reduction of 10.0% on identical cases, with a paired whole-storm interval excluding zero. The benefit
varies with architecture, lead and evaluation population, and adding the available wind fields leaves this
configuration’s mean wind MAE essentially unchanged. Attribution and perturbation analyses identify
sensitivity to language about intensity and its evolution without establishing a unique causal explanation.
These findings support further study of forecaster discussions beyond the selected numerical inputs.
Reanalysis inputs, discussions issued after the valid time of the tabular and image data and forecast
statements in their narrative limit operational interpretation. Interval coverage also varies across
storms and does not establish a general guarantee. An evaluation on a season not used during model
development should therefore test these models with richer operational inputs and data available at
forecast issue time, followed by calibration across seasons and evaluation in additional basins.

Appendix C Data Processing Pipeline
C.1 Tabular Data Preprocessing
The tabular input consists of the 13 SHIPS predictors in Table 2. SHIPS values are extracted at the
initial time. Latitude and longitude are expressed in degrees. For each storm, a target at lead time h is
formed by subtracting the initial intensity from the intensity at exactly t+h when it is available. Storm
identifiers, timestamps, discussion filenames, and columns containing future targets are excluded from
the predictor vector. The models use these 13 predictors without additional cyclical time encodings,
lagged predictors, interaction terms, or outlier clipping.
The dataset contains 74 missing TWAC values. The other 12 predictors contain no missing entries.
In the end-to-end loader, missing numerical inputs are replaced by -999 before transformation by the
feature scaler. The scaler itself is fitted on available training values before this replacement. In the
hybrid and temporal loaders, missing inputs are replaced by zero before fitting the scaler. Each fitted
32
scaler is then applied unchanged to validation and test inputs. The chronological split uses cases before
2020 for training, 2020–2021 for validation, and 2022–2023 for testing. Target availability is handled
separately from input missingness. Absent future intensities are not imputed. Neural losses use target
masks, and per-lead hybrid and temporal datasets require the targets needed by the fitted model.

C.3 Image Representation Comparison
The retained ERA5 inputs use the horizontal wind components u and v. They were obtained from
aligned magnitude/direction fields through u = |V |cosθ and v = |V |sinθ, where |V | =
√
u2 +v2
and θ = arctan2(v,u). The coordinate change does not add meteorological information. Direction is
undefined at zero wind speed, while both reconstructed components are zero. All reported predictions
use the fixed u/v representation. A controlled comparison of predictive performance under the two
representations remains future work.
Physically, 10 m surface winds are the most natural image representation for Vmax, which is itself a
maximum sustained surface wind speed. However, ERA5 strongly underestimates the maximum winds
of intense cyclones (Dulac et al., 2024), so these fields mainly describe the broader circulation. The
tabular inputs include a generalized wind-shear measure (SHGC) and upper-level divergence (D200).
Their presence does not establish that these predictors contain all useful information from upper-air
images. An exploratory comparison with ERA5 850 hPa u/v wind components, with or without 850 hPa
geopotential height, is left for future work.
C.4 Image Encoder Choice
For the hybrid image branch we use a CNN trained on ERA5 wind fields. It produces 128-dimensional
embeddings using the self-supervised objective described in subsection C.5. We also explored DINOv2-
ViT-S/14 (Oquab et al., 2024), a vision foundation model pretrained on natural images. These exploratory
experiments did not provide a useful alternative in our pipeline, so we did not retain DINOv2. All
reported hybrid results use the CNN trained on ERA5 wind fields.
C.5 Hybrid CNN pretraining objective
The hybrid image encoder is trained self-supervised on ERA5 frames alone, without any Vmax or MSLP
target, so that image embeddings can be produced even for frames that lack matched intensity labels.
Optimization uses only frames from 2002–2019 storms, with checkpoint selection on 2020–2021 storms.
Pretraining uses learning rate 5×10−4, batch size 256 and an epoch cap of 50, repeated independently
with five seeds. In each run, the CNN and hybrid regressors use the same random seed. Frames from
the 2022–2023 test storms are excluded from pretraining. Two objectives are optimized jointly. (i) A
next-frame prediction objective uses a small head that reads the encoder’s embedding of frame t and
predicts the embedding of frame t+1 of the same storm, using one minus cosine similarity to a detached
next-frame embedding as the loss. (ii) A frame-reconstruction objective uses a lightweight decoder
that reconstructs the input frame from the encoder embedding under a pixelwise MSE loss. The two
losses are summed with a weight of 0.5 on the reconstruction term. After pretraining the predictor and
the decoder are discarded. The 128-dimensional encoder output is passed to the XGBoost fusion head
together with the 13 raw SHIPS predictors and the text embeddings from the sentence encoder.
The hybrid and temporal CNNs differ from the end-to-end image encoder. They use three 3×3
convolutional layers with channel widths 64,64,256, BatchNorm and ReLU, max pooling after the
second and third layers, and average pooling to 4×4. Their FC path is 4096 →576 →256 →128, with
Linear–BatchNorm–ReLU in its first two blocks. Each hybrid run uses its own fixed pretrained encoder.
The temporal models train their own initially random CNN and GRU.

C.6 Text Data Processing
Storm-specific discussions are joined to cases using the bounded matching rule in subsection 3.2.
Matched discussion issue times occur 3–7 hours after the synoptic time in the dataset, usually three
hours. Discussions for unmatched cases remain missing. Fused models and the hybrid and temporal
text-only regressors use a zero text vector, and the neural discussion-only model omits those cases.
The end-to-end RoBERTa path trims the bulletin header to the first datetime line, removes the
FORECAST POSITIONS AND MAX WINDS section and all following lines, removes null/control and
non-ASCII characters, collapses whitespace, and lowercases the retained text. Its tokenizer truncates
and pads to a total sequence length of 512 tokens, including special tokens, and supplies an attention
mask. Narrative numerical predictions may remain in the retained discussion.
The frozen Qwen path uses the same forecast-section cutoff but otherwise preserves the discussion’s
letter case, bulletin header, and line structure before encoding. The cache stores 1,024-dimensional
normalized embeddings. The hybrid and temporal models use the first 768 coordinates, without
an additional normalization after this truncation. Missing or empty matched inputs produce zero
embeddings. These two text preprocessing paths are distinct.

E.3 Initialization
For the 24 h multi-modal ablations that include text, linear weights in the constructed model are reset
with Kaiming normal initialization and their biases to zero. LayerNorm scales and biases are reset
to one and zero. Other module classes, including convolutional and BatchNorm layers, retain their

fusion training and is not reset. Standalone D starts fine-tuning from its pretrained initialization. The
per-lead and joint configurations retain their separately specified initialization settings.
E.4 Computational Resources
The neural experiments were run on the University of Lausanne DCSR Curnagl high-performance
computing cluster as single-GPU NVIDIA A100 40 GB jobs. Exploratory runs on other GPU architectures
are separate from the main comparison. Models are implemented in PyTorch 2.x. The end-to-end neural
protocol uses fp32, disables TF32, and requests deterministic operations, including a deterministic
replacement for the adaptive-pooling backward pass. Some deterministic checks are configured to warn
rather than fail, so these settings do not by themselves establish universal bitwise reproducibility. The
reported temporal models use a controlled runtime with deterministic algorithms enforced, cuDNN
benchmarking disabled and TF32 disabled. The software settings of the earlier temporal runs were not
fully recorded, so exact reproduction of those runs is not established. The supplementary material pro-
vides predictions and reproducibility information. Fused text models require both the fusion checkpoint
and its external frozen RoBERTa backbone, using generic pretrained weights for generic text, or the
corresponding trained D backbone for forecast-tuned text. Transformer attributions are computed on
the GPU.
A temporal alignment check compared this timestamp-based construction with an earlier imple-
mentation that used preceding cases and could pair an image with an older discussion when synoptic
times were missing. Both versions were trained under the same runtime settings, including models
without text as controls. The reported temporal results use the timestamp-based construction.
Reproducibility checks distinguish rerunning inference from retraining. Before the discussion issue
times were corrected, CPU inference was repeated for 246 of 351 neural and fusion-control entries. At a

Appendix I Uncertainty Quantification
The post-hoc calibration analysis in subsection 6.3 is exploratory relative to the main result for the
text modality. The primary analysis uses the T+D ensemble of five models on the full 24 h test set. A
separate analysis across forecast leads evaluates T, T+D and T+I+D models trained independently at
each lead. The CLEAR comparison and its conditional-coverage analysis use paired storm splits shared
within that comparison but different from the analysis across forecast leads. Neither protocol calibrates
on earlier seasons and evaluates on a later season.
I.1 Dependence and empirical coverage on storm tracks
Split conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2005) guarantees marginal coverage under exchangeability of
calibration and test scores. Residuals in these data are dependent within storms, motivating evaluation
that holds out whole storms. This dependence alone does not determine the coverage of a particular
calibration method. The uncalibrated spread across five seeds gives substantial undercoverage in the
analysis across forecast leads. It is distinct from naive i.i.d. conformal calibration, which estimates a
residual quantile. Table 19 compares calibrated coverage across the separately trained per-lead families.
Coverage varies more across splits at longer leads, when fewer storms are available.
I.2 Method
We use whole storms as resampling blocks, following the storm-block bootstrap used for the MAE-gain
intervals in subsection N.4. Let i index cases from the calibration storms and j cases from the held-out
storms. For each case, y is the observed wind or pressure change, ̂y is the mean prediction of the
five-seed ensemble, and σ is the standard deviation of its five predictions. The score of calibration case i
is si = |yi− ̂yi|/wi, where the scale w is 1 for unscaled scores and σ+ϵ for ensemble-normalized scores.
The constantϵ is fixed at2 kt for wind and1.5 hPa for pressure.
Storm-block bootstrap calibration resamples the calibration storms with replacement B times.
In resample b =1,…,B, it pools the scores of all cases from the drawn storms and computes ̂q(b), their
split-conformal quantile at level 1−α. Here α = 0.1 is the target miscoverage of the 90% intervals.
The threshold ̂q↑ is the 80th percentile of ̂q(1),…, ̂q(B), and the interval for held-out case j is ̂yj ± ̂q↑wj.
The tables label the unscaled version storm-block bootstrap and the ensemble-normalized version
ensemble-normalized block. We chose the 80th bootstrap percentile as a moderate upward adjustment for
uncertainty in the estimated calibration threshold. Using the 90th percentile would be more conservative
and produce wider intervals. The choice is an empirical heuristic, and we do not claim that 80 is optimal.
The resulting intervals have no finite-sample coverage guarantee under storm dependence.
Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI, Gibbs & Candès, 2021) adjusts the miscoverage level at
which it issues intervals. Let αk be this level after k updates. When the outcome of an earlier interval
becomes available, ACI sets αk+1 =αk +γ(α−errk), where errk = 1 if the outcome fell outside that
interval and 0 otherwise. The step size is fixed at γ = 0.03. We use a bounded ACI variant: when
the adaptive quantile rank exceeds the calibration sample size, the half-width is capped at the largest
absolute calibration residual instead of becoming infinite. We evaluate this variant empirically and do
not invoke the original ACI coverage guarantee.
We compare these with naive i.i.d. conformal (a case-pooled quantile) and an EnbPI-style proxy
(C. Xu & Xie, 2021, using a sliding window of available residuals from a frozen ensemble, without
the leave-one-out bootstrap retraining of the original EnbPI protocol). For a lead time h, an outcome
for initialization time t enters either online method only at t +h. Before issuing an interval at time
τ, updates use only outcomes satisfying t +h ≤ τ, and state is reset for each held-out storm. ACI
updates use the miss indicator of the interval actually issued. Both online methods use unscaled absolute
residuals. This replay enforces lead-dependent feedback delay, but does not model further operational
reporting or best-track revision delays. Batch methods remain calibrated once per split, so online
methods receive a different information stream.

I.4 Limitations
This analysis is exploratory and narrower than the paper's evaluation of point forecasts. First, the
repeated random storm splits reuse the 2022–2023 storm pool. They do not test calibration on earlier
50
seasons followed by deployment on later ones. Seasonal and basin structure can also limit the exchange-
ability of whole storms. Second, the paired CLEAR comparison is at 24 h. Table 19 covers leads from 24
to 120 h, but only for three of the seven methods. It uses separately trained five-seed ensembles for
each lead and its own set of 120 splits. Third, the baseline interval constructions are symmetric around
their point forecast and cannot represent residual skew in the way a conditional quantile model might
(Romano et al., 2019). Fourth, online feedback enters only after the lead time has elapsed, but operational
reporting delays and later best-track revisions are not represented. Finally, we have not reimplemented
the external literature on uncertainty quantification for TCs (e.g. MTCIE, T. Song et al., 2024, X. Chen
et al., 2025; Meng & T. Song, 2024) on this dataset. These results are an empirical calibration comparison,
not a claim of state-of-the-art uncertainty quantification or operational coverage guarantees. Future
work could test calibration based on input availability and disagreement between modalities (Azizi,
2026a). Coverage could also be targeted within a group of cases identified before the forecast, such as
those at risk of rapid intensification, as was done for groups of questions in language model generation
(Azizi, 2026b). Both would require choices fixed before calibration and an evaluation that respects storm
dependence.

J.2 Discussion availability, storm phase, and missingness
Discussion availability could encode storm phase because fused models zero-pad the text branch when
no discussion is matched. Three checks assess this concern. First, a baseline that predicts the average
change in the training data, separately for cases with and without a matched discussion, performs close
to climatology, although this does not exclude interactions with other features. Second, the wind gain
associated with discussions persists when every evaluated row has a matched discussion (Table 25).
Third, it also persists in the pre-landfall subset, while the smaller post-landfall subset has a larger
but less precise point estimate. Here landfall is a proxy based on the first zero IBTrACS (Knapp et al.,
2010) distance-to-land value. Storms with no such record retain all rows in the pre-landfall group.
The pressure gain before first landfall remains unresolved. These pointwise subset comparisons do
not establish a between-phase interaction or a mechanism. The selected SHIPS inputs lack an explicit
distance-to-land predictor, but include latitude and longitude, so geography is not absent from the
numerical inputs.
