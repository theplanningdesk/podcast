Abstract 22 
 23 
Understanding the dynamics of natural and anthropogenic sources and sinks of greenhouse gases 24 
(GHG) is key to projecting anthropogenic climate change and detecting potential climate -carbon 25 
feedbacks. While our understanding of the sources and sinks has increased considerably over the 26 
past decades, much of the progress has focused on extratropical ecosystems, leaving large parts of 27 
the tropics understudied. Tropical ecosystems are assumed to be important carbon sinks and 28 
significant sources of methane, but much of the temporal and spatial heterogeneity and trends are 29 
still poorly understood. Observational efforts have largely concentrated on key tropical regions such 30 
as the Amazon Basin, whereas other tropical areas remain sparsely monitored with in -situ 31 
measurements. In particular, northern South America—despite being identified by bottom-up and 32 
top-down models as a significant contributor to global GHG budgets —currently lacks atmospheric 33 
observations to evaluate these estimates or resolve their spatial and temporal variability. To address 34 
this observational gap, here we present the establishment of the Villavicencio Tower (VLO) for 35 
atmospheric GHG measurements to provide additional constraints on GHG emission estimates 36 
across northern South America. This 26 m tower is located within the Orinoco lowlands, close to the 37 
Andean foothills in Colombia. Due to the predominantly easterly winds in the region, the footprint 38 
covers the Orinoco basin and the northern Amazon. Weekly duplicated flask measurements are 39 
taken, and laboratory analysis of trace gases (CO 2, CH 4, CO, N 2O, SF 6, H 2) and the stable isotope 40 
composition of CO 2 (13C and 18O) and CH 4 (13C and 2H) are performed, as well as O 2/N2 and Ar/N2. 41 
The tower started operating on 20 June 2023 and here we present the first 21 months of data. Time 42 
series of all measurements are presented as well as a discussion regarding uncertainties. These 43 
measurements will allow for improved estimates of GHG emissions across northern South America 44 
and to detect long-range transport across the Amazon to the Orinoco river basin. 45

1. Introduction 49 
 50 
Atmospheric greenhouse gas (GHG) mixing ratios are increasing year after year, driving 51 
anthropogenic climate warming. The main GHG gases driving this anthropogenic climate change are 52 
CO2, CH4 and N2O, in decreasing order of importance. Over the past few decades, our understanding 53 
of the drivers, sources, and sinks of these gases has advanced considerably (Arias et al., 2021). These 54 
advances have allowed the generation of functional processed -based models that are able to 55 
estimate global budgets for the main GHGs (Friedlingstein et al., 2025; Saunois et al., 2025; Tian et 56 
al., 2024). In parallel, improvements in atmospheric inversion methods (Houweling et al., 2017) , 57 
together with the increasing availability of globally distributed atmospheric observations and 58 
remotely sensed products, have helped constrain these process-based global estimates (Ciais et al., 59 
2013; Peylin et al., 2013). 60 
The annually updated global CO₂ budget estimates generated by the Global Carbon Budget 61 
(Friedlingstein et al., 2025) provide annual assessments of atmospheric CO₂ growth rates and their 62 
underlying drivers. Similar projects have produced budgets for methane (Saunois et al., 2025) and 63 
nitrous oxide (Tian et al., 2024) . These studies have clarified the importance of distinguishing 64 
between anthropogenic and natural sources, highlighting the dominant role of fossil fuel emissions 65 
in driving CO₂ increases, the substantial contribution of agriculture and wetlands to methane 66 
emissions, and the key influence of agricultural practices on nitrous oxide fluxes. Together, these 67 
coordinated efforts have improved our understanding of the global greenhouse gas budget and 68 
reduced uncertainties in source attribution. 69 
 70 
Despite these advances, substantial uncertainties remain regarding the magnitude, dynamics, and 71 
spatial distribution of greenhouse gas (GHG) sources and sinks (Canadell et al., 2021). Much of the 72 
progress to date has focused on extratropical regions, while research in the tropics has largely 73 
concentrated on a few key areas, such as the Amazon rainforest, leaving many tropical regions 74 
understudied (Watts et al., 2026). This imbalance leads to large uncertainties in these regions, both 75 
in process-based global models and in atmospheric inversion studies, where sparse observational 76 
networks limit representation. As a result, tropical GHG fluxes remain comparatively poorly 77 
constrained although some of these have been recognized as key source regions at a global scale 78 
(Feng et al., 2022; Gentine et al., 2019). 79 
 80 
One such region is the Orinoco basin in northern South America (Fernandes Amaral et al., 2026). 81 
This region is covered by natural savannas (the Llanos) and tropical rainforest. Part of these savannas 82 
present biomass-burning during the dry season (December -April) but also flooding during the wet 83 
season (May-November), which, together with the other wetland systems in South America (e.g. 84 
Pantanal (Gloor et al., 2021), and Llanos de Moxos (France et al., 2022) make it an important source 85 
of methane at a global scale. Furthermore, savanna management has traditionally been based on 86 
cattle ranching, but the region is currently undergoing rapid agricultural transformation driven by 87 
the expansion of oil palm plantations and rice cultivation. Due to its remoteness and political 88 
instability, limited field data is available, leaving us in the dark on the importance of this area for 89 
global GHG fluxes and how it is responding to ongoing climate change and socio -economical 90 
dynamics (Fernandes Amaral et al., 2026). 91 
 92 
One approach to monitoring such regions is through the use of remotely sensed data (He et al., 93 
2026). Over the past decades, substantial advances have been made in estimating atmospheric 94 
column-averaged mixing ratios of CO 2 and CH4 from satellite observations (Buchwitz et al., 2015) . 95

These datasets enable monitoring at the global and regional scale, helping to fill observational gaps 96 
in regions where in situ measurements are sparse or absent. However, satellite -derived products 97 
are subject to their own uncertainties and biases (Frankenberg et al., 2024) and therefore require 98 
validation against ground-based observations. The limited availability of such measurements across 99 
large parts of the tropics increases the uncertainty associated with these estimates in these key 100 
tropical regions (Hancock et al., 2025, 2026). 101 
 102 
An alternative approach for monitoring greenhouse gases (GHGs) is through high -precision 103 
atmospheric measurements at the surface. Temporal variations in atmospheric mole fractions, as 104 
well as differences among monitoring stations, can be used to infer the spatial dynamics and trends 105 
of regional GHG fluxes, typically in combination with atmospheric transport and inverse modeling, 106 
which relate observational gradients to surface fluxes. An important advantage of atmospheric flask 107 
monitoring over remotely -sensed columns is the ability to obtain stable and radioactive 108 
isotopologues on CO2 and CH4 (Heimann et al., 2022; Sierra et al., 2025), which are crucial for source 109 
attribution and process understanding. Although global atmospheric observation networks have 110 
been established in the last decades (Schuldt et al., 2025) , these stations are concentrated in 111 
extratropical regions, with a focus on monitoring background air and with relatively limited coverage 112 
across the tropics. This sparse coverage partly reflects the logistical and infrastructural challenges 113 
associated with establishing and maintaining long-term atmospheric monitoring stations in remote 114 
tropical environments. One notable exception is the Amazon Tall Tower Observatory (ATTO), which 115 
was established to monitor atmospheric composition and ecosystem –atmosphere i nteractions 116 
across a large portion of the Amazon rainforest (Andreae et al., 2015) . In addition, aircraft -based 117 
vertical profile measurements have been collected over the past decades in several parts of the 118 
Amazon Basin (Gatti et al., 2021, 2023), resulting in comparatively good observational coverage for 119 
this region. However, the prevailing easterly trade winds largely constrain the source regions 120 
represented by these measurements to central and eastern Amazonia, leaving the Orinoco plains 121 
and the northern Amazon poorly monitored (Botía et al., 2025). 122 
 123 
Considering the limited availability of field data on GHG fluxes and the fact that no atmospheric 124 
monitoring station adequately covers northern South America, we established the Villavicencio 125 
Tower (VLO) in the Orinoco floodplains near the Andean foothills, outside the city of Villavicencio, 126 
Colombia. The main objective of the VLO tower is to produce a record of high precision atmospheric 127 
measurements of greenhouse gases and their isotopic composition for the understudied region of 128 
the Orinoco Llanos. In addition, we aim at integrating the results of this monitoring program into a 129 
larger network of global atmospheric observations that are currently used for atmospheric inversion 130 
studies and for producing global budgets of GHGs. Here, we describe the characteristics of the 131 
tower, the sampling and analytical procedures, its seasonal footprint, and present the time series 132 
from the first 21 months of data collection. We also discuss data limitations and tower 133 
characteristics that should be taken into account for the appropriate use of the collected data. 134

2. Methods 137 
2.1 Tower and flask measurement description 138 
 139 
Measurements are taken from a 26 m high tower (4°04’23.75’’N; 73°35’05.31’’W), located within 140 
the Barcelona Campus of Universidad de Los Llanos (Uni llanos), approximately 9 km south-west of 141 
the city of Villavicencio, Colombia. Advantage was taken from an existing 20 m high structure, on 142 
top of which a 6 m high tower was constructed (see Figure S1 for a photo of the tower). The tower 143

is approx. 1750 km away from the nearest other atmospheric monitoring station at ATTO and 144 
approx. 1850 km away from the background NOAA station of Ragged Point Barbados (Figure 1). Due 145 
to the predominantly easterly winds, the atmospheric measurements of Barbados and Ascension 146 
Island (NOAA stations) should be informative regarding the background atmospheric conditions 147 
reaching the continent. 148 
 149 
Since 20 June 2023, weekly atmospheric flask samples have been collected at the tower site using a 150 
manual sampler (Figure S2 (Heimann et al., 2022)). The inlet is located at the top of the tower (26 151 
m), covered with an air filter (Solberg F -15-050) and attached to a 30 m long ¼ inch Synflex 1300 152 
Dekabon tubing (aluminium tape with an ethylene copolymer coating and an outer cover of high -153 
density polyethylene) with an inner diameter of approximately 4.3 mm. Air samples are pressurized 154 
to 1.6 bar in 1 L glass flasks (Figure S2), with duplicates collected to minimize data loss, ensure quality 155 
control, and support uncertainty estimation. Prior to sample co llection, the sampling tubing was 156 
flushed to remove residual air from the inlet system. Subsequently, air was directed through the 157 
sampling flasks, which were flushed with at least three times their internal volume. To avoid the 158 
influence of water vapor on greenhouse gas (GHG) mixing ratio measurements (Baier et al., 2020), 159 
the sampled air was dried using magnesium perchlorate before entering the flasks. Flasks are 160 
shipped from Colombia to the Max Planck Institute for Biogeochemistry (MPI -BGC) for analysis 161 
approximately every six months. Due to these logistical constraints, there is a substantial delay 162 
between sample collection and the availability of laboratory results. Experiments evaluating the 163 
effects of sample storage on atmospheric mixing ratios and isotopic composition have shown that 164 
storage does not substantially alter the measurements (Rothe et al., 2005). The transfer of samples 165 
from the measurement site to MPI -BGC results in an average lag of 238 days for greenhouse gas 166 
concentration measurements and 283 days for isotopic composition analyses (Figure S3). Since 13 167 
May 2025, continuous wind speed and direction are measured at the top of the tower using a 1405-168 
PK-165 Gill WindSonic® instrument. 169

2.2 Determination of surface influence or footprint 171 
 172 
Spatially explicit surface influence maps (footprints) were calculated using the Stochastic Time 173 
Inverted Lagrangian Transport (STILT) model (Lin et al., 2003) , following the setup of Botia et al. 174 
(Botía et al., 2022) for the Amazon Tall Tower Observatory. Back trajectories were computed by 175 
releasing 100 particles at each measurement coordinate (x, y, z, t), at the height of the flask inlet (z, 176 
26 m). Gridded footprints were derived at a spatial resolution of 0.25° × 0.25° and defined as the 177 
flux sensitivity of mole fractions at the receptor location (units: ppm per μmol m⁻² s⁻¹). From these, 178 
we computed: (1) the mean footprint over the full measurement period, with areas of influence 179 
corresponding to the 50th and 75th cumulative percentiles, and (2) monthly average footprints 180 
illustrating the seasonally varying surface influence over the 2023–2025 period. 181

2.3 Laboratory analysis 198 
 199 
 The atmospheric samples are analyzed for their GHG concentration and isotopic composition 200 
respectively in the Jena GasLab and IsoLab of the MPI-BGC. Gas Chromatography is used to measure 201 
the concentration of CO2, CH4, CO, N2O, SF6, H2 in the samples. Mass spectrometry is used to analyze 202 
the isotopic composition of CO 2 (13C and 18O) and CH 4 (13C and Deuterium), as well as O 2/N2 and 203 
Ar/N2. Table S1 gives an overview of the analytical methods and precision of each measurement. A 204 
detailed description of the analysis methods and instruments has been provided in Heimann et al. 205 
(Heimann et al., 2022) . Uncertainties in the dataset are quantified by means of individual 206 
measurement uncertainty as provided by the laboratory, differences between duplicated samples 207 
and outlier detection (Figure 2 & 3). 208

In addition to the uncertainty provided by the Gas - and Iso-Labs, we identify statistical outliers in 212 
the flask time series by performing a two -stage procedure combining a robust spline fitting with 213 
iterative sigma-clipping based on the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD). For each species, a per -214 
timestamp mean was first computed by averaging the upstream and downstream flask values. In 215 
the first stage, the spline was fitted to all duplicate means and the RMSD of the residuals was 216 
calculated. Any point whose residual exceeded three times the RMSD was removed, the spline was 217 
re-fitted to the remaining points, and the RMSD was recomputed. This process was repeated until 218 
no further points were rejected, yi elding a robust spline that is not influenced by outliers. In the 219 
second stage, all original data points (i.e. the duplicate means) were evaluated against the final 220 
robust spline and a point was classified as an outlier if its residual exceeded three times the RMSD. 221 
The smoothing parameter of the cubic spline was set to s = f · n · 𝜎2, following the parameterisation 222 
of the UnivariateSpline function in the SciPy library (Virtanen et al., 2020) in python, where n is the 223 
number of data points and 𝜎2 is the sample variance. The species -specific scaling factor f was 224 
selected by visual inspection of the fitted spline for each species, balancing smoothness against the 225 
ability to capture real atmospheric variability (f = 0.35 for CH 4; f = 0.5 for CO2, N2O, CO, SF6, and H2; 226 
f = 0.7 for the isotope ratios, O2/N2 and Ar/N2). 227

2.5 Data availability and format 229 
 230 
The data are available in the following Zenodo repository: 231 
https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23300649 and will be updated as new measurements become 232 
available. The current version (v1) covers the period June 2023 to March 2025. Data are provided 233 
as separate ASCII-formatted CSV files for each gas species or isotope measured. Each file starts with 234 
a short commented header containing metadata: station name, coordinates, sampling and ground 235 
altitude, time convention (UTC), flag definitions, dataset version and date, and the measured gas or 236 
isotope with its units. The header is followed by the observations, with one row per sampling date, 237 
giving the date and time (UTC), the mean mole fraction or isotopic value of the flask replicates, the 238 
standard deviation across replicates (σ), a quality flag indicating whether the value failed the time -239 
series anomaly test, and the number of flasks averaged (N). Further details on the data format and 240 
processing are given in Heimann et al. (2022). 241

3. Results 244 
 245 
The mole fraction time series of the different gases measured at the VLO tower are presented in 246 
Figure 2. The time series are characterized by temporally coherent measurements without any clear 247 
gaps, but with some outliers and discrepancies between duplicate samples detected for most gases 248 
(see also Figure S4). CO₂ shows an increase in mole fraction over time, with a clear seasonal pattern 249 
in which concentrations begin to decrease slightly from April–May and rise steeply around the end 250 
of the year (December–January). Methane mole fractions show a small but discernible increase over 251 
time, without a clear seasonal pattern, yet with mean mole fractions consistently above the global 252 
mean. N₂O shows a linear increase of approximately 3 ppb over the 21-month period analyzed here, 253 
with a similar trend observed in SF 6 mole fractions; however, SF 6 also exhibits a distinct seasonal 254 
pattern. CO displays a strong seasonal pattern, with peaks during February –April and, in 2024, an 255 
additional peak during August–September. Hydrogen exhibits an almost identical seasonal pattern 256 
to that of CO. 257

4. Discussion 298 
Here we present the first 21 months of atmospheric mixing ratios and stable isotopic composition 299 
measurements from the new atmospheric monitoring site in Villavicencio, Colombia (VLO). The time 300 
series collected up to date do not contain major long -term gaps and show overall good temporal 301 
consistency, although some spurious values and samples with large differences between duplicates 302 
were observed (Figures 2, 3 and S4, S5 ). 303

We find that the mean annual footprint of the tower covers a large part of northern South America, 304 
with a particularly strong influence from the savannas of the Orinoco plains (Figure 1). However, 305 
this mean pattern masks important seasonal differences, which corresponds to the seasonality of 306 
most of the gases analyzed. Two main factors likely explain the observed seasonal variability in GHG 307 
mixing ratios and, to a lesser extent, in stable isotopic composition: (1) the footprint from which the 308 
atmospheric si gnal originates changes seasonally, and (2) the region is characterized by a well -309 
defined dry season from December to April, which strongly affects biosphere -atmosphere gas 310 
exchange. 311 
During the dry season (December–April), the footprint is dominated by the savannas of the Orinoco, 312 
whereas during the wetter months it shifts southward and becomes more strongly influenced by 313 
the northern Amazon forest region (Figure 4). Such a seasonal shift in footprint is expected to 314 
transport air masses originating from the Southern Hemisphere and passing over Amazonian 315 
forests, which have markedly different land-atmosphere exchange characteristics compared to the 316 
tropical savannas of the Orinoco plains. Consequently, this shift likely contributes substantially to 317 
the atmospheric signal measured at VLO. 318 
The transition from predominantly Northern Hemisphere to Southern Hemisphere air masses can 319 
be clearly observed in the SF 6 time series. Atmospheric SF 6 concentrations are increasing nearly 320 
linearly at the global scale; however, because most emissions originate in the Northern Hemisphere, 321 
mixing ratios are systematically higher in the northern hemisphere than in the Southern 322 
Hemisphere. In our time series, we observe an initial linear increase in SF6 concentrations, followed 323 
by a temporary stabilization when the footprint shifts southward, and finally a renewed increase 324 
when Northern Hemisphere air masses become dominant again from October onwards (Figures 2 325 
& S4). 326 
The second driver of the observed seasonal dynamics is the pronounced dry season that 327 
characterizes the region. Precipitation is generally very low during the December –April period, 328 
limiting photosynthesis and promoting widespread fires in the Orinoco savannas, as well as 329 
deforestation fires along the edge of the Amazon rainforest in Colombia (Arias et al., 2025) . The 330 
influence of these fires is clearly visible in the CO time series, as CO is a tracer of incomplete 331 
combustion and is therefore strongly associated with wildfire occurrence (Figure 2). We observe a 332 
marked CO peak at the beginning of 2024 and the onset of another peak at the start of 2025. In 333 
agreement with the CO peak, we also observe elevated H2 concentrations, higher CO2 mixing ratios, 334 
and decreases in δ13C and δ18O in CO2. 335

VLO is located relatively close to the Andean foothills, where the pronounced topographic gradient 342 
influences local wind patterns (Figure S6). This influence is especially noticeable from the evening 343 
until the early morning, when wind direction is dominated by westerly winds originating from the 344 
mountainous region. During daytime, the prevailing wind direction shifts and becomes aligned with 345 
the dominant regional easterly circulation. Consequently, the flask measurements which are 346 
conducted between 12-2pm predominantly sample air bringing signals from the targeted regions to 347 
the east. However, because the city of Villavicencio is located only 9 km west of the tower, the 348 
atmospheric composition measured at the site can be affected by the urban plume during the 349 
relatively infrequent periods when winds originate from westerly directions. 350

5. Conclusions 362 
Atmospheric measurements have been conducted continuously at the Villavicencio Tower (VLO) 363 
since 20 June 2023. The current time series does not contain any major gaps and shows overall good 364 
temporal consistency, with only a limited number of anomalous values or samples exhibiting large 365 
differences between replicates. The footprint of VLO covers large parts of northern South America, 366 
a region that is currently not represented by any other atmospheric monitoring station. As such, 367 
VLO provides a unique observa tional dataset for monitoring atmospheric composition and 368 
improving our understanding of biosphere–atmosphere interactions in this key tropical region. As 369 
the observational record continues to expand, the VLO dataset is expected to contribute to regional 370 
and global atmospheric inversion frameworks and to help reduce current uncertainties in 371 
greenhouse gas sources and sinks across northern South America. 372
