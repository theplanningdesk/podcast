Abstract
Supply-chain disruptions vary in intensity, scale, and frequency, producing temporally hetero-
geneous impacts on network viability. The emphasis on exposure and topology has anchored
resilience in network structure, obscuring how collaboration and trust govern its phase-
speciﬁc temporal dynamics, including resistive capabilities, adaptation speed, containment,
and recovery. This study thus develops an agent-based simulation of the Netherlands logistics
network, in which nodes operate as autonomous agents that redistribute load under capacity,
link, and trust constraints. Cascading failures are simulated with trust-gated, capacity-aware,
and collaboration-dependent load reallocation, capturing both immediate contagion and
multi-step redistribution dynamics. The framework compares rule-based heuristics with
adaptive AI agents trained using supervised imitation, online multiplicative adaptation, and
cross-entropy policy search. Temporal resilience is then measured across each phase speciﬁc
metrics of absorptive, adaptive, and recovery phases across the collaboration structure. This
points to the main contribution of this article, in which AI agents improve adaptation and
cascade containment through targeted use of high-trust, high-capacity links, while recovery
dynamics remain bounded by structural and exogenous constraints. This points to a layered
view of resilience, where performance depends on the interaction between network conﬁg-
uration, trust-mediated coordination, and agent-level adaptation.

1 Introduction
Supply chain management has evolved into a collaboration-centred approach to value cre-
ation, where cross-functional and cross-enterprise coordination is a key driver of service,
proﬁtability, asset utilisation, and cost efﬁciency. However, supply chains remain highly
exposed to disruptions. Environmental shocks such as Typhoon Hato in 2017 or the Ice-
landic volcanic eruption in 2010 disrupted ports and ﬂights, delaying construction projects
and increasing operational costs (Fang et al., 2022; V oskaki et al., 2023). Because supply

networks are interconnected, localized disruptions can propagate into system-wide failures,
creating a ripple effect that is difﬁcult to predict and manage and that threatens operational
continuity and sustainability (Dolgui & Ivanov, 2021;I v a n o v ,2017; Proselkov et al., 2024).
As a result, resilience has become a central consideration in supply-chain network design,
reﬂecting the ability of systems to withstand, adapt to, and recover from disruptions (Kim,
Chen and Linderman, 2015;L ie ta l . ,2020).
Within this context, collaboration and trust shape how partners share capacity and
exchange resources under shock. Networks with enabling structures and high relational trust
can reassign capacity and respond faster than rigid, low-trust chains (Ivanov & Keskin,
2023;I v a n o v ,2024a). While redundancy and backups remain important, their effectiveness
depends on trust and reconﬁguration of collaborative arrangements that allow mutual sup-
port; horizontal cooperation combined with trust-building can therefore create distributed
adaptive capacity that reduces system fragility (Hosseini Shekarabi et al., 2025; Williams
et al., 2021). Such systems resemble “sponge-like” networks that absorb shocks through
coordinated action (Hu & Ma, 2025; Patrício, Ferreira and Gerschewski, 2025), where struc-
tural position further enables ﬂexible tactical and operational responses (Chen et al., 2025a,
2025b). This capacity can be further enhanced through AI-enabled decision-making tools,
which support dynamic allocation and load redistribution under disruption (Cao et al., 2020).
The concept of resilience in supply chains can be broken down into distinct temporal
phases, which include pre-disruption, adaptation, and recovery phases (Hosseini, Ivanov and
Dolgui, 2019;B r u c k l e re ta l . ,2024). Shocks that range from routine ﬂuctuations to extreme
events affect these phases differently, amplifying vulnerabilities across interconnected net-
works (Ivanov, 2024a; Wei et al., 2023). Rapid-onset events such as ﬂoods or cyclones can
trigger immediate cascading failures, whereas repeated or prolonged disruptions constrain
the time available for adaptation and recovery (Ivanov, 2025b). Disruptions in supply-chain
networks vary in severity and can be described by their frequency, duration, and intensity,
each of which shapes the extent of performance loss and network impact (Burkhart & Bode,
2024;D e y ,2023;I v a n o ve ta l . ,2019). V ariations in duration and magnitude also shape the
speed of loss propagation and the persistence of disruptions across the network (Holgado &
Niess, 2023; Roshani et al., 2024).
Cascading-failure approaches quantify ﬁnal disruption outcomes but rarely account for
the dynamic propagation of loads, temporally staggered failures, and the system’s adaptive
adjustments that are central to understanding resilience in operationally realistic networks
(J. Shi et al., 2026a, 2026b). Although some recent work has started to incorporate dynamic
aspects, there remains a signiﬁcant gap in detailed analyses that track resilience metrics
as functions of time throughout disruption propagation and adaptive response phases, par-
ticularly under realistic load and capacity conditions (Lv et al., 2025). Y et most research
examines trust at the dyadic level and relates it primarily to responsiveness and performance
(Qian et al., 2021;Y a v a p r a b h a se ta l . ,2025), while only limited work considers trust as
an emergent network-level property shaped by interdependence and topology (Capaldo &
Giannoccaro, 2015; Roozee et al., 2024). This indicates the need for a propagation model
that integrates trust and adaptive decision-making under varying shock characteristics.
A key limitation is that most propagation models treat load redistribution as exoge-
nous, relying on predeﬁned rules or centralized optimisation. This overlooks that, under
disruption, ﬁrms must decide how to reallocate ﬂows under capacity constraints, partial infor-
mation, and trust-dependent relationships. To model this decentralised decision process, ﬁrms
are represented as learning agents operating under bounded rationality and evolving inter-
organisational trust, which implement position-dependent redistribution policies governing
how resources are allocated, shared, and rerouted during disruptions (Arora & Brintrup,2021;

Doroudi et al., 2020). Methodologically, they provide a controlled means to compare coordi-
nation logics and assess how local decision-making shapes cascade dynamics and resilience
outcomes, while remaining relevant to emerging discussions on autonomous supply-chain
decision support (Xu, Mak, et al., 2024). Accordingly, the study addresses two research ques-
tions: (1) How do disruption propagation and resilience trajectories emerge under varying
shock conditions and collaboration structures in capacity-constrained networks? and (2) How
do trust-based relationships and position-dependent decision rules endogenously shape load
redistribution, cascade dynamics, and system-level resilience outcomes?
Therefore, this study makes three contributions. First, the study reconceptualises disrup-
tion propagation in supply-chain networks as a policy-driven load redistribution process,
in which resilience emerges from the interaction between network structure, capacity con-
straints, and endogenous coordination behaviour rather than predeﬁned rules. Second, it
develops an agent-based modelling framework that endogenizes redistribution decisions
through learning-based policies under trust and capacity constraints, enabling systematic
comparison between rule-based and adaptive coordination regimes. Third, it establishes a
phase-speciﬁc linkage between agent decision policies and temporal resilience outcomes,
showing how absorptive, adaptive, and recovery performance are differentially shaped by
trust-mediated coordination and local decision dynamics.
2 Literature review
2.1 Collaboration structures in the supply chain network
Supply chains form connections through which collaboration exerts indirect effects. These
effects depend as much on form as on intent, particularly in exchanging resources to operate
interactively across ties, framing collaboration as a conﬁguration of interdependent ties that
shape multi-node resource accessibility and diffusion (Bygballe, Dubois and Jahre, 2023).
Weighted ﬂows and tie strengths further determine whether collaborative links reinforce or
reconﬁgure structural connectivity: strong ties tend to cluster locally, whereas weak ties bridge
communities and shorten path lengths (Mrowinski et al., 2024). This distribution of coordi-
nation rights and decision-making control modulates centralization versus decentralization,
thereby inﬂuencing network robustness and vulnerability (Pfaff, Birkel and Hartmann, 2023;
Keller, Lasch and Matook, 2024). Additionally, capabilities at organizational, chain, and
industry levels act as constraints or enablers for sustaining alternative routing and redun-
dancy under disturbance (Hong et al., 2023).
Several forms of collaboration relationships can be characterised through network archi-
tecture and its associated trade-offs. At the dyadic level, direct ties provide immediacy in
coordination; however, the “dark side” of deep relational ties has been identiﬁed (Villena
et al., 2011). Structurally, such low-degree conﬁgurations exhibit limited redundancy and
longer effective path lengths, reducing reachability and weakening static robustness (X. Hu
et al., 2026). To overcome these limitations in reach and coordination, more centralized
forms of network organisation emerge. In such conﬁgurations, hub-and-spoke networks con-
centrate coordination and disruption exposure in central nodes (Zhalechian et al., 2018).
While this improves efﬁciency, it also increases sensitivity to disruption, as degradation of
high-centrality hubs sharply reduces giant connected components, weighted efﬁciency, and
overall connectivity (Saberi & Aref, 2025; Sharmin et al., 2026). By contrast, more mod-
ular or clustered topologies redistribute connectivity across densely connected subgraphs.

Annals of Operations Research
Here, resilience depends on connectivity patterns such as local clustering and inter-cluster
link sparsity (Kiani Mavi et al., 2023). Geographic proximity often characterises these clus-
ters (Rivera et al., 2016), although agglomeration may simultaneously amplify systemic,
location-bound risk (Zhao et al., 2025). Within such local communities, dense internal ties
preserve redundancy, whereas sparse inter-cluster links render ﬂexibility contingent on the
bridging nodes that connect modules (Zhu et al., 2025).
Under disruption, however, this reliance on limited bridging capacity constrains the ability
to reallocate resources across modules . Emergency pooling temporarily reconﬁgures these
boundaries. In this sense, it forms an emergency collaboration network in which node den-
sity, centralization, and average path length determine how resources are concentrated and
redistributed under stress (Liu et al., 2022). As complexity increases, such reconﬁgurations
extend beyond pairwise exchanges, giving rise to many-to-many collaboration structures.
These can be represented as higher-order interactions in hypergraphs, where group-based
links create redundant, short-path connectivity that supports simultaneous ﬂows and buffers
failures (Majhi, Perc and Ghosh, 2022). Notable contributions include hypergraph models
that formalize multi-entity ties, showing how many-to-many topologies increase clustering
and multi-node cohesion relative to pairwise networks (Lotito et al., 2026), how higher-order
links govern cascading failures and recovery dynamics (Luo et al., 2025) and how mesoscale
structures inﬂuence cohesion and alternative routing in the topology (Lotito, Montresor and
Battiston, 2024).
Within these architectures, trust operates as a structural selection mechanism. At the
micro level, preferential linking to trusted partners increases local clustering and reduces
fragmentation (Han et al., 2021). While at the meso level, agent-based simulations show that
trust-based partner choice reshapes degree distributions and produces more robust, random-
like connectivity than price-driven rules (Hou et al., 2018). This effect, however, depends on
the distribution of interdependence as trust densiﬁes links and increases cohesion, whereas
concentrated trust constrains reachability (Szaller et al.,2024; Jun, Xiaoyu and Weiqi,2025a).
At the system level, resilience depends on both network structure and the timing of
shocks. Although network topology and trust-based partner choices support system viability
by preserving functional capacity during disruptions (Ivanov, 2021;Z h a ne ta l . ,2025)a n d
multi-stage ripple effects interacting with node vulnerability can either amplify or contain
shock variability depending on how trust-enabled behaviours such as information sharing
or substitution are enacted (Al-Sukhni & Migdalas, 2025; Zhou, 2025). Trust has also been
observed to improve adaptive performance in local, small-world, and random networks, but
its advantage diminishes as disruption frequency rises, limiting its ability to sustain viability
under repeated or rapid shocks (Giannoccaro & Iftikhar,2022). A persistent limitation among
these contributions is that conceptual discussions of resilience rarely clarify how structure and
behaviour drive recovery. This gap has been shown to affect cascading effects, recovery dura-
tion, and the reliability, responsiveness, and persistence of interventions, constraining their
timing, direction, and overall scope (Habibi, Chakrabortty and Abbasi, 2023). To bridge this
gap, we examine how disruptions unfold in collaborative networks and inﬂuence resilience
phases.
2.2 Propagation and temporal dynamics in collaborative networks
These observations motivate a closer examination of propagation and temporal dynamics
in collaborative networks, where the magnitude and velocity of ripple effects are condi-
tioned by network topology, interdependencies, and governance arrangements (Ivanov &
123
Annals of Operations Research
Dolgui, 2021). Epidemic and cascading-failure models demonstrate that mitigation mecha-
nisms entail intertemporal trade-offs: reducing transmission probability enhances immediate
robustness but may prolong recovery duration, indicating a structural tension between short-
term containment and long-term restoration (Li & Zobel, 2020). These networks are highly
threshold-dependent: when nodes require a critical fraction of functional supply links to oper-
ate, small increases in shock intensity can push the system past a tipping point, triggering
abrupt, system-wide collapse (Hosseini, Ivanov and Dolgui, 2019). Within this context, the
literature distinguishes critical nodes, whose failure triggers systemic cascades, from suscep-
tible nodes, whose vulnerabilities amplify downstream disruption, motivating differentiated
intervention strategies (Kim, Chen and Linderman, 2015; Balakrishnan & Zhang, 2020).
Directionality, moreover, adds another layer: forward and backward propagation expose
different tiers asymmetrically, yet the temporal implications of such asymmetry remain under-
theorised (Li et al., 2021).
Building on this perspective, the literature on disruption propagation can be organized into
three interrelated dimensions: (1) absorptive capacity, (2) adaptive response, and (3) recov-
ery dynamics (Ivanov et al., 2025; Chowdhury & Quaddus, 2016). The absorptive capacity
reﬂects the system’s ability to absorb disruptions before service degradation. It is commonly
operationalised through Time-to-Survive or Time-to-Shock, the maximum duration the sup-
ply chain can maintain required service levels under disruption (Simchi-Levi et al., 2014,
2015). Node functionality typically degrades as load is redistributed following upstream
failures. This makes absorptive capacity dependent on whether nodes can absorb additional
load without exceeding their limits (J. Shi et al., 2026a, 2026b). In cascading-failure mod-
els, failure thresholds act as tipping points: systems remain stable under incremental stress
until a critical limit is reached, after which failure spreads rapidly (Zhang et al., 2024a,
2024b). The effect is ampliﬁed in scale-free and hub-dominated networks, where highly
connected nodes accumulate disproportionate load and become structural vulnerabilities (X.
Hu et al., 2026). However, most models treat resistive duration and residual performance
separately, limiting understanding of how performance decline interacts with systemic col-
lapse.
The response or adaptation phase, by contrast, reﬂects the phase in which corrective actions
are initiated once a disruption is detected to contain and improve performance. This phase is
commonly captured through Time-to-Adapt (TTA), understood as the interval between dis-
ruption identiﬁcation and the ﬁrst reactive action that sustains operational continuity (Ivanov,
2021; Dolgui et al., 2024; Mosayebi et al., 2024), alongside response-time indicators such as
disruption detection delay and the duration required for rerouting, reallocation, or reconﬁg-
uration of ﬂows. For example, indirect neighbouring redistribution, node replacement, and
dynamic adaptive reconﬁguration under incomplete information, which redirect stress away
from affected nodes and delay cascade expansion as a reaction to disruption (Fu et al., 2026).
Similarly, edge rewiring reduces risk propagation and alters topological exposure (Chen et al.,
2025a, 2025b). Cross-layer analyses indicate that aligning detection, response, and adaptive
strategies, such as ﬂexible sourcing and rolling substitution, is decisive for limiting cascade
ampliﬁcation and sustaining long-term viability (Zhou et al., 2025;C h e ne ta l . ,2026).
The recovery phase concerns restorative performance and is typically measured through
Time-to-Recover (TTR), deﬁned as the period required to restore full operational capacity
after disruption, together with recovery duration, recovery rate, and restored performance
( H a b i b ie ta l . ,2023). Restoration, in turn, depends on the network’s structural proper-
ties: interdependencies and redundancy inﬂuence how quickly full or partial functionality
can be regained, and prioritizing repair sequences can signiﬁcantly reduce recovery times
(Almoghathawi, Selim and Barker, 2023). Similarly, network recoverability, the time needed
123
Annals of Operations Research
to regain pre-disruption performance, is higher in denser, more redundant topologies, with
structural indicators such as average path length and redundancy directly linked to mean
time to restore (Massobrio & Cats, 2024). This is reﬂected in resilience curves, where the
rate and efﬁciency of restoring critical functionality determine how prolonged functional
loss translates into slower recovery (Tang & Shen, 2024). However, sequentially reducing
node recovery times can improve initial invulnerability but has a limited effect on residual
resilience, underscoring the limits of restoration timing alone (Song et al., 2025). In addition,
network centrality and clustering shape the speed and extent of cascades, making both adap-
tation and recovery timing critical (Habibi, Chakrabortty & Abbasi, 2023; Mosayebi et al.,
2024). Consequently, propagation dynamics, in combination with node heterogeneity and
disruption–recovery interactions, determine overall system viability, with networks either
sustaining, partially losing, or fully collapsing functional states (Shi et al., 2026b). Despite
these advances, much of the literature models propagation as structurally determined, with
limited consideration of adaptive decision-making.
2.3 Decision policies in network-based resilience propagation
Decision policies introduce an alternative perspective, explicitly modelling how agents
respond to disruptions and shape propagation dynamics. First, structural approaches treat
disruptions as node/link removals or deterministic topology-driven propagation rules, diag-
nosing fragility via connectivity loss and accessibility decline (Gangwal & Dong,2022;Z h a o
et al., 2022). For example, in their food-trade network model, a gravity-like equation is used
where each node’s export surplus is redistributed to neighbours based on supply and shortest
path distance, so that shocks reduce local production and propagate passively through the
network, making resilience outcomes determined by topology rather than adaptive decision
(Dolﬁng, Leuven and Dermody, 2019).
A second strand emphasizes semi-active, threshold-based decision logic. For example,
Chen et al. simulate single- and multi-node failure scenarios, deﬁning node load capacities
and redistribution rules that govern how a failed node’s load spreads across the network
(Chen et al., 2022). This generates cascading overloads whose propagation and equilibrium
depend on local thresholds and network topology. Complementary approaches, such as prob-
abilistic contagion and compartmental node-state models, add time-resolved representations
of disruption spread and recovery (Chen, Xia and Hua, 2025; Jiang et al., 2026). Even so,
decision logic in these models remains mostly local and rule-based, and system-level coordi-
nation is often centralized, optimization-driven, or based on strong assumptions about global
information and limited behavioural heterogeneity. For example, multiplex models with time-
varying load redistribution and adaptive threshold rules capture how information propagation
and heterogeneous node states shape cascade outcomes across shock ensembles (Song et al.,
2026; XiuwenFu et al ., 2026). Y et, these frameworks largely rely on pre-deﬁned redistribu-
tion rules, offering limited insight into decentralized, agent-driven adaptation across scenario
ensembles.
The third strand explicitly addresses this limitation by emphasizing distributed, agent-
level adaptation. This is often represented in multi-agent and hybrid simulation models that
demonstrate heterogeneous decision rules that signiﬁcantly inﬂuence network response to
disruptions (Wang et al., 2025a, 2025b; Ahmad, Al-Bazi and Clegg, 2026). Y et these agents
still rely mainly on local and network feedback, which limits their ability to exploit multi-
path connectivity or respond effectively as conditions change. More recently, multi-task
pre-trained decision models have been developed that extract shared decision logic across
123
Annals of Operations Research
diverse multi-agent tasks, decoupling perception from decision-making and improving gen-
eralization and transfer in networked MARL settings (Wang et al., 2025a, 2025b). Similarly,
graph-aware and communication-based MARL have enabled topology-sensitive coordina-
tion under partial observability, but these models still tend to stay within a single network task
rather than modelling phase-speciﬁc redistribution policies (Kotecha & Del Rio Chanona,
2025; Zhu et al., 2024). However, their application is largely conﬁned to route selection
within a static or single-environment setting, offering limited insight into how different
agentic decision logics perform under dynamic disruptions (Liu, Wang and Xu,2022). Cross-
entropy methods likewise remain primarily heuristic policy-search optimizers for structured
routing and scheduling problems, rather than capacity-constrained, or topology-sensitive
adaptation (Wang et al., 2024). These approaches illustrate the frontier of automated pol-
icy learning, yet their conﬁnement underscores the challenge of developing generalizable,
topology- and capacity-aware decision frameworks capable of robust, multi-phase resilience
planning.
3 Research gaps
Despite substantial advances in understanding supply chain collaboration, disruption prop-
agation, and decision policies, the literature remains fragmented in integrating behavioural
adaptation, network structure, and temporal dynamics. It often treats behavioural response
as secondary to structure, reducing adaptation to predeﬁned or ex-post rules.
These gaps can be summarized into several key points. First, the joint role of heterogeneous
network structure and shock characteristics in shaping cascades remains underexplored.
While topology and disruption intensity are recognized as inﬂuential, models rarely inte-
grate link types and shock scale to explain how cascades unfold across collaborative ties.
This limitation constrains recent efforts to conceptualize supply chains as viable systems,
where sustained functionality under disruption depends on disruption–recovery dynamics,
interconnection patterns, functionally heterogeneous node roles, and the ability to reconﬁgure
and adapt over time (Ruel et al., 2024; X. Shi et al., 2026a, 2026b). Second, there is a limited
mechanistic representation of adaptation at the ﬂow level. Although the literature extensively
evaluates structural properties, disruption propagation, and recovery performance, it provides
limited insight into how ﬁrms make relational decisions such as partner selection and ﬂow
rerouting under stress, or the information available to them when doing so (Habibi et al.,
2025;Z h a ne ta l . ,2025).
Third, transient disruption–recovery dynamics remain insufﬁciently characterized. While
recent studies examine reroute ﬂexibility, adaptive resilience, multitier disruptions, and cas-
cading recovery, the intermediate reallocations that emerge under ﬁnite capacity are still not
well resolved (Hart Nibbrig, Sharif Azadeh and Maknoon, 2025;H a b i b ie ta l . ,2026). This
gap limits the ability to capture dynamic equilibrium states emerging from disruption–recov-
ery dynamics, where systems sustain functionality through continuous adaptation rather than
full recovery, consistent with the disruption–reaction balancing and long-term, open-system
evolution that deﬁne supply chain viability (Ivanov, 2022;I v a n o v&K e s k i n ,2023;W a n g&
Ya o ,2023).

4 Methodology
To address these gaps, the study adopts an agent-based simulation in which an empirical
logistics network is represented as a directed graph and each node acts as an autonomous
agent that redistributes load to its neighbours under capacity, link, and trust constraints. When
a regional shock causes a node or link to fail, its load is passed to neighbouring nodes, whose
ability to absorb it depends on their available capacity and on the trust between partners.
Repeated across the network, these local decisions generate the cascading dynamics that
the model tracks over discrete time steps. This framework allows both research questions to
be examined together, linking cascade and resilience trajectories to network structure and
shock conditions, and the role of trust and position-dependent rules to redistribution and
system-level outcomes.
Figure 1 summarises this framework: disruptions that vary in frequency, intensity, and
scale act on a collaborative network shaped by structure and trust, driving an agent-based
redistribution mechanism in which rule-based and adaptive policies are compared, with per-
formance evaluated as temporal resilience across absorptive, adaptive, and recovery phases.
The model treats network topology, node attributes, and the shock process as exogenous, while
load redistribution, trust dynamics, and the resulting resilience are determined endogenously.

sentation, capacity formulation, and shock propagation mechanics; Sect. 3.2 speciﬁes the
collaborative load-sharing mechanism and the trust and recovery rules; Sect. 3.3 introduces
the agent-based allocation policies; Sect. 3.4 assembles these into the cascade simulation
procedure and reports the veriﬁcation checks; Sect. 3.5 deﬁnes the phase-speciﬁc resilience
metrics; and Sect. 3.6 describes the case network, scenarios, and experimental protocol.

4.2 Collaborative load sharing mechanism

capacity- and link-constrained allocation. The process has three parts: measure available
receiving capacity and link headroom, compute trust-weighted offers and proportionally
allocate the failed node’s excess, and apply transfers while recording success for trust learning.

4.3 AI agents implementation and role
Agents are deﬁned as local decision modules embedded at individual nodes of the network.
When a node ui becomes overloaded or fails, its associated agent is invoked to determine how
excess load should be allocated across neighbouring nodes. In this framework, agent-based
policies replace only the allocation step of the redistribution process, while all feasibility
constraints and physical ﬂow rules remain unchanged.
The agent’s role is limited to proposing a normalized allocation over admissible neigh-
bours. Execution remains within the simulation engine, where capacity limits, collaboration
constraints, and residual link headroom are enforced through feasibility checks and clipping.
This separation ensures that differences across policies arise from allocation decisions rather
than constraint handling.
To keep the decision problem consistent across nodes with different degrees, the agent
input is encoded as a ﬁxed-size vector. For each candidate neighbouru j ,u pt oam a x i m u mo f
max _nb slots are included. Each neighbour slot contains four local features: pairwise trust
τ t ij , spare receiving capacity /Delta1Ct j , current load Ft j , and a binary collaboration indicator σij ,
which equals 1 when neighbour j is admissible for assisting node i. Neighbours are ordered
deterministically by descending /Delta1Ct j yielding a stable ﬁxed-size representation, consistent
with learning over unordered sets (Xie & Tong, 2025), then truncated or zero-padded to
length max _nb. A short global feature block is appended, containing the excess load at the
failed node, the shock intensity Sk , the regional shock scale /Theta1r , and the current number of
neighbours with the full symbols and deﬁnition summarized in Table 3. This construction
reduces permutation ambiguity and yields a stable input representation across network states.
Three policy families are considered:
1. Deterministic baseline (DB). A heuristic policy used as a training target and comparison
protocol. The main heuristic scores each neighbour by the product of trust and spare capacity.

Agents are trained using synthetic failure episodes generated across network topologies,

trained for 30 epochs with mini-batches of size 128 using the Adam optimizer with a learning
rate 10−3. Input features are standardized with a ﬁtted scaler prior to training.
The MLP policy is trained by minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence between pre-
dicted and target allocation distributions, consistent with recent formulations of imitation
learning as distribution matching between expert and learned policies (Cui et al., 2024). In
practice, this objective is used as the supervised learning loss for the softmax output layer,
and the training procedure updates the network weights by stochastic gradient descent with
an adaptive optimizer. During simulation, the engine enforces acceptance and physical feasi-
bility constraints before executing transfers. A neighbour is considered admissible only if its
spare receiving capacity is positive and its trust exceeds a minimum threshold. The alloca-
tion proposal is masked with σij and renormalized over feasible neighbours. If no admissible
neighbour remains, the simulator applies a fallback rule: it distributes load uniformly among
feasible neighbours when available, or otherwise records the unsatisﬁed load.
In addition to supervised learning and online adaptation, a black-box policy search is
used to directly optimize the neural network parameters with respect to simulation-level per-
formance. A population-based cross-entropy method treats the network weights as decision
variables and iteratively updates a Gaussian search distribution using elite candidates eval-
uated in full simulation runs. In the reported conﬁguration, the population size is 48, the
elite set size is 6, the initial search standard deviation is 0.08, and optimization is run for 30
iterations. The best-performing parameter vector is retained for comparison. Evaluation com-

is measured on held-out synthetic samples using Kullback–Leibler divergence, Jensen–Shan-
non divergence, and top-k probability mass. Online performance is assessed through repeated
stochastic replications within the simulator, with each experimental condition evaluated over

4.4 Cascade simulation
The cascading failure process can be systematically executed through the following steps, as
illustrated in Figure 3. Pseudocode is presented in online Appendix 1 of the Supplementary.
The AI-agent module is invoked at the redistribution stage whenever a node becomes over-
loaded or fails, and its proposed allocation is subsequently passed through the same capacity
and link-feasibility checks used by the rule-based mechanism.
Step 1: Initialization. At t /equal1 0, the supply chain network is initialized by setting the node
set, edge set, initial loads, initial capacities, link loads, collaboration masks, trust values, and
recovery states. The initial edge weights are computed according to Eq. ( 1), and the initial
node capacities are set using Eq. ( 2).
Step 2:Shock realization and failure detection. At each simulation step, shocks are sampled
by region according to the prescribed shock probability. When a shock occurs, the effective

capacity of the affected nodes is reduced through the degradation term and the regional
shock-scale term. The updated node loads are then compared with the corresponding effective
capacities to determine whether a node remains functional, becomes overloaded, or fails.
Step 3: Allocation of excess load. For each failed or overloaded node, the corresponding
excess load is identiﬁed and prepared for redistribution to neighbouring nodes. At this point,
the allocation is generated in one of two ways. In the rule-based version, the collaborative
load-sharing mechanism computes trust-weighted offers, normalizes them into allocation
shares, and determines the proposed transfers. In the AI-agent implementation, the local
agent associated with the failed node takes the neighbourhood state as input and outputs a
normalized allocation proposal over eligible neighbours. In both cases, the resulting pro-
posal is not executed directly; instead, it is treated as the requested redistribution and then
checked against spare capacity, collaboration eligibility, and residual link headroom before
any transfer is committed.
Step 4: Feasibility enforcement and state updating. The proposed transfers are clipped to
satisfy physical feasibility constraints. Recipient nodes can only absorb load up to their spare
capacity, and each edge can only carry load up to its residual headroom. After transfer, the
updated node loads and link loads are committed to the system state. Nodes whose updated
loads exceed their effective capacities are added to the cascade set, so they may trigger
additional redistribution in the next iteration.
Step 5: Trust updating and recovery dynamics. Each realized transfer produces a success
score based on the fraction of the proposed load that is actually delivered. Pairwise trust is
then updated through the exponential moving average rule so that recent outcomes have a
stronger inﬂuence while preserving interaction history. In parallel, down nodes accumulate
recovery state over time, and previously inactive links may be reactivated when trust exceeds
the deterministic threshold or when the probabilistic reopening rule is satisﬁed.
Step 6: Iteration and stopping. The process repeats until no new overloaded nodes are
generated or the simulation horizon is reached. Once the cascade stabilizes, the resulting
failure and recovery outcomes are used to compute the resilience metrics reported in Table
4.
To ensure model veriﬁcation and credibility, several steps are taken in the form of internal
veriﬁcation, literature-based benchmarking, and robustness validation. First, internal veriﬁca-
tion ensures that the simulation engine satisﬁes fundamental physical and logical constraints
at each time step. In particular, all load redistribution operations are checked to preserve con-
servation of ﬂow, while respecting node capacity limits and residual link headroom. Transfers
are clipped to feasible levels, and infeasible allocations are either re-normalized or recorded
as unmet demand. These checks guarantee that cascade dynamics arise from modeled interac-
tions rather than numerical inconsistencies alongside metrics are properly recorded for each
step. Second, the proposed redistribution mechanism is benchmarked against both classical
cascade formulations and recent transport and supply-chain resilience models, such as the
overload-based cascading failure model (Crucitti, Latora and Marchiori, 2004), and the pref-
erential redistribution framework (Wei et al., 2012), and the work on redistribution intensity

4.5 Network structure and resilience metrics
The simulation outputs are thus organised into three resilience phases: absorptive, adaptive,
and recovery, together with two system-level disruption metrics. The primary metrics are
time-based and capture the timing of shock propagation, response, containment, and restora-
tion as illustrated in Figure4. The processed metrics are derived from these primary measures

4.6 Simulation design
The case study used in the study is Netherlands logistic network. The logistics network
in the Netherlands reﬂects a complex structure due to the vast number of facilities, and
their interconnected operations. With 26,951 logistics buildings, including XL and XXL
distribution centers (DCs) larger than 20,000 m 2, the network supports trade, transportation,
warehousing, and retail functions published by TU Delft (Nefs, 2023). These buildings are
distributed across key regions, such as the East-Southeast freight corridor, which is the busiest
logistics area in the country. Each facility acts as a node in the logistics network, while material
ﬂows between them form edges, translating the network into a weighted graph.
To keep experiments tractable while preserving spatial and capacity heterogeneity, the
empirical set of 26,951 logistics buildings is aggregated into K /equal1 10 representative super-
nodes using K-means on geographic coordinates and facility size/throughput. Primary
experiments are conducted at a coarsened aggregation level ( ≈260 super-nodes), selected
to balance representational ﬁdelity and computational tractability. To assess the sensitivity
of model outcomes to spatial resolution, a multi-resolution analysis is performed across low
(≈260 nodes), medium ( ≈2600 nodes), and full-resolution (26,951 nodes) networks under
the base scenario. Spatial clustering approaches, such as K-Means and hierarchical cluster-
ing, are commonly used in supply chain network modeling to reduce problem size while
preserving structural properties, making large-scale simulation tractable without substan-
tially degrading key outcomes (Regal et al., 2023;T a r ee ta l . ,2024). On this constructed
network, ﬁve scenarios (S0–S4) are deﬁned to represent distinct collaboration architectures,
each inducing a different network topology for load redistribution. These scenarios vary in

multimodal connectivity coexists with strong reliance on key hubs and corridors. (Nefs &
Daamen, 2023; Punt et al., 2023). This makes the Netherlands an appropriate case for exam-

through well-connected structures and disproportionately affect critical nodes, leading to
cascading accessibility and economic impacts (Shan et al., 2025). Flood-prone areas, like
Den Haag, face heightened risks from rising sea levels and heavy precipitation, disrupting
operations and transportation path. In this paper, we consider the disruption resulting from
environmental shocks to differ in intensity, scale, and frequency to cause node failures as
a motivating element for investigating the uncertainty and the resilience of the Netherlands
logistics network system. Once a disruption occurs, i.e., node failure, the related downstream
nodes may not operate normally and the related upstream nodes may end its operation, due
to the supply failures and causing demand to decline respectively.
The main experiments are designed to isolate the effect of collaboration architecture on
resilience outcomes under a ﬁxed simulation protocol. Five scenarios (S0–S4) represent
distinct collaboration structures, each inducing a different network topology for load redis-
tribution as illustrated in Fig. 6. Within each scenario, four allocation policies are evaluated:
(A) a heuristic baseline based on trust and spare capacity, (B) a supervised imitation policy
(MLP), (C) an online adaptive variant with multiplicative updates, and (D) a policy optimized
via a cross-entropy method. All policies generate normalized redistribution proposals that are
subsequently ﬁltered by feasibility constraints before execution. Each condition is evaluated
over 50 Monte Carlo replications with a 100-timestep horizon. Stochastic shocks vary in
intensity, scale, and frequency, and paired random seeds are used across policies to ensure
consistent comparisons. For each scenario, run-level outputs were compared across policies
using Kruskal–Wallis tests for omnibus inference, followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U
tests with Holm correction. Effect sizes were reported using Cliff’s delta. All tests were
two-sided with a signiﬁcance level of α /equal1 0.05, and adjusted p-values are reported where
applicable.

5.3 Adaptive phase
The adaptive phase reveals how policies translate initial containment into coordinated sys-
tem response. In the baseline scenario (S0), all policies remain relatively close, with T f /equal1
1 time steps, Tcontain /equal1 28–31 time steps, and Ta/equal1 6–13 time steps. This is supported by
the effect-size analysis: Cliff’s δ values for T f are effectively zero for all pairwise compar-
isons, while Tcontain and Ta exhibit only small effects (| δ|< 0.15), with several comparisons
indistinguishable from zero. This indicates that, under homogeneous structure, adaptation
is constrained more by the network itself than by policy choice. As structural complexity
increases, statistically signiﬁcant differences emerge across all adaptive metrics (for exam-
ple, in S1–S3, T f , Tcontain and Ta all show strong omnibus effects with p < 10 -18 ). This
attributes to the differences when containment is achieved and when loads successfully ﬁt
recipient capacities are no longer attributable to noise. This separation reveals a decoupling
between speed of ﬁrst response and speed of feasible reconﬁguration.
In S1, OA achieves the shortest containment and adaptation times ( Tcontain /equal1 25 time
steps, Ta /equal1 11 time steps), while CEM exhibits the longest adaptation time ( Ta /equal1 29 time
steps) despite strong absorptive performance. This divergence demonstrates that performance
in the absorptive phase does not directly carry over to adaptation under centralized coordi-
nation. In S2 (clustered), adaptation times compress across policies ( Ta /equal1 5–7 time steps
for CEM/OA vs. 7 time steps for DB), while its speed of adaptation relative to degradation
increases sharply for CEM and OA compared to S1, indicating improved efﬁciency despite
longer structural paths. In S3, both CEM and OA align tightly ( Tcontain /equal1 24 time steps, Ta
/equal1 8 time steps) and outperform DB ( Ta /equal1 18 time steps), suggesting that dense connectivity
supports synchronized adaptation across policies that can exploit multiple paths. In S4, Ta
reaches its minimum (4 time steps) for CEM and OA with moderate CR, representing the
most efﬁcient adaptation observed across all scenarios. This divergence reﬂects differences
in how policies utilize available redistribution pathways. For example, OA quickly adapts in
centralized and dense networks, CEM struggles under hub dominance but excels in multi-path
structures, and DB’s rigidity slows adaptation regardless of topology.
The interaction between trust structures and decision rules is reﬂected in how adaptation is
distributed over time. In S1–S3, SI exhibits a distinct proﬁle withT f /equal1 0 time steps butTcontain
increases substantially (62–67 time steps), while CR and AC reach their highest values, which
are (4.13–4.47) and (0.50–0.56). This combination indicates that SI mobilizes immediately
and broadly, but containment is slow precisely because redistribution spans the full network:
the cascade neighbourhood keeps expanding long after ﬁrst response because there are many
neighbours absorbing load in parallel. This pattern shifts in S4, where T f increases to 3 time
steps, Tcontain decreases to 44 time steps, and CR drops to 2.93, bringing SI closer to the
range of the other policies and suggesting that the mutual-aid pool structure concentrates
redistribution into a small number of highly efﬁcient transfers rather than distributing load
broadly.

6 Discussion and implications
6.1 Topology as a moderator of policy-driven resilience
The observed convergence of policy performance in the baseline network (S0) challenges
the assumption that collaboration mechanisms such as trust-based or coordination-driven
policies always produce distinct resilience advantages. For instance, trust-based decision
rules have been shown to generate the most robust networks under both random and targeted
disruptions, suggesting that certain coordination logics can be structurally advantageous
(Hou et al., 2018). Similarly, agents use trust to accept locally suboptimal decisions in favour
of a higher system-wide payoff (Giannoccaro & Iftikhar, 2022). However, the results here
indicate that these same mechanisms can produce the opposite effect under disruption. The
stability and the immediacy of response associated with trust may broaden participation in
redistribution. This can disperse loads unevenly, amplify stress propagation, and prolong
the resistive phase. As such, trust-reinforcing dynamics can become a liability, with better
outcomes emerging in structures that constrain and balance redistribution rather than expand
it. In the present model, the baseline network is sparse and stable, leaving limited spare
capacity and few feasible redistribution options. As a result, the policies operate within
a narrow action space, and differences in absorptive, adaptive, and recovery performance
remain small. S0 thus functions as a limiting case that isolates structural variation and provides
a controlled reference for identifying when such variation becomes decisive. This framing,
that topology sets the ceiling on what any policy can achieve, runs through the ﬁndings that
follow and should be read as the organising principle of this section.
The most pronounced divergence appears in the hub-and-spoke structure (S1) in Fig. 7
and Table 7. Here, SI reaches the recovery threshold early, but without a corresponding gain
in recovery quality, suggesting that centralized coordination can produce the appearance
of recovery while leaving the network functionally fragile. By contrast, the dense mesh
(S3) allows CEM and OA to reduce both recovery time and MTTR, indicating that higher
connectivity is beneﬁcial when the policy can actually exploit alternative routes and rerouting
paths. This suggests that speed of recovery is not a reliable indicator of system robustness,
especially in centralized structures. The ﬁnding also reinforces the complementary role of
redundancy in multi-tier supply networks, while clarifying that this effect is stronger when the
policy has structural options to use (Habibi et al.,2025). Importantly, the contrast between S1
and S3 also reveals a measurement problem: threshold-crossing speed and genuine restoration
depth can move in opposite directions, meaning that standard performance indicators may
systematically misrepresent resilience in centralised topologies.
The mutual-aid pool structure (S4) further complicates this picture by extending recovery
time while producing the strongest completeness indicators, showing that slower recovery
can still be beneﬁcial when it yields a more durable post-disruption state. This pattern both
supports Ivanov’s distinction between stability-based and adaptation-based resilience, where
viability requires preserving performance under uncertainty as well as restoring normal states
(Ivanov, 2024b) and resonates with Zhan et al.’s emphasis on timing, interaction, and collab-
orative mitigation in viable network management (Zhan et al., 2025). At the same time, the
results suggest that delayed response improves viability when the network structure enables
recovery time to translate into stronger overall outcomes, rather than prolonged instability.
This also complements the multiplex SEIRSD model that links which links viability to time-
to-recovery and service performance under different control settings (Broekaert et al., 2025),
by showing that similar recovery timelines can still lead to different outcomes depending on

how the network is organized. Accordingly, ﬁrms should organize collaboration selectively:
centralized coordination may accelerate initial recovery, but distributed, interaction-driven
collaboration is more likely to support sustained adaptation and robust long-term perfor-
mance.
6.2 Selective impact of trust on adaptive and recovery dynamics
The key insight is that trust thresholds inﬂuence each policy differently, and higher trust
does not guarantee better resilience. When link reactivation ψ is increased from the baseline
(Fig. 8), the marginal changes in absorptive metrics show only modest variation across all
policies, with DB exhibiting the clearest decline. This suggests that trust gating primarily
ﬁlters weaker coordination pathways rather than materially altering ﬁrst-impact buffering.
This qualiﬁes the broader topology-and-recovery literature, which often relies on stylized
or bounded scenarios, for example, static network-growth models (Perera et al., 2017), or
random and targeted disruption tests in ER, scale-free, and small-world networks (Shi et al.,
2020). Even two-stage recovery models with time constraints (Zhang et al., 2024a, 2024b)
assume redistribution can fully exploit structural options. By contrast, our results show that
in sparse or structurally simple systems, higher trust only strengthens early buffering when
it can be operationalized into redistribution rules that actively use limited capacity without
repeating the same allocation pattern.
This distinction becomes clearer when comparing different allocation logics. A ﬁxed deter-
ministic baseline preserves consistency, but it also traps redistribution in a narrow repeated
path, limiting its ability to dampen ripple effects. By contrast, OA and CEM revise allocation
more actively during simulation, but this ﬂexibility can also concentrate load on a small set
of attractive neighbours, creating new pressure points despite faster response. This extends
previous work on forward-looking reconﬁguration (Brusset et al., 2023), and aligns with
Ivanov’s point that redistribution is shaped by shock characteristics, network dependencies,
node degrees, and visibility constraints, especially for inventory sharing (Ivanov, 2023). The
added insight is that policy logic can still concentrate the burden even when rerouting is
available. In practical terms, this suggests that policies promoting trust or collaboration are
insufﬁcient on their own; their effectiveness depends on whether they are coupled with alloca-
tion rules that diversify redistribution pathways, particularly in more structurally constrained
systems.
Another key novelty is the mismatch between apparent responsiveness and actual recovery.
SI is the clearest example: it often crosses thresholds early, but this has little effect on actual
restoration. In structured networks, early response expands containment far more than it
improves recovery, which is why SI achieves high CR and AC values in adaptation but still
performs poorly in Tpartial , Tr , and MTTR. This extends the recovery-as-reconﬁguration
literature by showing that rapid redistribution can remain phase-bound: a policy may mobilize
the network quickly without converting that mobilization into durable restoration (Kurian
et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2025). It also qualiﬁes recent AI-driven supply chain studies by
indicating that learning-based policies such as reinforcement learning, improve disruption
detection and adaptive response, but still struggle with generalization and alignment to real-
world recovery dynamics, limiting their ability to ensure effective restoration (Aboutorab
et al., 2024; Batsis & Samothrakis, 2024). For managers, interpreting early activation metrics
as evidence of recovery can be misleading; these indicators need to be assessed together with
restoration depth, recovery duration, and functional continuity.

Furthermore, the capacity bufferβ sensitivity results in Fig.9 indicates that capacity buffer
has a modest effect on absorptive performance but is associated with heterogeneous responses
during the adaptive phase. Some policies react sharply to changes in β, reﬂecting sensitivity
to available buffer, while OA maintains a comparatively steady adjustment, suggesting more
gradual exploitation of capacity buffer. This asymmetry is not simply a matter of agentic
responsiveness. OA performs better not because autonomy alone resolves recovery bottle-
necks, but because explicit reconﬁguration strategies "ﬁlling," "repairing," and "recruiting"
are embedded in the action/reward structure to actively rebuild network structure under dis-
ruption risk (Ding et al., 2026). The implication is that self-decision-making and multi-agent
design are promising precisely when they carry embedded reconﬁguration logic, rather than
when they are implemented as general-purpose adaptive mechanisms (Xu, Almahri, et al.,
2024; Xu, Mak, et al., 2024). For managers, this means that investing in autonomous systems
without specifying their permitted reconﬁguration actions, which nodes to recruit, and which
links to repair, produces responsiveness without restoration.
6.3 Decision-rule design mediates structural potential in network resilience
Multi-sourcing fosters resilience by providing multiple distribution paths, but its contribution
depends on how those paths are coordinated within the broader network structure, not merely
on the number of links (Amico, V accario and Schweitzer,2024). The ﬁndings point to the same
pattern within routing diversity; extra paths improve resilience when the decision rule can use
them selectively. The AI-based mechanisms, CEM and OA, are the most topology-sensitive
policies in our analysis. They perform best in S3 and S4, where connectivity, path redundancy,
and spare capacity provide enough alternative routes for coordinated redistribution. This
suggests that adaptive and optimization-based mechanisms can exploit structural richness
when the topology allows rerouting without exhausting residual link capacity. Redistribution
that respects available link headroom and route feasibility is therefore a prerequisite for
structural capacity to become effective recovery, reinforcing the idea that network structure
enhances resilience only when policy mechanisms can leverage it (Cao, Xin, Wang, et al.,
2025a, 2025b). More importantly, these ﬁndings show that the missing layer in recovery
performance is the decision rule itself, which determines whether structural potential is
actually operationalized (Habibi, Chakrabortty and Abbasi, 2023).
Compared with the AI-based policies, DB is less adaptive but also less volatile, a trade-off
consistent with recent studies showing that deterministic or rule-guided approaches often
preserve stability at the expense of ﬂexibility (Zhang et al., 2024a, 2024b). This makes DB
comparatively robust in simpler or more constrained settings, where stability matters more
than adaptability. By contrast, SI shows the opposite weakness: it is more active early on,
yet its stronger redistribution activity does not yield proportionally better resilience, which
is consistent with ﬁndings that imitation-based or learned policies may respond quickly but
remain limited when coordination is weak, or the expert policy is not sufﬁciently strong (Liu
et al., 2024). The contrast between DB and SI is instructive because they fail differently:
DB through rigidity and SI through premature mobilisation, thereby deﬁning the boundaries
of the design space in which the AI-based policies must operate. This provides a more
concrete account of why timely coordination (Zhan et al., 2025) and graph-based learning
(Liang et al., 2026) improve recovery in some settings but not others. Accordingly, faster
local reaction is an insufﬁcient resilience strategy. In centralized structures, the priority is
preventing premature load concentration; in denser structures, the priority is ensuring that
adaptive policies can exploit alternative paths without overloading a small set of recipients.

The pattern points to a clear trade-off between robustness and adaptability, especially in
the sensitivity analysis, as DB is comparatively steady, but that steadiness comes from a more

conditions. Moderate trust often aligns with stronger adaptive performance, while very low
or very high trust can weaken coordination by either over-constraining or over-permitting the
network, as illustrated by the non-monotonic responses of ﬂexible policies in the adaptive

suggest that resilience design should favor coordination rules that combine selectivity with
ﬂexibility, rather than simply increasing trust or responsiveness, reinforcing that recovery
performance depends on balancing adaptability and overreaction in how redistribution is
executed across the network (Bussieweke et al., 2025).
7 Conclusion
In this study, we developed a phase-based resilience framework to examine how decision rules
and network structures jointly shape disruption dynamics in supply networks. Several net-
work architectures were tested through disruptions of varying intensity, scale, and frequency
to trace how shocks propagate and how the system absorbs, adapts, and recovers over time.
Resilience was operationalized through absorptive, adaptive, and recovery metrics, enabling
phase-speciﬁc evaluation of network performance. Comparative analysis across the heuris-
tic baseline, supervised imitation, online adaptive updates, and cross-entropy optimization
revealed clear heterogeneity in temporal outcomes across scenarios. The conclusions are
summarized below.
First, absorptive performance is relatively stable in simple networks but diverges sharply
under structured architectures, with centralized and dense networks promoting faster early
absorption, whereas clustered and mutual-aid pool conﬁgurations reduce cascade size while
altering the timing of later response. Temporal indicators such as absorption ratio and shock
damping reveal complementary aspects of buffering, showing that similar absorption levels
can mask very different loss trajectories, where some conﬁgurations preserve functionality
yet amplify cumulative loss, highlighting that absorption involves not only limiting cascade
size but also managing how losses evolve during the resistive period. Second, adaptation and
recovery are more strongly shaped by network structure and exhibit clearer phase separation:
adaptation is fastest in topologies with redundant paths and coordinated redistribution, while
hub-dominated or constrained structures slow containment, and recovery follows a two-
stage process in which partial stabilization occurs well before full restoration, indicating
that local repair and system-wide recovery do not coincide. Dense meshes support faster and
more efﬁcient recovery, whereas mutual-aid pools favor completeness over speed. Sensitivity
analysis indicates that ψ shapes early reconnection in absorption, whereas β drives later
load reallocation, with adaptation exhibiting the largest structural sensitivity despite limited
statistical signiﬁcance.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The model relies on simplifying assumptions
about link and node behaviour that are useful for exploring large experimental spaces, but
abstract away important physical realities. Link capacity dynamics were treated as residual
123
Annals of Operations Research
headroom rather than fully constrained transport channels in some formulations, recovery
was modelled as an incremental replenishment process with ﬁxed rates, and environmental or
transport latencies were not simulated at detailed temporal resolution. As a result, the timestep
interpretation should be understood as model-relative rather than policy-calibrated. Second,
the agentic decision layer combines supervised imitation, online multiplicative updates, and
cross-entropy optimization, which enables controlled comparison but constrains the strategy
space. Notably, SI often triggers premature threshold crossings with extended node-level
recovery, indicating that lightweight learning can be brittle in complex topologies, whereas
OA and CEM sustain more stable absorptive, adaptive, and recovery performance.
Future research should extend this framework in three directions. First, more formal ana-
lytical approaches, such as mean-ﬁeld, percolation, or dynamical systems methods, could
identify threshold conditions under which connectivity or pooling shifts from beneﬁcial to
detrimental across temporal phases. Second, the agentic layer could be expanded to multi-
agent reinforcement learning or constrained optimization to test whether the advantages of
OA and CEM persist under strategic, adversarial, or nonstationary shocks. Third, empiri-
cal calibration using ﬁrm-level logistics data, contractual networks, and observed disruption
and recovery episodes would strengthen the interpretation of temporal metrics and improve
external validity particularly given that emissions are proxied rather than directly observed.
Furthermore, translating these metrics into concrete infrastructure design decisions remains
crucial. Future work could explore how redundant hubs, capacity buffers, and modular han-
dling nodes interact with structural mitigations and operational policies, including formal
mutual-aid agreements and liability rules, to better understand trade-offs between early
absorption, adaptation speed, and long-term recovery in critical logistics networks.
