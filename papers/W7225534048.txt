Abstract: Integration of operational functions is an important issue in manufacturing 
management because the missynchronization of information on demand, planning, production 
implementation, and quality can reduce performance. This study aims to analyze the effect o f 
cross-functional coordination on the integration of operational processes and operational 
performance in one of the manufacturing companies in Surabaya. The study used a mixed -
method explanatory sequential based on synthetic data, involving 180 responden ts, 12 key 
informants, 96 interview answers, and 72 operational observations for 24 months on three 
product families. Quantitative data were analyzed through correlation, operational data through

between coordination –integration (r=0.444), integration –performance (r=0.627), and

INTRODUCTION 
The manufacturing industry is facing an increasingly dynamic operating environment due 
to changing demand patterns, cost pressures, technological acceleration, and supply chain 
disruptions arising from geopolitical tensions, climate change, and changes in consumer 
behavior. These conditions make companies no longer rely enough on efficiency on a single 
function, but need to ensure that demand information can be translated consistently into 
planning, production execution, and quality control. Polater (2025) point out that various global 
megatrends have increased pressure on supply chain resilience and demand organizations to 
build more adaptive capabilities. At the production level, Stauder et al. (2025) also emphasized 
that changing product and component requirements increase uncertainty in manufacturing 
planning so that production flexibility needs to be aligned with demand. Thus, the ability to 
integrate demand forecasting, production planning and contr ol, production execution, and 
quality assurance are important issues in modern operations management. 
This urgency is also relevant for Indonesia's manufacturing industry. Data from the 
Central Statistics Agency shows that the processing industry contributes around 19.07% to 
Indonesia's Gross Domestic Product in 2025 and grows by around 5.30%, so it remains one of 
the main sectors in the national economy. The large role of this sector increases the need for 
companies to maintain productivity, quality, schedule accuracy, and service to customers at the 
same time. In practice, operational problems can arise w hen forecasts change without being 
immediately translated into production plans, when production schedules do not fully consider 
capacity and material availability, when actual production data is received late by the planning 
department, or when quality findings do not immediately become feedback for the production 
process. Conditions like this show that operational performance is not only determined by the 
technical capabilities of each function, but also by the organization's ability to coordinate 
information and decisions across functions. 
Theoretically, these needs are in line with the concept of Sales and Operations Planning 
(S&OP) and process integration in operations management. Kreuter et al. (2022) explain that 
S&OP aims to balance demand and supply by aligning the decisions of various functions into 
an integrated plan, while Bhalla et al. (2023) emphasize that cross-functional planning plays a 
role in connecting day-to-day operational decisions with broader organizational goals. From a 
resource-based perspective, cross -functional coordination can be viewed as an organizational 
capability that allows resources, information, and knowledge from different parts to be used in 
an integrated manner. Özkanlısoy (2024) found that integration plays a positive role in supply 
chain performance, while Ganbold et al. (2021) showed that information-enabled integration 
is related to improving various dimensions of operational performance, including quality, 
delivery, production costs, inventory levels, and flexibility. Therefore, cross -functional 
coordination can be positioned as a mechanism that allows the forecasting, planning, execution, 
and quality processes to work as a single operational system.

Although the relationship between integration and performance has been widely 
discussed, the literature still shows mixed results and focuses. Ganbold et al. (2021) place more 
emphasis on the capabilities of information technology as a driver of supply chain integration, 
while Özkanlısoy (2024) examine the relationship between Industry 4.0, supply chain 
integration, and performance. El Mokadem & Khalaf (2023) show that the relationship between 
integration and operational performance can change according to the supply chain strategy 
used, which means integration does not always yield the same benefits in every organizational 
condition. These findings are reinfo rced by Wong et al. (2025) , who show that higher 
integration does not automatically always result in better performance because its effect 
depends on the combination and configuration of integration dimensions. Meanwhile, modern 
manufacturing studies focus heavily on technology. Arcidiacono & Schupp (2024) , for 
example, point out the contribution of smart manufacturing to cost, quality, and delivery 
performance, while Mahin et al. (2024) place quality assurance within the framework of 
Quality 4.0 and emphasize the importance of integration between technology, quality 
management, and leadership to achieve operational excellence. 
Cross-functional coordination refers to aligning information, activities, decisions, and 
objectives across departments. In manufacturing, it is essential because sales, planning, 
production, quality, inventory, and logistics decisions are interdependent. Li et al. (2022) show 
that system, process, and team coordination improve information processing and operational 
performance, consistent with the S&OP emphasis on cross-functional alignment of demand and 
supply (Kreuter et al., 2022) . Demand forecasting supports capacity, material, inventory, and 
production decisions, while S&OP integrates demand and operations through cross -functional 
planning (Kreuter et al., 2022 ; Bhalla et al., 2023 ). Khannan et al. (2025) further emphasize 
integrated production planning in collaborative manufacturing environments. Therefore, cross-
functional coordination is expected to strengthen integration across demand –planning–
execution–quality processes through effective communication , information sharing, and 
decision alignment (Li et al., 2022; Kreuter et al., 2022; Bhalla et al., 2023), leading to the first 
hypothesis, H1: Cross -functional coordination has a positive effect on the integration of 
operational processes. 
Operational integration connects demand forecasting, production planning and control, 
production execution, and quality assurance. Battesini et al. (2021) highlight the 
interconnectedness of manufacturing decisions, while Arcidiacono & Schupp (2024) and Mahin 
et al. (2024) emphasize the contribution of smart manufacturing and Quality 4.0 to operational 
performance and quality. Ganbold et al. (2021) found that integration improves quality, 
delivery, cost, inventory, customer service, and flexibility. Özkanlısoy (2024) also found a 
positive relationship between integration and performance, although El Mokadem & Khalaf 
(2023) showed that its benefits may depend on supply chain strategy and operating conditions. 
Operational performance includes quality, cost, timeliness, delivery, inventory, flexibility, and 
responsiveness. Therefore, greater integration among operational proce sses is expected to 
improve operational performance, leading to the second hypothesis, H2: Integration of 
operational processes has a positive effect on operational performance. 
Cross-functional coordination may also directly improve operational performance by 
enabling faster responses to demand changes, material constraints, production delays, and 
quality issues. Li et al. (2022) and Ganbold et al. (2021) demonstrate the importance of 
coordination and information integration for operational performance. Despite these findings, 
previous studies generally examine supply chain integration, technology, S&OP, or quality 
management separately. This study address es this gap by integrating demand forecasting, 
production planning and control, production execution, and quality assurance within one 
operational process integration framework. Effective cross-functional coordination is therefore 
also expected to directly support operational performance, leading to the third hypothesis, H3: 
Cross-functional coordination has a positive effect on operational performance.

Overall, the literature supports a model in which cross -functional coordination 
strengthens operational process integration, which subsequently supports operational 
performance, while also examining the direct relationship between coordination and 
performance. 
Based on these studies, there is still room for research that needs to be developed. The 
literature referenced earlier generally looks at integration at the supply chain, technology, 
S&OP, or quality management levels separately, while the interconnectedne ss of internal 
processes from demand forecasting, production planning and control, production execution, to 
quality assurance has not been widely placed in a single operational integration framework that 
has been tested together. In addition, previous research has positioned technology, supply chain 
strategy, or integration configuration as an explanatory factor, while cross -functional 
coordination as a managerial mechanism that connects the entire set of internal processes still 
requires further testing. This is important because the existence of systems, data, or procedures 
does not necessarily guarantee that information from demand planning will be consistently 
translated to the implementation and quality stages. Thus, this study fills the gap by placing the 
integration of operational processes as a construct that includes four main dimensions—demand 
forecasting, production planning and control, production execution, and quality assurance —
and tests cross -functional coordination as a factor that drives inte gration and operational 
performance. 
Based on these gaps, this study aims to analyze the influence of cross -functional 
coordination on operational process integration, the effect of operational process integration on 
operational performance, and the direct influence of cross -functional coordi nation on 
operational performance in one of the manufacturing companies in Surabaya. This research is 
expected to make a theoretical contribution by expanding the study of operations management 
through the explanation that performance comes not only from t he effectiveness of each 
function, but also from the ability of the organization to unify a series of demand –planning–
execution–quality through cross -functional coordination. Practically, this study provides a 
framework for management to evaluate the quali ty of coordination, information 
synchronization, and decision connectivity between departments as the basis for improving 
forecasting accuracy, adherence to production schedules, equipment effectiveness, product 
quality, and delivery accuracy. With this ap proach, the study not only assesses whether 
integration is related to performance, but also explains how cross -functional coordination can 
be the foundation for the creation of a more integrated and responsive operational process. 
 
METHODS 
Research Design 
This study uses a mixed-method approach with a sequential explanatory design (QUAN 
→ qual → integration) in the framework of a case study at a manufacturing company in 
Surabaya. The quantitative stage is used to analyze the relationship between cross -functional 
coordination, operational process integration, and operational performance, then followed by 
the qualitative stage to explain the mechanisms underlying the quantitative results. The 
sequential explanatory design was chosen because it allows statistical findings to be explained 
through the experiences and views of actors directly involved in organizational processes 
(Toyon, 2021; Othman et al., 2020 ). The integration of the two types of data is carried out at 
the interpretation stage through joint display. According to the status of the research dataset, all 
data used in the preparation of this manuscript are synthetic/simulative and are used as a 
research analysis model. 
 
Population and Sample 
The target population of the study is company personnel involved in the demand –
planning–execution–quality process flow. The quantitative sample consisted of 180

respondents from eight functions, namely Sales/Demand Planning, PPIC/PPC, Procurement, 
Warehouse, Production, Maintenance, QA/QC, and Logistics. Respondents included 
Staff/Specialist, Foreman/Coordinator, Supervisor, Engineer/Planner, Section Head, and 
Manager with a working period between 1–22 years. The sampling technique used purposive 
sampling, because respondents were selected based on their involvement in planning, 
coordinating, implementing, or controlling operations. 
The qualitative stage involved 12 key informants who were purposively selected because 
they were directly involved in the decision-making and flow of demand-plan–execution-quality 
information and had cross -functional exposure. The informants consisted of S ales/Demand 
Planning, PPIC/PPC, Production, QA/QC, Warehouse, Procurement, Maintenance, Logistics, 
and Operations personnel with a working period of 6 –18 years. The composition allowed for 
perspective to be obtained from the planning level to operational decision-making. 
 
Research Variables and Instruments 
Quantitative data were collected using a questionnaire of 23 items with a Likert scale of 
1–5. The Cross-Functional Coordination construct was measured by five items (CFC1–CFC5) 
which included the accuracy of information exchange, the effectiveness of coor dination 
meetings, the involvement of functions in decisions, the resolution of priority conflicts, and the 
alignment of KPIs. The conceptualization of this construct follows the thinking about cross -
functional coordination and organizational information p rocessing as discussed by Li et al. 
(2022). 
The Operational Process Integration construct consists of 12 items and is treated as a 
multidimensional construct that includes: Demand Forecasting (DF1 –DF3), Production 
Planning and Control (PPC1–PPC3), Production Execution (PE1–PE3), and Quality Assurance 
(QA1–QA3). The development of the dimensions refers conceptually to the study of planning 
and S&OP integration (Kreuter et al., 2022) , process integration and operational performance 
Ganbold et al. (2021) , and quality integration in modern manufacturing (Mahin et al., 2024) . 
The items were not adopted verbatim from a previous scale, but were structured contextually 
based on the constructs used in the research. Operational Performance constructs are measured 
through six items (OP1–OP6) that represent delivery accuracy, output achievement, defect and 
rework control, lead time efficiency, schedule adherence, and flexibility to changes in demand. 
In addition to the questionnaire, the study used operational documentation data for 24 
months, January 2025–December 2026, on three product families, resulting in 72 observations. 
The indicators analyzed included actual demand, forecast, MAPE, production p lan, actual 
production, schedule adherence, OEE, defect rate, rework rate, on -time delivery, inventory 
days, and downtime. 
 
Validity and Reliability 
Evaluation of quantitative instruments was carried out through a measurement model. 
Convergent validity was assessed based on outer loading ≥0.70 and Average Variance 
Extracted (AVE) ≥0.50, while construct reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha an d 
composite reliability ≥0.70. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the Heterotrait –
Monotrait Ratio (HTMT). The evaluation followed the latest practice of PLS -SEM which 
emphasizes testing measurement models before interpretation of structural relation ships (Hair 
& Alamer, 2022; Sarstedt et al., 2022). 
 
Qualitative Data Collection 
Qualitative data was collected through semi -structured interviews with 12 informants. 
Each informant answered eight questions that addressed: demand information flow, constraints 
in translating forecasts into production plans, communication of schedule cha nges, actual 
production monitoring, quality feedback, effectiveness of cross -functional coordination,

changes in performance indicators, and integration improvement priorities. This stage resulted 
in 96 units of interview answers. From the data, 48 main citations were used for the structured 
coding process. The analysis was directed at the themes of demand information 
synchronization, production planning alignment, execution visibility, quality feedback, 
coordination and decision -making, as well as shared KPIs and operational performance. The 
thematic analysis procedure follows the principles of data introd uction, coding, theme 
formation, theme review, and interpretation as described by Byrne (2022). 
 
Research Procedure 
The research was carried out through five stages. First, the constructs and research 
indicators were determined based on conceptual models and literature review. Second, the 
quantitative stage was carried out through the collection of questionnaire data fr om 180 
respondents and the processing of operational data for 24 months. Third, quantitative results 
were used to identify key relationships and operational issues that required further explanation. 
Fourth, interviews were conducted with 12 key informants and analyzed thematically. Fifth, 
survey results, operational data, and interview results were integrated through joint displays to 
identify convergence and explanation of the relationship between cross -functional 
coordination, operational process integration, and performance. 
 
Data Analysis 
Quantitative analysis was carried out in several stages. Descriptive statistics were used to 
describe respondent characteristics and the distribution of answers. Pearson correlation was 
used as an initial analysis to see the direction and strength of the r elationship between cross -
functional coordination, operational process integration, and operational performance. Further 
structural model testing was carried out with Partial Least Squares –Structural Equation 
Modeling (PLS-SEM) as the model includes latent constructs and integration constructs that 
are multidimensional in nature. PLS -SEM is also suitable for research oriented towards 
explaining and predicting relationships between variables (Hair & Alamer, 2022; Sarstedt et al., 
2022). The structural analysis is geared towards testing the following three hypotheses: 
H1: Cross -functional coordination has a positive effect on the integration of operational 
processes. 
H2: Integration of operational processes has a positive effect on operational performance. 
H3: Cross-functional coordination has a positive effect on operational performance. 
PLS-SEM is conducted using SmartPLS 4, while Microsoft Excel is used for dataset 
management, calculation and comparison of operational KPIs, and the preparation of joint 
displays. Qualitative data analysis is carried out through thematic coding in a structured manner 
with spreadsheets. In the final stage, quantitative results, operational KPI data, and interview 
themes are compared to obtain meta -inference on how cross -functional coordination 
strengthens process integration and supports operational performance.

forecast–plan–actual–quality data integration, while two answers emphasize a single source of 
operational data, escalation rules, and forecast update discipline.

(r=0.444; β=0.444; p<0.001), while qualitative data showed the existence of a coordination 
mechanism through daily meetings, update of request information, revision of schedules, and 
cross-functional confirmations. 
For H2, Operational Process Integration had the strongest relationship with Operational 
Performance (r=0.627; β=0.561; p<0.001). In parallel, operational data showed an increase in 
Schedule Adherence, OEE, and On -Time Delivery as well as a decrease in MAPE Forecast, 
Defect Rate, Rework Rate, Inventory Days, and Downtime between the two 12-month periods. 
For H3, the bivariate relationship between Cross -Functional Coordination and 
Operational Performance reached r=0.396 (p<0.001), while the direct effect after Operational 
Process Integration was incorporated into the model remained positive and significant

decision-making, coordination of schedule changes, resolution of quality issues, and the need

Discussion 
The results show that cross -functional coordination is an important factor in shaping 
operational process integration in the demand forecasting –production planning and control–
production execution–quality assurance series. The effect of cross -functional coordination on 
operational process integration was β=0.444 (p<0.001), with R²=0.197, indicating that the 
increase in communication, decision-making, and priority alignment between departments is in 
line with the increasingly integrated operational processes. The qualitative findings clarify the 
mechanism: demand changes need to be translated through forecast updates, schedule revisions, 
cross-functional confirmations, and daily coordination before they can become production 
decisions. This pattern is in line with the information processing view that places cross -
functional coordination as an organization's ability to meet increasing information needs when 
processes become increasingly interdependent. Li et al. (2022) found that cross -functional 
system, process, and team coordination strengthens information processing capabilities and 
supports operational performance. Similarly, Kreuter et al. (2022) and Bhalla et al. (2023) 
explain that S&OP creates alignment between demand and operations through cross-functional 
decisions. Thus, H1 is not only statistically supported, but also derives an operational 
explanation that coordination becomes a means to transform information scatt ered between 
parts into consistent decisions throughout the process flow. 
The strongest finding was found in the relationship between Operational Process 
Integration and Operational Performance (β=0.561; p<0.001), with a correlation of r=0.627. 
When Operational Process Integration and Cross-Functional Coordination were taken together, 
the model was able to explain 41.0% variation in Operational Performance. The results showed 
that the greatest benefit did not simply come from the existence of individual forecasting, 
planning, execution, or quality assurance, but from the company' s ability to connect the four 
processes. Operationally, the pattern was consistent with changes in KPIs during the 
observation period: MAPE Forecast decreased from 10.51% to 8.20%, Schedule Adherence 
increased from 88.27% to 92.45%, OEE increased from 73.5 2% to 77.77%, and On -Time 
Delivery increased from 89.69% to 93.57%. At the same time, the Defect Rate decreased from 
2.86% to 2.26%, the Rework Rate from 1.31% to 1.03%, the Inventory Days from 26.26 to 
23.32 days, and the Downtime from 52.70 to 45.32 hours. Simultaneous changes in forecasting, 
production, quality, inventory, and delivery show that operational performance is 
multidimensional, as stated by Battesini et al. (2021) , and cannot be explained through just one 
production indicator.

These results reinforce the findings of Ganbold et al. (2021) , who showed that integration 
is related to quality, delivery, cost, inventory, customer service, and flexibility, and Özkanlısoy 
(2024) , who found that integration contributes positively to performance in the manufacturing 
context. However, this study expands the focus of the literature. Ganbold et al. (2021) placed 
integration in the context of supply chain integration and information technology capabilities, 
while Özkanlısoy (2024) linked it to Industry 4.0. This study focuses on the internal integration 
of operational processes, i.e. how demand forecasting is translated into production planning and 
control, passed on to production execution, then controlled and returned through qual ity 
assurance. Thus, integration in this study is not only the integration of information or 
relationships between organizations, but the connection of decision processes and 
implementation within the manufacturing system. This perspective is also in line with the study 
of Khannan et al. (2025) , which shows the need for the integration of production planning with 
broader manufacturing activities. 
The quality assurance dimension provides significance in the integration chain. The 
decrease in the Defect Rate of around 21.12% relative and the Rework Rate of around 21.32% 
between the two periods shows that the improvement in performance occurs not only in the 
volume and accuracy of schedules, but also in quality results. Interviews show that defect 
information is returned through QC reports and daily meetings, while recurring problems are 
forwarded to cause analysis and corrective actions along with pro duction. This supports the 
idea of Quality 4.0 that quality needs to be placed as part of the information system and 
decision-making, not just the final inspection. Mahin et al. (2024) emphasize that the integration 
of processes, technology, leadership, and quality management are important components in 
achieving operational excellence. In parallel, Arcidiacono & Schupp (2024) found that 
improving manufacturing capabilities can contribute to the dimensions of cost, quality, 
delivery, and flexibility. In this study, the evidence emerged in the form of a relationship 
between quality feedback and production execution as well as the improvement of several KPIs 
simultaneously. 
H3 also gained support, but provided more nuanced findings. Bivariately, the relationship 
between Cross -Functional Coordination and Operational Performance reached r=0.396 
(p<0.001). However, after Operational Process Integration was incorporated into the model, the 
direct effect of coordination on performance dropped to β=0.147 (p=0.024), much smaller than 
the effect of Operational Process Integration of β=0.561. This pattern suggests that coordination 
does have direct consequences for performance, for exa mple through decision acceleration, 
material shortage resolution, schedule changes, or responses to quality issues, but the benefits 
of coordination are greater when the coordination is actually realized into process integration. 
These findings are consist ent with Özkanlısoy (2024) , who found that integration plays an 
intermediary role in the relationship between Industry 4.0 capabilities and performance. 
However, this study did not formally test the mediation hypothesis so the pattern should be 
understood as an indication of mechanism, not statistical evidence of mediation. 
These findings also help explain why the literature does not always have a uniform 
influence on integration. Wong et al. (2025) show that integration does not universally result 
in improved performance because each dimension of integration can play a different role and 
the combination determines the final outcome. El Mokadem & Khalaf (2023) also found that 
the relationship between integration and operational performance is contingent on supply chain 
strategies. In this study, there are still a number of barriers that indicate that integration is not 
yet fully optimal: sudden changes in demand still require manual confirmation, priority changes 
can occur after frozen schedules, data sources are not fully integrated, and KPIs between 
departments still need alignment. This may explain why coordination explains only 19.7% of 
variations in Operational Process Integration. In other words, integration is not determined by 
coordination alone; material availability, machine capacity, changeovers, equipment reliability,

frozen schedule disciplines, data quality, and information infrastructure also have the potential 
to contribute to integration variations that the model has not yet explained. 
The qualitative results reinforce this interpretation by showing that the integration 
problem is cross-process. On the demand side, there is a need for faster forecast updates and 
communication of priority changes. On the planning side, the stability of fr ozen schedules, 
materials, capacity, and changeovers is a concern. In execution, informants emphasize the 
visibility of actual output, downtime, schedule attainment, and plan–actual deviation. In quality 
assurance, the effectiveness of feedback defects and corrective actions are key. Five answers 
explicitly point to the need for an integrated dashboard from demand to quality, the other five 
emphasize shared KPIs, meeting standardization, and forecast –plan–actual–quality data 
integration, while two answers emphasize a single source of operational data, escalation rules, 
and forecast update discipline. Thus, qualitative data show that integration does not stop at 
information exchange, but requires the same decision -making structure and process visibility 
across functions. This is in line with the development of collaborative manufacturing, which 
emphasizes the integration of production and planning as a mechanism to improve the ability 
to respond to operational needs Khannan et al. (2025). 
The novelty of this research lies in the unification of four internal processes —demand 
forecasting, production planning and control, production execution, and quality assurance—as 
one construct of Operational Process Integration, then placing Cross -Functional Coordination 
as a managerial antecedent that is linked to operational performance. The previous literature 
has tested S&OP, supply chain integration, Industry 4.0, smart manufacturing, or Quality 4.0 
separately. This study brings together the upstream process of demand information with the 
planning, production realization, and quality feedback processes in one demand –planning–
execution–quality framework. Another contribution comes from the use of three layers of 
evidence: the perception of 180 responden ts, 72 KPI observations over 24 months, and 96 
answers from 12 informants. With this design, changes in operational performance are not only 
reflected in the perception score, but can also be seen in the patterns of MAPE Forecast, 
Schedule Adherence, OEE, defect, rework, OTD, inventory, and downtime. Theoretically, the 
results expand the perspective of integration from mere structural linkage to process -based 
operational capability, i.e. the ability of organizations to continuously convert demand 
information into plans, production actions, and quality feedback. 
The practical implication of these findings is that management should not improve 
forecasting, PPIC, production, or quality in isolation. The first priority is to build a single source 
of truth that connects forecast –plan–actual–quality so that each functi on uses consistent 
information. Second, cross -functional dashboards need to display at least Forecast MAPE, 
schedule adherence, actual versus plan, OEE, downtime, defect/rework, inventory, and OTD. 
Third, daily operational meetings need to use shared KPIs, clear PICs, completion deadlines, 
and escalation rules for changes in demand or schedule. Fourth, quality assurance needs to be 
maintained as a closed feedback loop so that quality findings can influence production 
execution and subsequent planning. This approach is closer to the principles of S&OP and 
collaborative manufacturing that emphasizes cross -functional decision alignment rather than 
simply digitizing individual processes. 
This study has several important limitations in interpreting the results. First, the research 
dataset is synthetic/simulative, so the statistical relationship figures and KPI patterns should be 
viewed as the result of the developed analysis model and canno t be generalized as empirical 
evidence in the field. Second, the single -case design limits the heterogeneity of the 
organizational context. Third, although 24 months of operational data are available, the main 
structural relationships use cross -sectional perception data, so the causal direction cannot be 
ascertained longitudinally. Fourth, the model only includes cross -functional coordination and 
process integration to explain performance, while technological factors, organizational culture, 
human resource competence, machine reliability, supply uncertainty, and production strategy

have not been analyzed. Fifth, the pattern of decline in the direct coefficient of 
CFC→performance after integration is included suggests the possibility of an indirect

The next research should use actual field data from several companies, longitudinal design, link 
the survey with actual ERP/MES/QMS data, compare industry types or manufacturing 
strategies, and explicitly test the mediating role of Operational Proce ss Integration and the 
possibility of moderation of environmental uncertainty or digital integration. 
 
CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
Based on the findings and discussion presented above, this study concludes that 
operational performance in manufacturing companies is strongly influenced by the integration 
of operational processes and cross-functional coordination. 
1. Cross-Functional Coordination has a positive effect on Operational Process Integration 
(β=0.444; p<0.001), while Operational Process Integration has the greatest influence on 
Operational Performance (β=0.561; p<0.001). Cross -Functional Coordination also ha s a 
smaller but significant direct influence on Operational Performance (β=0.147; p=0.024), 
with both predictors explaining 41.0% of performance variations. These results are 
supported by improvements in MAPE Forecast, Schedule Adherence, OEE, Defect Rate, 
Rework Rate, OTD, Inventory Days, and Downtime. 
2. The findings indicate that improving operational performance depends more strongly on the 
organization's ability to integrate processes than on direct cross -functional coordination 
alone. Therefore, demand forecasting, production planning and control, prod uction 
execution, and quality assurance should be managed as a single operational integration 
capability. Companies are advised to establish a single source of operational data, cross -
functional dashboards, shared KPIs, standardized coordination forums, cl ear PICs and 
escalation rules, and closed-loop quality feedback. 
3. Subsequent research should validate the proposed model using actual, multi -company, and 
longitudinal field data. Future studies should also formally test the mediating role of process 
integration and other contextual factors to strengthen the model's gener alization and causal 
capabilities. 
 
Limitation 
This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the 
findings. The use of synthetic data and the focus on a single manufacturing company in 
Surabaya limit the scope of generalizing the findings to broader manufacturing context s. In 
addition, this study does not specifically examine the mediating role of operational process 
integration or other contextual factors. These limitations provide opportunities for future 
research to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the relationships examined in this 
study. 
 
Rcomendation 
Future research should validate the proposed model using actual data from multiple 
manufacturing companies and longitudinal observations. Further studies should also examine 
the mediating role of operational process integration and relevant contextual factors to 
strengthen the model's generalizability and causal interpretation.
