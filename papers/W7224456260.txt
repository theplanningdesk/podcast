ABSTRACT
Motivated by the energy-intensive heat-treatment operations of a European lead-acid battery manu-
facturer, this study addresses energy- and labour-aware scheduling for a stochastic batch-processing 
system with waiting-time constraints and programme-compatibility requirements. We formalise 
the problem as a two-level optimisation decomposed into five sub-decisions and design a generic 
scheduling framework spanning from simple Rule-Based Heuristics (RH) to rules automatically syn-
thesised by Genetic Programming (GP) for a single sub-decision. All approaches are evaluated in an 
agent-based discrete-event simulation mirroring the company’s production. Optimising all five sub-
decisions using handcrafted rules (Best RH) significantly reduces costs by 40.8% at low and 85.4% 
at high utilisation over Current Practice, with substantially improved solution robustness. Substitut-
ing a single sub-decision with GP-evolved rules reveals both what evolved rules achieve alone and 
what potential handcrafted rules leave. At high utilisation, it yields a significant 41.9% cost reduction 
over Current Practice, whereas on the already optimised Best RH the cost gain is marginal and non-
significant – but robustness still improves markedly, a critical advantage in stochastic environments. 
The central contribution is methodological: partitioning the problem by decision structure rather 
than machine structure reduces complexity enough for handcrafted rules to remain competitive with 
evolved ones.

1. Introduction
Globalisation and price transparency pressure manufac-
turers to deliver high-quality products at competitive 
prices. In Europe, unit labour costs increased by 4.7% 
in 2024 while productivity declined by 0.2% (European 
Central Bank 2025). Energy represents an equally crit-
ical cost driver, with industry accounting for 24.6% of 
the European Union’ s final energy consumption (Eurostat 
2025). With EU climate policy and carbon prices pro-
jected to reach e126/tCO2 by 2030 (Glushchenko 2025), 
these trends force manufacturers to lower energy use and 
improve shop-floor productivity. Scheduling governs job 
execution via machine allocation, batching, and sequenc-
ing under operational constraints (Pinedo 2016), offering 
significant cost reduction potential without major capital 
expenditure (Noegraheni and Nuradli 2016). The follow-
ing case from energy-intensive manufacturing illustrates 
this.

1.1. Real-world case: energy-intensive 
manufacturing
A European lead-acid battery manufacturer faces a 
stochastic scheduling problem. Lead plates – the main 
battery component – arrive stochastically from pre-
production on pallets and must be assigned to cham-
bers for energy-intensive heat-treatment within strict 
waiting-time limits. Violations result in scrap. Each pallet 
requires a specific heat-treatment programme defining a 
temperature-humidity profile over time, which directly 
governs energy consumption. While pallets with com-
patible programmes can be batched together, programme 
mixing may increase energy demand. Current Practice
(CP) schedules by job ID, prohibits programme mix-
ing, and often starts heat-treatment with residual cham-
ber capacity. Heat-treatment serves two purposes: (1) 
completing chemical curing and (2) reducing relative 
humidity to target levels. Actual energy requirements

1.2. Research gap
While energy-aware and labour-aware scheduling have 
each received growing attention, integrated approaches 
remain limited. In deterministic environments, Gong 
et al. (2019) combine energy and labour in flexible 
job shops, as do Shao, Shao, and Pi (2022) in dis-
tributed hybrid flow shops. Most recently, Schorn and 
Mönch (2026) combine energy, batch processing, and 
waiting-time constraints, but do not consider labour. 
None of these, however, address a stochastic batch-
processing environment with programme-compatibility 
requirements.
Under uncertainty, prior work is sparser. Bokor et al. 
(2026) address a stochastic job shop with a threshold-
based heuristic for joint machine stopping and shift 
staffing, but outside a batch-processing setting. The clos-
est work, W ang et al. (2016), schedules heat-treatment 
batches under energy uncertainty, but represents this 
uncertainty through fuzzy rather than stochastic mod-
elling and considers neither programme-compatibility 
effects on energy nor labour. In our case, stochastic 
resource consumption gives rise to stopping problems 
that themselves drive the processing-time decisions, with 
the subsequent inspection adding labour as a further cost 
driver. T ogether with strict waiting-time constraints, this 
process uncertainty raises both the scrap risk and the 
scheduling difficulty – a combination that no existing 
approach addresses.
1.3. Contribution
W e address this gap for a stochastic multi-item, multi-
stage production system that jointly exhibits five char-
acteristics: energy, labour, uncertainty, batch processing, 
and waiting-time constraints. T o our knowledge, no prior 
study addresses more than three of these jointly. The 
core of our work is methodological: we decompose this 
complex problem into a small set of well-defined sub-
decisions – each addressed by a generic, energy- and 
labour-aware approach. The study makes four contribu-
tions.
First, we formalise this problem from the real-world 
heat-treatment operations of a lead-acid battery manu-
facturer, yielding a decision aid that uniquely combines 
all five characteristics.
Second, we decompose the problem into a two-level 
optimisation with five sub-decisions. At the system level, 
three scheduling decisions are optimised: (1) Pallet Eval-
uation, (2) Chamber Dispatching, and (3) Chamber Early-
Start. At the chamber level, planned processing times are 
optimised for (4) Main Process Steps and (5) Rework Pro-
cess Steps to address two independent stochastic stopping 
problems. An established decomposition approach in 
scheduling is to split a multi-machine problem into mul-
tiple single-machine subproblems (Mönch et al. 2007). 
In contrast, we partition by decision, yielding function-
ally distinct sub-decisions that can each be addressed by 
a dedicated approach.
Third, we design a generic scheduling framework 
spanning from simple Rule-Based Heuristics (RH), rely-
ing on basic shop-floor information, to rules automati-
cally synthesised from real-time shop-floor conditions. 
T o synthesise these rules, we employ Genetic Program-
ming (GP) in its classical single-tree form (Koza 1992) 
and use it as a benchmark for a single sub-decision.
Fourth, we conduct a comprehensive empirical evalu-
ation using an agent-based discrete-event simulation that 
mirrors the case company’ s lead plate production. Per-
formance is evaluated across three utilisation scenarios 
in terms of total costs (i.e. scrap, energy, and labour), 
production logistics indicators, and solution robustness, 
measured by the coefficient of variation (CV) across 
replications. W e evaluate all combinations of approaches 
to identify the best-performing configuration (Best RH), 
which is then benchmarked against Current Practice (CP 
Base). Finally, we substitute Pallet Evaluation with GP-
evolved priority rules while fixing all other sub-decisions, 
in two configurations: (1) applied to CP Base and (2) inte-
grated with the Best RH. This reveals both what evolved 
rules achieve on their own and what potential hand-
crafted rules leave.
This study addresses the following research questions:
1. How can a complex stochastic scheduling problem 
be decomposed into well-defined sub-decisions?
2. How can energy- and labour-related considerations 
be jointly integrated into stochastic batch-processing 
scheduling with waiting-time constraints?
3. What is the cost and robustness performance of 
Rule-Based Heuristics relative to Current Practice?
4. What are the effects of Rule-Based Heuristics on 
production logistics indicators?

Results show that optimising all five sub-decisions 
with handcrafted rules (Best RH) significantly reduces 
costs by 40.8% at low and 85.4% at high utilisation com-
pared to CP Base, with substantially improved robust-
ness. At high utilisation, substituting a single sub-
decision with GP-evolved rules significantly reduces 
costs by 41.9% when applied to CP Base, whereas on the 
already optimised Best RH the cost gain is marginal but 
robustness still improves markedly.
Although tailored to lead plate production, the 
decomposition and framework are expected to generalise 
to other energy-intensive batch-processing systems with 
stochastic process completion, waiting-time constraints, 
compatibility restrictions, and batch-composition effects. 
Examples include semiconductor diffusion furnaces, 
pharmaceutical batch reactors, food processing ovens, 
automotive paint curing, and glass tempering furnaces.
2. Related work
Production scheduling research increasingly addresses 
energy and labour as joint cost drivers. On the energy 
side, Gahm et al. (2016) categorise energy-efficient 
scheduling along three dimensions – energetic coverage, 
energy supply, and energy demand – identifying energy 
demand optimisation as the most widely studied. Within 
this category, scheduling can reduce energy use during 
non-value-added phases (e.g. idle times) and processing 
(e.g. speed scaling, batch composition). On the labour 
side, the dual-resource constrained literature (J. Xu, Xu, 
and Xie 2011) treats workforce as a resource alongside 
machines, typically as an allocation problem. This study 
addresses both dimensions: batch composition affects 
energy demand, and labour enters through the inspec-
tion effort required to verify completion, which increases 
with rework.
Moreover, real production systems are subject to 
uncertainty, for instance in demand, processing times, 
and energy requirements (Li and Ierapetritou 2008). Y et 
scheduling research has predominantly addressed deter-
ministic settings, limiting practical applicability (Bassière 
and Benatia 2025; Herroelen and Leus 2005). Under 
such stochastic conditions, RH – also referred to as dis-
patching or priority rules – offer a computationally effi-
cient alternative, particularly as modelling complexity 
increases (Pinedo 2016). More recently, such rules have 
been complemented by learned dispatching policies for 
dynamic settings (Liu, Piplani, and T oro 2022).
Since their introduction in early scheduling theory 
(Conway, Maxwell, and Miller 1967), these heuristics 
select the next job using simple criteria based on system-
state attributes (Panwalkar and Iskander 1977). Classical 
rules such as first-in-first-out (FIFO), earliest-due-date 
(EDD), or shortest-processing-time (SPT) remain widely 
used for their simplicity and robustness. EDD even gains 
theoretical support: Bryant, Lakner, and Pinedo (2022) 
prove optimality of a preemptive EDD variant for min-
imising expected tardiness on heterogeneous parallel 
servers with exponentially distributed processing times, 
motivating slack-based prioritisation.
Batch processing adds a requirement: heuristics must 
account for job compatibility and capacity utilisation 
when forming batches (Fowler and Mönch 2022). W ahl, 
Gahm, and T uma (2025) classify batch-scheduling prob-
lems and review their solution methods. For incompat-
ible job families – as in heat-treatment, where different 
programmes cannot share a batch – Mehta and Uzsoy 
(1998) show that dynamic programming yields optimal 
solutions for fixed family counts and machine capacities, 
though heuristics remain necessary for larger instances.
The problem studied here is characterised by five 
features that jointly drive its difficulty: energy con-
sumption, labour effort, stochastic process completion, 
batch processing with programme-compatibility require-
ments, and strict waiting-time constraints. They have 
been addressed effectively in isolation and partly linked, 
but never jointly. Table 1 compares the approaches most 
closely aligned with our problem, those sharing at least 
two of its defining characteristics. 
Several studies combine two or three of these char-
acteristics, but always as partial subsets. Gong et al. 
(2019) jointly optimise energy and labour cost in a flex-
ible job shop, matching worker levels to shift-dependent 
wages under time-varying electricity prices. Similarly,

these, Xiang et al. (2026) address wafer batch schedul-

uncertainty in heating times and consumption, though 
modelled with fuzzy rather than stochastic parameters 
and without labour or waiting-time constraints.
Integrating these interacting requirements into a sin-
gle set of handcrafted rules is difficult (Branke et al. 
2016). A common strategy for managing this complexity 
is decomposition. Existing approaches often decompose 
along the machine structure, as in shifting-bottleneck 
procedures solving one machine subproblem at a time 
(Mönch et al. 2007). Others separate only a small num-
ber of decisions, such as machine allocation from worker 
assignment (Müller and Kress 2022) or batch formation 
from job assignment (Xiang et al. 2026). What remains 
missing is a decomposition along the decision struc-
ture: a formalised partition of the full decision space into 
a small set of functionally distinct sub-decisions, each 
addressable by a dedicated approach.
W e address each sub-decision with a rule-based 
heuristic, which raises a natural question: how do auto-
matically synthesised rules compare to such handcrafted 
ones? Genetic Programming, introduced by Koza (1992), 
has become an established method for evolving schedul-
ing rules (Zhang et al. 2024). Such evolved rules have 
been shown to outperform established priority rules in 
dynamic flexible job shops, whether via gene expression 
programming (Ozturk, Bahadir, and T eymourifar 2019) 
or tree-based GP (Braune et al. 2022).
3. Problem description
This section introduces the lead plate production pro-
cess, presents the scheduling decisions and chamber-level 
processing, formalises the optimisation problem, and 
illustrates their interaction in an example.
3.1. Lead plate production at the case company
Figure 1 presents the lead plate production process via 
value stream mapping. The facility produces 21 distinct 
lead plate types, first differentiated by electrical charge 
(positive/negative), then by design-specific characteris-
tics. As battery assembly withdraws plates from fin-
ished inventory, this study’ s scope ends at heat-treatment
completion.
Lead plate demand is satisfied through production 
jobs generated by a reorder-point system. A job is 
released whenever the inventory position falls below

the reorder point (Hopp and Spearman 2011). Each job 
corresponds to a single lead plate type with demand-
dependent lot sizes.
Pre-production involves two sequential operations: 
plate forming and pasting (i.e. applying lead oxide and 
additives). The process flow depends on the plate type: 
negative plates use continuous forming with a subse-
quent FIFO buffer, while positive plates use the expanded 
metal technique with immediate pasting and no buffer-
ing. Job assignment to one of four parallel lines depends 
on charge type, with the lower-loaded line selected based 
on standard-time shop load. As operations are stochas-
tic, the schedule is updated dynamically after each pallet 
completion using Drum-Buffer-Rope forward schedul-
ing, as detailed in Bokor, Seiringer, and Altendorfer 
(2025).
After pre-production, plates are palletised and buff-
ered, awaiting heat-treatment in one of the 31 chambers. 
Heat-treatment must start within a maximum allow-
able waiting-time to avoid scrap from improper curing 
(see Figure 1), and serves two purposes: (1) strength-
ening the paste microstructure through chemical cur-
ing and (2) reducing relative humidity to ensure con-
sistent acid ratios. Lead plate types follow one of four 
predefined programmes – Negative, Positive, Positive-
with-V aporisation, or Start/Stop – each with a unique 
temperature-humidity profile over time. Details are 
shown in Bokor, Altendorfer, and Matta (2025). These 
profiles govern energy consumption; deviations reduce 
efficiency and product quality. While energy input accu-
mulates deterministically based on these profiles, actual 
requirements are batch-specific and stochastic, driven by 
moisture, thickness, and thermal losses.
After processing, batches are inspected to verify com-
pletion targets: curing is assessed via the proportion 
of free metallic lead, while relative humidity is mea-
sured directly. Failure to meet either target triggers a 
rework cycle, incurring energy and labour penalties 
from chamber reheating and repeated inspection. This 
uncertainty creates two independent stopping problems: 
determining the optimal times for (1) completing cur-
ing and (2) reaching relative-humidity targets, all while 
minimising energy consumption. An analytical solu-
tion is intractable, as the minimum required energy 
remains unknown until completion and varies stochas-
tically across batches. Short planned processing times 
increase rework probability, whereas excessive ones waste 
energy and prolong chamber occupation.
3.2. Scheduling decisions and chamber-level 
processing
The lead plate production process (see Figure 1) reveals 
a naturally decomposable scheduling problem with two 
levels and five sub-decisions. Scheduling occurs at the 
system level across all chambers n ∈ N, while heat-
treatment is executed at the chamber level to address two 
independent stochastic stopping problems (see Section 
3.1). Figure 2 illustrates this: blue-shaded elements 
denote scheduling decisions, orange-shaded ones the 
process steps where planned processing times are opti-
mised. Table 2 summarises the notation.
Let I be the set of all pallets and k ∈ { 1, . . . , K} the 
index of decision points tk ∈ T during the observation 
time T. Let I′(tk) ⊆ I represent the unassigned pallets 
and I′′(tk) ⊆ I′(tk) those located at the buffer before 
the chambers (see Figure 1). Available chambers are 
denoted by n ∈ N′(tk) ⊆ N and running chambers by 
n ∈ N′′(tk) ⊆ N. A decision point tk is triggered when an 
unassigned pallet arrives at the buffer or a running cham-

three scheduling decisions are made:
1. Pallet Evaluation – for each unassigned pallet i ∈
I′(tk) compute an evaluation score d(1) i (tk) ∈ R+ 0 (∈
R under GP; see Section 4.3). A value of zero indi-
cates that pallet i is not assignable at tk due to applied 
rules, while higher values imply earlier assignment 
priority.
2. Chamber Dispatching – starting with the pallet i
with the highest evaluation score, for each i ∈ I′(tk)
evaluate all available chambers n ∈ N′(tk) by com-

and 0 otherwise.

Once heat-treatment starts, the first stochastic stop-
ping problem – completing curing – is addressed. The 
process begins with the maturation phase (8), followed by 
metallic-lead inspection to assess curing completion (9). 
If curing is not completed, rework is required (10). Since 
inspection requires opening the chamber, rework con-
sists of a re-warm-up phase followed by post-maturation, 
and may repeat across multiple cycles until completion. 
After curing, the relative-humidity stopping problem is 
addressed by initiating drying, preceded by a manda-
tory re-warm-up phase (11). Following the same prin-
ciple, rework (i.e. post-drying preceded by re-warm-
up) repeats until the relative-humidity target is reached 
(12–13). Once both targets are met, the chamber is 
unloaded (14). Re-warm-up durations are fixed by equip-
ment settings. In contrast, planned processing times for 
maturation, drying, post-maturation, and post-drying 
are determined by predefined processing-energy factors. 
Since identical factors apply to both stopping problems, 
these are generalised into two chamber-level decisions. 
Let d(4) ∈ R+ denote the processing-energy factor for 
Main Process Steps (maturation/drying) and d(5) ∈ R+
for Rework Process Steps (post-maturation/post-drying). 
These factors specify the cumulative energy input as 
a multiple of the expected minimum requirement. For 
example, a factor of 1.1 corresponds to a processing time 
supplying 10% more energy than the expected minimum.

4.2. Rule-based heuristics
W e propose nine Rule-Based Heuristics (RH) for the

4.2.1. Pallet evaluation
All Pallet Evaluation approaches restrict evaluation to 
buffered pallets I′′(tk). Preliminary experiments showed 
that extending evaluation to all unassigned pallets I′(tk)
increased costs. Assigning pallets still in pre-production 
blocks chamber slots prematurely, preventing more 
urgent pallets from being loaded and increasing scrap 
from waiting-time violations. For all approaches a, pal-

waiting-time wi (see Equation 14).
4.2.1.1. Least pallet slack time (LS). At CP, assignable

identical for all pallets, LS corresponds to FIFO by buffer 
arrival across jobs.

4.3. Genetic programming
Under approach a = GP, evolved rules serve as a bench-
mark for a single sub-decision – Pallet Evaluation. W e 
adopt the single-tree representation of Braune et al. 
(2022) and rely on the classical Genetic Programming

that dynamically combine system-state attributes. Unlike 
the handcrafted rules, the GP-evolved score follows a 
minimisation convention: a lower d(1),GP i indicates higher 
assignment priority. Consistent with the RH approaches, 
GP evaluation is restricted to buffered pallets ˜IGP(tk) =

5.1. Job generation
Job inter-arrival times follow log-normal distributions 
(CV = 0.20), with the mean varied across the three util-
isation scenarios of the numerical study. Each job rep-
resents one of 21 plate types with lot sizes sampled from 
plate-specific log-normal distributions (see Table 4: selec-
tion probabilities, means, CVs).
5.2. Pre-production
Setups at plate forming and pasting are required when 
switching between most plate types, with asymmetric 
changeover times depending on switching direction. Pro-
cessing times are also plate-type-dependent. The simula-
tion uses complete plate-type-specific setup matrices and 
processing times; due to company disclosure restrictions 
and the scope of this study (treating pre-production as a 
stochastic input process), these are not reported in detail. 
Table 4 reports demand-weighted means per operation 
and process route (calculated as sum-product of required 
times and plate-type selection probabilities), minimum, 
and maximum values. Setup and processing times follow 
log-normal distributions (CV = 0.20).
5.3. Heat-treatment
Loading and unloading follow log-normal distributions 
(mean = 1 min/pallet, CV = 0.20), with loading priori-
tised when both operations compete for the operator. 
The maximum allowable waiting-time for buffered pal-
lets is 12 h (see Equation 4). In contrast, all other heat-
treatment steps are batch-based and deterministic (i.e. 
realised and planned processing times match). Re-warm-
up durations are fixed at 60 min per cycle. Planned pro-
cessing times for Main Process Steps and Rework Process 
Steps are set via processing-energy factors (see Section 
3.2) and optimised in the numerical study.
Table 4 reports expected minimum energy require-
ments for each heat-treatment programme (CV = 0.30 
for all programmes), derived from metallic-lead inspection 
data (Bokor, Altendorfer, and Matta 2025). Batch-specific

The simulation declares curing complete or the relative-

Inspection effort enters the objective through the

5.4. Model validation
The simulation model was validated in three steps. First, 
all input parameters were drawn from the case com-
pany’ s 2024 operational data, as specified in Sections 
5.1–5.3 and Table 4, establishing input validity. Second, 
the model structure and logic were refined with the com-
pany’ s domain experts through plant visits, interviews, 
and repeated feedback rounds, confirming that the model 
represents the real production process and establishing 
structural validity. Third, the resulting outputs were pre-
sented to these experts, who assessed them as plausible 
and consistent with operational experience, supporting 
the model’ s face validity. Since the production process, 
decision logic, and optimisation model of Section 3 are 
implemented in this simulation, the validation includes 
all of these aspects.
6. Numerical study
W e conduct a two-stage numerical study to quantify 
cost-reduction potential and examine effects on system 
behaviour via production logistics indicators.
First, a full-factorial study evaluates all RH (includ-
ing CP) introduced in Section 4 across three utilisation 
scenarios, varied through mean job arrivals per day. The 
lowest utilisation represents the current company sce-
nario; medium and high scenarios increase utilisation by 
approximately 10% and 20%, respectively. Five heuris-
tics are assessed for Pallet Evaluation, four for Chamber 
Dispatching, and three for Chamber Early-Start, with five 
values tested for the maximum chamber waiting-time ¯τ
(see Equation 29). T o keep the experimental design uni-
form, all parameter combinations are enumerated even 
for heuristics not requiring ¯τ . For Main Process Steps and 
Rework Process Steps, five values are evaluated per factor 
to set planned processing times. Table 5 lists the investi-
gated parameters, their ranges (selected based on prelim-
inary runs), and the resulting full-factorial design. This 
design generates 22,500 parameter combinations, each 
replicated 20 times to account for stochastic variability, 
resulting in 450,000 simulation runs.

7. Numerical results
W e identify the configurations minimising the mean 
daily cost from the Global Objective Function (1), aver-
aged over 20 replications and reported per day over the 
150-day evaluation window. For each configuration, we 
report individual cost components – scrap S, energy E, 
labour L – along with four mean production logistics 
indicators: chamber utilisation U, the fraction of time 
a chamber is occupied; fill rate F, the ratio of loaded 
to maximum pallet capacity; heat-treatment time H, the 
chamber run time from first pallet loading until unload-
ing completion; and loading time V, the loading phase as 
a component of H.
7.1. Performance of the rule-based heuristics
W e compare the Base Setting with the Best Setting. For 
the Base Setting, we alter one scheduling decision while 
fixing all others at CP. For the Best Setting, we optimise 
everything else around the RH under study: the other 
two scheduling decisions and the processing-energy fac-
tors. This allows us to (1) isolate individual RH effects, 
(2) identify the best achievable solution for each RH, 
and (3) analyse the improvement potential over CP 
Base. Recall that under CP Base (see Section 4.1), each 
scheduling decision follows current company practice: 
single-job batching, waiting-time-based pallet ranking, 
and processing-energy factors set to 1.20 and 0.25.
W e focus on low (i.e. the current company scenario) 
and high utilisation, as the medium scenario shows simi-
lar but less pronounced effects. Results for medium utili-
sation are provided in the Supplemental Online Material; 
the configuration transfer in Section 7.3.2 covers all three 
scenarios. Tables 7–9 show the performance of Pallet 
Evaluation, Chamber Dispatching, and Chamber Early-
Start heuristics, respectively. CP Base appears in the top-
left cell in italic. Configurations with statistically lower

7.2. Performance of genetic programming
At high utilisation, we substitute Pallet Evaluation with 
GP-evolved rules in two configurations (see Section 6): 
applied to CP Base and integrated with the Best RH.
7.2.1. Cost and robustness of evolved rules
In the CP Base configuration, GP reduces costs from 
e24,057 to e13,971, a statistically significant 41.9% 
reduction (p < 0.001, α = 0.05). In addition, the CV 
across replications decreases by 26.2%, indicating higher 
robustness. In the Best RH configuration, the same substi-
tution reduces costs from e3502 to e3484, a statistically 
non-significant 0.5% reduction. Despite the marginal 
cost effect, the CV across replications decreases by 20.7%, 
substantially improving solution robustness. This limited 
improvement points to the effect of the two-level decom-
position: it reduces complexity to a point where the hand-
crafted slack-based heuristics already capture the essen-
tial scheduling logic, leaving little residual potential for 
GP (Zhang et al. 2024).
7.2.2. Analysis of evolved rules
T o understand why the evolved rules perform well, we 
examine their structure and the terminals they rely on. 
Figure 7 shows the best-performing rule of each configu-
ration as an expression tree (operators in blue, terminals 
in black), while Equations (31–32) give the correspond-
ing rules in compact form.
In the CP Base configuration, the best-performing 
evolved rule is:
d(1),GP i = (4.17 + 3.11∗I_ST) (3.08∗ O_RUI_B) (31)
The rule prioritises pallets by slack time I_ST in the 
numerator (coefficient 3.11). A smaller I_ST means less 
remaining slack, yielding a smaller score and thus higher 
priority under the minimisation convention, analogous 
to LS. The denominator O_RUI_B (coefficient 3.08) fur-
ther lowers the score for pallets from jobs already well 
represented in the buffer, accelerating their assignment.
In the Best RH configuration, the best-performing

T o assess whether this pattern generalises, we exam-

of each configuration. Slack time I_ST appears in all

These patterns all follow from the decomposition. 
Slack dominates because the waiting-time constraint 
imposes an implicit deadline. Pallets close to their limit 
must be assigned first, so slack directly captures the 
relevant urgency. Programme-related terminals are dis-
carded because programme compatibility is already gov-
erned by a separate sub-decision – Chamber Dispatching
– so programme information carries no useful signal at 
the Pallet Evaluation level. The choice of job terminal 
tracks the batching structure: under CP Base, batches are 
limited to a single job, so only same-job pallets already 
in the buffer can fill a batch; under Best RH, the IH
relaxation and time-based early-start extend this to same-
programme pallets across jobs shop-wide. In each case, 
GP selects the job terminal capturing the batchable pal-
lets (O_RUI_B when it is buffer-local, O_RUI when it is 
shop-wide), confirming that the decomposition assigns 
each concern to the sub-decision responsible for it.
7.3. Synthesis of the best configuration
After analysing the sub-decisions and the evolved rules 
separately, we now synthesise the results into the best-
performing configuration.
7.3.1. Cost structure
Table 10 summarises cost and robustness improvements 
across utilisation levels. The Best RH configuration sig-
nificantly outperforms CP Base – costs decrease by 40.8% 
at low and 85.4% at high utilisation, while CV across 
replications decreases by 86.6% and 87.1%, respectively. 
Independent of the utilisation level, the best configura-
tion combines LS for Pallet Evaluation, IH for Chamber 
Dispatching, and TC for Chamber Early-Start with ¯τ =
30h. Only the processing-energy factors differ, decreasing 
as capacity tightens.
Scrap is the dominant driver of this improvement, 
falling from e1026/e19,780 to e6/e31 (low/high util-
isation). At high utilisation – a 20% demand increase 
on the same chamber capacity (see Section 6) – CP 
Base is overwhelmed by scrap, whereas the optimised 
schedule absorbs the additional demand almost without 
scrap. Scheduling therefore unlocks substantial through-
put potential through optimised decisions alone, without 
adding capacity. Beyond scrap, energy decreases mod-
erately at high utilisation while labour stays essentially 
flat. At low utilisation, scrap remains the largest share, 
but energy contributes a substantial part of the reduction. 
The cost-component view also confirms the decompo-
sition: since GP re-optimises only Pallet Evaluation, its 
improvement over Best RH is confined to scrap (falling 
from e31 to e13), while energy and labour remain 
unchanged.
However, the cost improvements reported above must 
be interpreted with caution due to production system 
stochastics (see Table 4), particularly in lot sizes. Cases 
where lot sizes exceed chamber capacity by one pal-
let would require manual planner intervention – such 
exceptions are not covered in this study.
7.3.2. Configuration transfer
While Table 10 reports robustness across replications, 
a further question is whether a configuration remains 
effective when utilisation changes. Since the full-factorial 
design evaluates every parameter combination in every 
scenario (see Section 6), the performance of each opti-
mum is known in all three. The scheduling decisions 
are unaffected across all three scenarios, whereas the 
processing-energy factors decrease. As the main factor 
applies to every batch and the rework factor only to those 
requiring additional cycles, the broader lever is used first: 
d(4) decreases from 1.10 to 1.05 at medium utilisation, 
while d(5) follows only at high utilisation, decreasing 
from 0.35 to 0.30.
Table 11 reports the resulting transfer matrix, with 
rows denoting the scenario a configuration was opti-
mised for and columns the scenario it is applied to. Trans-
ferring a configuration costs at most 4.7%, and the loss is 
asymmetric: only transfers into the high-utilisation sce-
nario are significantly worse, while all others lie within 
the confidence interval of the scenario optimum. These 
losses are far below the 40.8% to 85.4% improvement 
over CP Base, confirming that the configuration is robust 
across utilisation scenarios.

7.3.3. Impact of sub-decisions
T o identify the impact of the individual sub-decisions, 
we take for each sub-decision its worst-performing rule 
at the Best Setting. Comparing the resulting costs shows 
what a poor choice imposes even under an other-
wise optimal configuration. The single-job restriction 
shared by Chamber Dispatching and Chamber Early-Start
reaches e9651 at high utilisation (see Tables 8 and 9) 
– more than double the e4680 reached by the weakest 
Pallet Evaluation rule. The reason is that single-job batch-
ing keeps the fill rate low, so chambers stay occupied 
and fewer are available to process waiting pallets, which 
drives scrap. By contrast, the choice of rule within Pal-
let Evaluation – the only sub-decision addressed by both 
handcrafted and evolved rules – is far less consequential. 
Table 12 shows cost and robustness across replications of 
all Pallet Evaluation approaches at high utilisation. At the 
Best Setting, LS improves over CP by 1.1% in cost and 
37.0% in CV , and substituting the rule with GP improves 
over the already optimised Best RH by only 0.5% (see 
Section 7.2.1).
7.4. Managerial insights
Six key insights for production managers emerge. (1) 
Under high utilisation, items should be prioritised by 
how close they are to their waiting-time limit (slack); 
relaxing job-level restrictions adds only marginal benefit 
(CP/LS). (2) Batches should group items with the same 
process programme rather than mixing them to raise fill 
rates. Mixed batches run longer and at higher energy 
demand, and the resulting scrap outweighs the fill-rate 
benefit (IH). (3) Switching from a completion-based to 
a time-based trigger for starting a batch only pays off 
once the upstream grouping and assignment decisions 
are sound. On its own, a time trigger starts batches too 
early and wastes capacity; combined with good grouping, 
it raises fill rates and frees capacity (TC). (4) Some deci-
sions are make-or-break: a restrictive dispatching rule 
caps overall performance no matter how well the remain-
ing decisions are tuned (CP). (5) Automated rule synthe-
sis pays off where scheduling is currently poorly config-
ured, delivering large cost and robustness gains (GP/CP 
Base); once a decision is well handled by a simple rule, 
the gain is mainly in robustness, not cost (GP/Best RH). 
(6) When utilisation changes, the scheduling rules can 
be kept and only the processing-energy factors adjusted; 
tuning them for the highest expected level is the safer 
choice.
8. Conclusion
Scheduling energy- and labour-aware batch processes 
under uncertainty is difficult because several coupled 
INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF PRODUCTION RESEARCH 
 23
decisions interact. W e addressed this for a European 
lead-acid battery manufacturer, where lead plate heat-
treatment must balance scrap, energy, and labour under 
stochastic process completion, strict waiting-time con-
straints, and programme-compatibility requirements. 
The core of our work is methodological and problem-
centric: we decomposed this complex problem into a 
small set of well-defined sub-decisions – each addressed 
by a generic, energy- and labour-aware approach. On this 
basis, the study made four contributions.
First, we formalised a real-world stochastic schedul-
ing problem that jointly combines energy, labour, uncer-
tainty, batch processing, and strict waiting-time con-
straints – a combination not previously addressed 
together. Second, we decomposed it into a two-level opti-
misation with five sub-decisions: three at the system 
level (Pallet Evaluation, Chamber Dispatching, Chamber 
Early-Start) and two at the chamber level (Main Process 
Steps, Rework Process Steps) that set planned processing 
times for two independent stochastic stopping problems. 
This partitions the problem along its decision structure 
rather than its machine structure. Third, we designed 
a generic scheduling framework spanning from simple 
Rule-Based Heuristics (RH) to automatically synthesised 
rules. Genetic Programming (GP) served as a benchmark 
for a single sub-decision, testing whether its evolved rules 
could outperform the handcrafted ones. Fourth, we con-
ducted a comprehensive empirical evaluation grounded 
in the real-world case, using an agent-based discrete-
event simulation to identify the best-performing con-
figurations and benchmark them against the company’ s 
Current Practice.
Results showed that optimising all five sub-decisions 
with handcrafted rules (Best RH) significantly reduced 
costs by 40.8% at low and 85.4% at high utilisa-
tion (α = 0.05) over Current Practice, with substan-
tially improved solution robustness. Substituting a sin-
gle sub-decision with GP-evolved rules revealed both 
what evolved rules achieve alone and what potential 
handcrafted rules leave. Compared to Current Practice, 
it significantly reduced costs by 41.9% at high utilisa-
tion, whereas on the already optimised Best RH the cost 
gain was marginal and non-significant – but robustness 
still improved markedly, a critical advantage in stochas-
tic environments. The best configuration further proved 
robust across utilisation scenarios, requiring only lower 
processing-energy factors as capacity tightened. The 
analysis of the evolved rules showed that GP consistently 
prioritised slack while ignoring programme information, 
independently confirming that each sub-decision han-
dles only what is relevant to it. For production managers, 
these findings translate into concrete operating rules and 
highlight which scheduling decisions are make-or-break. 
Beyond this case, the decomposition and framework are 
expected to generalise to other energy-intensive batch-
processing systems with stochastic process completion, 
waiting-time constraints, compatibility restrictions, and 
batch-composition effects.
Future research could pursue several directions. First, 
comparing decomposed versus integrated GP approaches 
would quantify the decomposition’ s contribution. Sec-
ond, extending GP to other scheduling decisions, apply-
ing simulation-based optimisation with rolling-horizon 
re-evaluation, and exploring multi-objective formula-
tions could reveal additional improvement potential. 
Finally, validating the framework on further batch-
processing cases would confirm its transferability.
