Abstract
District heating energy hubs require reliable heat load forecasts for efficient operational
scheduling. Forecasting models trained on historical data may require retraining as networks
evolve. Zero-shot time-series foundation models and in-context forecasting therefore offer a
promising alternative: they can adapt at inference time from recent observations rather than
by repeated retraining. This study systematically evaluates TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2 for
probabilistic heat load forecasting in two German district heating networks and compares
them with trained baselines. Of particular interest is whether TabPFN-TS, whose under-
lying model is pretrained entirely on synthetic tabular rather than time-series data, can
capture complex district heating dynamics. We analyze covariate choice, context length,
temporal resolution, and forecast horizon on selected operating weeks, evaluate the selected
configuration over the full year, and assess cross-network transfer. The principal benchmark
assumes perfect weather forecasts; a separate sensitivity analysis uses retrospective weather
predictions. Hourly 24-hour forecasting with a 12-week rolling context and ambient tempera-
ture provides a parsimonious configuration; longer context windows do not improve accuracy.
BothTSFMsoutperformalltrainedbaselinesindeterministicaccuracyinthefull-yearbench-
marks. Chronos-2 achieves the best deterministic scores, with TabPFN-TS remaining close:
their CVRMSE values on the main data set are 12.48% and 13.07%, respectively. Chronos-2
also achieves lower continuous ranked probability scores in both networks, with TabPFN-TS
remaining close. Finally, a TSFM-based Multi-Resolution Residual-Correction Forecaster
combines an hourly base forecast with short-term high-resolution corrections. Relative to di-
rect high-resolution forecasting, it generally reduces errors in total heat demand over 12-hour
periods and recorded prediction times.

1 Introduction
To meet the climate targets of the European Union1, the heat supply of buildings must be de-
carbonized. District heating networks (DHN) provide one promising pathway toward this goal2
because they enable the integration of waste heat and renewable heat sources3. Realizing this
potential requires coordinating heat sources and storage under fluctuating availability, making
accurate heat-demand forecasts particularly important for cost- and emission-optimal opera-
tion 4. Heat demand in DHNs is shaped by multiple superimposed drivers5, including weather-
dependent space-heating demand6, comparatively stochastic domestic hot water consumption7,
and additional operational, daily, and seasonal variations8.

DHNs evolve as buildings are connected or disconnected, renovated, equipped with addi-
tional technologies, or subject to changes in occupancy and usage. These changes may not be
fully known to the operator and can alter the underlying heat-demand dynamics.9 Data-driven
forecasting methods can leverage operational observations, but conventional supervised machine-
learning models typically require explicit training and retraining in response to such changes.10
We hypothesize that in-context learning (ICL), which conditions predictions on supplied obser-
vations without updating model weights, can support adaptation to concept drift as a sliding
context window continually incorporates recent observations. Whether such models can rapidly
distinguish relevant post-change observations from older context remains an open question.
Recent advances in foundation models, models pretrained for reuse across different down-
stream tasks, are highly promising in this regard. TabPFN 11 and TabICL12 achieve strong
performance on tabular prediction benchmarks through ICL without task-specific training. The
time-series extension TabPFN-TS13 applies time-series featurization to TabPFN and is com-
petitive with leading forecasting models14. Other foundation models, such as Chronos-215, are
pretrained directly for time-series forecasting. Both approaches enable zero-shot forecasting
from historical observations without task-specific weight training. However, it remains unclear
whether TabPFN-TS’s synthetic tabular prior adequately captures the complex heat-demand dy-
namics of DHNs, or whether time-series-specific pretraining provides an advantage. We therefore
systematically evaluate these two contrasting time-series foundation model (TSFM) approaches
to probabilistic heat load forecasting. We compare their accuracy, predictive uncertainty and
operational requirements, using classical statistical, machine-learning and deep-learning models
as trained benchmarks.
The contributions of this work are as follows:
•We provide a systematic evaluation of two TSFMs, TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2, for prob-
abilistic heat load forecasting in DHNs.
•We investigate how covariate choice and context length affect TSFM forecasts to identify
configurations that achieve strong performance with limited input-data requirements.
•Wecomparehourlyand15-minuteresolutionstoassesswhetherTSFMscancapturehigher-
frequency heat load dynamics that are relevant for district heating operation.
•We evaluate forecast horizons from 4 hours to one week to characterize horizon-dependent
TSFM performance and its interaction with suitable context length.
•We compare both foundation models with trained forecasting baselines and evaluate cross-
network transfer.
•We derive and evaluate a Multi-Resolution Residual-Correction (MRRC) Forecaster based
on the diagnostic findings.
2 Related Work
This section reviews established forecasting approaches for DHNs, the foundation-model concepts
underlying TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2, recent energy-forecasting benchmarks, and residual-
correction architectures relevant to the present study.
2.1 Heat Load Forecasting in District Heating Networks
Heat load forecasting methods range from physically informed models to statistical, machine-
learning, and deep-learning approaches.16 Physically motivated and gray-box methods incor-
porate knowledge of building and district-heating dynamics17;18, while engineered load-shape
2
indicators provide a more empirical representation of operational demand patterns19. Classical
data-driven approaches formulate forecasting as supervised regression, including linear regression
and autoregressive models with exogenous inputs20;21.
Machine-learning methods extend these formulations through nonlinear relationships. Com-
mon examples include support vector regression22, decision-tree models23, and gradient-boosting
methods such as XGBoost24. Neural approaches include feed-forward networks 25, nonlinear
autoregressive models with exogenous inputs26, convolutional-recurrent architectures27, recur-
rent neural networks 28, attention-based long short-term memory (LSTM) networks 29, and
Transformer-based models such as Informer30. Rather than replacing earlier methods, these
model families provide increasingly flexible alternatives for representing heat-demand dynamics.
Most DHN load-forecasting models are fitted to a specific network and may require retraining
as system dynamics change.31 Adapting to concept drift remains challenging32, as changes must
be detected, suitable adaptation windows selected, and recurring concepts accounted for33. Such
recurrence is particularly relevant in district-heating systems, where seasonal shifts in the relative
dominance of space-heating and domestic-hot-water demand create recurring regimes5. These
limitations motivate forecasting methods that can adapt through context rather than repeated
parameter fitting. The following subsection introduces in-context learning for tabular prediction
and its extension to time-series forecasting.
2.2 Tabular Prediction with TabPFN and Its Extension to Time-Series Data
TabPFN11 is a foundation model for tabular prediction based on in-context learning. It is pre-
trained on a large collection of fully synthetic supervised-learning tasks and, at inference time,
predicts query labels from labeled context examples. Unlike conventionally trained neural net-

TabPFN-TS, as proposed by Hoo et al.13, can be directly used for time series forecasting by
reformulating a time series as a tabular regression problem. Each time step becomes a row in a
3
table. The feature set includes temporal features such as a running time index, cyclic calendar
features, anon-cyclicyearfeature, automaticallyextractedseasonalfeatures, andoptionallytime-
varying covariates whose future values are known at prediction time. The historical observations
form the labeled contextDc, while the future time points form query rows with known features
x∗ and unknown targetsy∗. TabPFN then outputs a probabilistic forecast for the full prediction
horizon in a non-autoregressive forward pass. This is notable because the same study reports
strong GIFT-Eval36 performance for TabPFN-TS, although the underlying TabPFN model was
pretrained only on synthetic non-temporal tabular data. The synthetic pretraining paradigm
is important for the present application: since TabPFN is pretrained on synthetic supervised
prediction tasks rather than on collections of real district-heating time series, the risk of direct
or indirect pretraining-test overlap with historical district-heating backtesting data is eliminated.
In addition, the probabilistic forecast can be interpreted as an approximation to the posterior
predictive distribution under the learned synthetic prior. This makes TabPFN-TS particularly
interesting for heat load forecasting, where reliable uncertainty estimates are operationally rele-
vant and where point forecasts alone do not fully describe the stochastic demand process. Hoo
et al.13 report that TabPFN-TS can distinguish signal from noise, adapt its probabilistic predic-
tions accordingly, incorporate covariates, and handle seasonal patterns, while trend recognition,
especially for linear and exponential trends, remains a reported weakness.
2.3 Time-Series Forecasting with Chronos-2
Chronos-2, proposed by Ansari et al.15, is a time-series foundation model capable of probabilistic
predictions. The model uses normalization of the covariates and targets. Additionally, it applies
patching where each patch represents several consecutive observations. Its transformer architec-
ture combines temporal attention and group attention, which allows relationships across time and
across targets and covariates to be captured. The model supports both past-only covariates and
covariates with known future values. Chronos-2 is pretrained on time-series forecasting tasks
using real and synthetic data. It differs from TabPFN-TS in its architecture and pretraining
assumptions.
Since Chronos-2 was partially pretrained on real-world time-series data, potential train-test
contamination between the pretraining corpus and the downstream evaluation data must be
considered. Based on the disclosed Chronos-2 pretraining corpus, leakage of time-series training
data into the 2024 heat load forecasting data sets for the DHNs in Munich or Flensburg appears
unlikely. The real pretraining data sets listed in Appendix A of the Chronos-2 documentation are
univariate time series from broad domains such as electricity, solar and wind generation, weather,
transport, web, cloud operations, and simulated U.S. building energy, but no district-heating
heat load data from Munich or Flensburg are reported15. Moreover, Chronos-2’s multivariate
and covariate-informed training tasks are stated to rely entirely on synthetic data.
2.4 Foundation-Model Benchmarks in Energy Forecasting
Recent work indicates that time-series foundation models can be useful in energy-forecasting
applications. Meyer et al.37 show that foundation models can provide performance comparable
to approaches trained from scratch for household electricity-demand forecasts. Koch et al.38
study thermal-energy forecasting and compare multisource transfer-learning strategies with time-
series foundation models, including TimesFM, Toto, and Chronos-2. Chronos-2 achieves the
strongest performance among the considered time-series foundation models. They further find
that multisource transfer learning requires at least 16 building data sets before it outperforms
time-series foundation models.
Comparative benchmarks including both TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2 in energy forecasting
provide the closest context for this study: Hertel et al.39 compare TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2
for load forecasting in electricity grids at transmission-system-operator level and use Shapley-
4
based explanations to increase interpretability. In their study, Chronos-2 outperforms TabPFN-
TS in both the univariate and covariate settings. Obermeier et al.14 benchmark several time-
series foundation models on energy-forecasting tasks, including Chronos-2 and TabPFN-TS in a
covariatesetting. ThebenchmarkincludesadatasetfromFlensburg’sDHNaspartofaheatload
prediction subset. For this heat load subset and overall, covariate models outperform univariate
models. Chronos-2 again outperforms TabPFN-TS overall and on the heat load subset, although
TabPFN-TS obtains some wins over Chronos-2 in the covariate setting on other subcategories
and several wins overall. These findings motivate evaluating both approaches under the same
district-heating forecasting conditions.
TabPFN has also been successfully applied to energy-related regression tasks. Liu et al.40
evaluate TabPFN for regression tasks in solar-energy meteorology and report that TabPFN out-
performs multiple regression, artificial neural networks, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting.
However, they do not directly address TabPFN-TS as a time-series forecasting method.
Alkhulaifi et al.41 show the importance of feature engineering for time-series forecasting with
TabPFN. TabPFN-TS largely automates this step by embedding generic temporal and seasonal
feature construction into the forecasting pipeline.
Ramachandran et al.42 compare several time-series foundation models and classical machine-
learning baselines with a method that combines a continuous wavelet transform with LSTMs
for heat-demand forecasting. Their proposed method outperforms the benchmark approaches.
However, it is specifically engineered for the considered forecasting problem and is therefore less
directly transferable than general-purpose time-series foundation model approaches.
Overall, the literature motivates a systematic comparison of TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2 for
zero-shot probabilistic heat load forecasting. Open questions concern their application-specific
configurations, deterministic and probabilistic performance, computational requirements, com-
parison with trained baselines, and transfer across DHNs with different characteristics. Their
different pretraining approaches additionally allow us to examine how competitive a synthetic
tabular prior remains against a model pretrained directly for time-series forecasting.
2.5 Residual-Correction Architectures for Time-Series Foundation Models
Residual correction of TSFM forecasts and the use of TSFMs as residual forecasters are emerg-
ing directions in time-series forecasting. Chitsaz and Haratizadeh 43 investigate TSFM-based
residual correction for several regression and machine-learning models in stock-price forecasting.
They compare this approach with a relational architecture in which a TSFM separately forecasts
the stock price and auxiliary financial variables, and the concatenated forecasts are passed to
a multivariate predictor. Residual correction reduces mean squared error (MSE) relative to the
standalone multivariate baseline for all five model classes and outperforms the relational archi-
tecture in four of five comparisons. For price-direction classification, the relational architecture
achieves a higher macro-averaged F1-score in three comparisons, residual correction in one, with
one tie.
In contrast, Dai et al.44 use a frozen TSFM to generate the base forecast. Their Online
Residual Contextual Adaptation (ORCA) framework estimates an additive residual correction
using a linear adapter that decomposes the concatenated historical input and base forecast into
trend and seasonal components. The base and corrected forecasts are then combined using
a Boltzmann softmax weighting mechanism based on recent prediction errors. Across eight
data sets covering energy, finance, meteorology, and traffic, the authors report an average MSE
reduction on every data set, with results aggregated over five TSFMs and three forecasting
horizons.
Gao et al.45 proposeSMR 2, a TSFM wrapper that constructs forecasts using the same frozen
TSFM with context data at different resolutions. A separate ridge-regression model predicts a
residual correction for each resolution-specific forecast. The corrected forecasts are then com-
bined using weights determined from validation performance. The authors report consistent
5
improvements across several TSFMs for regional drought forecasting.
Related residual-correction schemes have also been investigated for Transformer-based fore-
casting models. Biswas et al. 46 train a base model on the original time series and a second
model to forecast its residuals. The predicted residuals are scaled before being used to correct
the base forecast. Both models use the same look-back length and prediction horizon. Their main
iTransformer-based experiments show improved accuracy over the uncorrected model across all
eight data sets from energy, transportation, meteorology, and finance.
These studies motivate adapting residual-correction architectures to the distinct heat-load
dynamics and operational requirements of DHNs. In particular, they leave open how to combine
longer-horizon planning forecasts with frequently updated, high-resolution residual corrections.
Within the reviewed literature, using a TSFM to forecast the residuals of a TSFM prediction re-
mains underexplored. The present study investigates this combination as an application-specific
adaptation of residual correction.
3 Methodology
This section describes the forecasting models and rolling prediction setup, followed by the pro-
cedures for evaluating deterministic accuracy, probabilistic forecast quality and computational
cost. It then defines the anomaly-screening criteria and the metric used to quantify interannual
distributional change, before introducing the MRRC forecasting architecture.
3.1 Forecasting Models and Prediction Setup
The forecasting workflow uses rolling historical context windows comprising past heat load and
observed weather variables. Known future covariates comprise weather variables over the pre-
diction horizon and calendar features. Unless stated otherwise, ambient temperature is the only
weather variable. The prediction target is the future heat load. Both foundation models are
evaluated without task-specific weight training and produce quantile forecasts. The median is
used as the deterministic prediction, while the stored quantiles support probabilistic evaluation.
TabPFN-TSandChronos-2areevaluatedonthesameoperationalforecastingtasktocompare
their different pretraining paradigms and inference architectures. Both models receive the same
future covariate values over the prediction horizon and, where permitted by model constraints,
the same rolling context windows. The maximum number of context points that can be passed
to Chronos-2 is 8192 time steps. If a selected context window exceeds this limit, only the most
recent 8192 time steps are passed as context to Chronos-2, and results are reported at the effective
context length.
AutoGluon TimeSeries10;47 is used as a benchmark suite for the selected hourly day-ahead
forecasting task. The benchmark contains several model families. Persistence baselines are rep-
resented by Naive and Seasonal naive. Naive repeats the last observed heat load value over the
prediction horizon. Seasonal naive uses a seasonal period of 24 hours and therefore predicts
each future hour from the corresponding observed value on the previous day. Classical statisti-
cal baselines include exponential smoothing (ETS), automatic ETS (AutoETS), and automatic
autoregressive integrated moving average (AutoARIMA). Tabular machine-learning baselines in-
clude direct LightGBM (LGBM) and XGBoost models as well as a recursive LGBM model.
Neural baselines include DeepAR, TemporalFusionTransformer (TFT), Feed-forward, and DLin-
ear. AutoGluon’s weighted ensemble is reported as AutoGluon Ensemble.
The predictors are configured with ambient temperature as a known covariate, so future
ambient-temperature values are supplied over each prediction horizon. Models with native
known-covariate support can use this information directly, including the direct and recursive
tabular models, DeepAR, and TemporalFusionTransformer. Other models in the benchmark,
such as the persistence, statistical, Feed-forward, and DLinear baselines, do not use the future
6
covariate in the same way.
These AutoGluon benchmarks fitted once are not constrained to the same rolling context used
for the selected foundation-model setup. Instead, each prediction call receives the observed target
history from the start of 2023 up to the respective forecast start. Individual AutoGluon model
classes then use this history according to their own defaults: statistical models may truncate long
series internally, neural models use model-specific context lengths, and tabular models construct
lag, time, and covariate features.
In the main experiments, future ambient temperature is provided as the realized temperature
over the prediction horizon. This setting can be interpreted as a perfect-weather-forecast assump-
tion: the heat load forecasting problem is isolated from errors in numerical weather prediction,
and model behavior is evaluated conditional on known future weather.
All experiments were run on the RWTH Aachen University HPC cluster. Each job used
one NVIDIA H100 GPU with 95,830 MiB memory, 8 CPU cores, and 64 GB RAM. Nodes are
equipped with Intel Xeon Platinum 8468 CPUs, with two sockets and 48 physical cores per
socket. The software environment used Python 3.12.3 and CUDA 12.6.3, with tabpfn-time-series
1.1.0, tabpfn 8.0.3, chronos-forecasting 2.2.2, and autogluon.timeseries 1.5.0.

3.3 Anomaly Screening and Exclusion
Potentially anomalous heat-load observations are identified using a model-independent screening
proxy: nonpositive heat load or at least three consecutive exactly identical values at the data
set’s native resolution. These patterns can indicate maintenance, faults or signal outages, but
do not establish their cause. As independently verified anomaly labels are unavailable, flagged
observations are treated as suspected anomalies.
Weeks containing flagged observations are excluded from configuration selection to prevent
brief anomalous periods from driving the configuration choices. The principal full-year bench-
mark retains the recorded heat-load series without anomaly-specific modification. This evaluates
the selected configuration under continuous operational conditions and follows a data-driven set-
ting in which no manual preprocessing of the target signal is applied for the final assessment.

3.5 Multi-Resolution Residual-Correction Forecaster
Building on the residual-correction approaches reviewed in Section 2.5 and our configuration
findings, we develop the MRRC Forecaster as a follow-up extension of the evaluated forecasting
approaches. It combines a long-horizon base forecast with a short-horizon residual correction at
higher temporal resolution. The architecture is illustrated in Figure 1.
The first component, referred to as the Base Forecaster, provides the general daily load
trajectory potentially needed for scheduling and storage planning. Its point forecast is linearly
interpolated to the higher temporal resolution of 15 minutes. The second component, referred to
astheResidualForecaster, forecaststheremaininghigh-frequencyerrorofthisbaseline. Thefinal
point prediction is obtained by adding the predicted residual to the interpolated base forecast.
The architecture aims to improve the representation of short-term dynamics while retaining a
longer planning horizon. The MRRC Forecaster is evaluated here as a point-forecast architecture.
From a boosting perspective, the setup can be interpreted as a single residual-correction
step. An initial modelF(x)produces the base prediction, while a second modelh(x)predicts
the residualsr=y−F(x). The final prediction is then given byˆy=F(x) +h(x). In the MRRC
Forecaster, the Base Forecaster corresponds toF(x), and the Residual Forecaster corresponds
toh(x).
Historical residual context is constructed by subtracting the interpolated base forecasts avail-
able at each historical timestamp from the observed high-resolution heat loads. The Residual
Forecaster uses these residuals as its target context, with ambient temperature and the interpo-
lated base prediction as covariates. Future inputs contain the corresponding temperature and
base predictions.

4 Case Studies and Experimental Setup
This section introduces the Munich and Flensburg DHNs and their heat-load and weather data.
Munich is a small, expanding mixed-use network, whereas Flensburg is substantially larger and
comparatively stable. Munich’s operational data are proprietary; the openly available Flensburg
datasupportreproducibleexternalvalidation. Thesectionthenpresentstheexperimentaldesign,
supplementary analyses and MRRC configuration and evaluation.
4.1 District Heating Networks
The data used for model development and primary evaluation originate from a DHN serving a
mixed-use urban district in Munich, Germany. At the end of the available measurement period,
the network served 11 individual buildings, including large consumers such as hotels and office
buildings. The building stock also includes retail, restaurants, cultural and educational facilities,
and industrial uses.
Heat is supplied by a centralized energy hub, where the network heat load is measured. The
network’s supply line is approximately 400 m long. The design supply temperature is 80◦C,
while the measured supply temperature averaged 84.44◦C with a standard deviation of 3.95◦C
in 2024. The generation portfolio includes two combined heat and power units with a thermal
capacity of 1111 kW each, a 1870 kW gas boiler, a 1284 kW high-temperature heat pump,
and a 250 kW power-to-heat unit, supported by two 12m3 thermal buffer tanks. The combined
nominal heat-generation capacity is 5.626 MW. The system is operated using a rule-based control
strategy that prioritizes combined heat and power generation, while the heat pump, gas boiler,
and power-to-heat unit provide supplementary heat depending on operating conditions.
The available Munich heat-load record spans 1 January 2020 to 31 January 2025 at a native
temporal resolution of 15 minutes. Measured weather covariates are obtained from German
Weather Service (DWD) stations and aligned with the 15-minute heat load data. Temperature,
relative humidity, wind speed, and precipitation are taken from station 03379 München-Stadt.48
As this station has no solar irradiation sensor, irradiation data are obtained from station 05404
Weihenstephan-Dürnast. In 2024, these data were 99.47% complete; remaining gaps were filled
using surrounding DWD stations. In the primary benchmark, these weather variables are treated
as known future inputs.
The measured heat-load signal contains occasional periods flagged by the screening procedure
10
described in Section 3.3. After excluding flagged days, the maximum 15-minute load over the
entire available data set was 4.929 MW, and the maximum hourly mean was 3.496 MW.
The Munich network combines consumers with diverse heat-demand characteristics, while
its small number of connected buildings limits the smoothing of individual demand fluctuations
through aggregation. Together, these features make it a challenging case for district heat-load
forecasting. Ongoing expansion adds a further source of change and is accompanied by a substan-
tial increase in annual heat demand, as shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Table 1 summarizes
interannual differences and changes detected within individual years: the Hellinger distance
compares each year’s heat-load distribution with the preceding year, while the annual number
of Kolmogorov–Smirnov Windowing (KSWIN) drift detections indicates how frequently distri-
butional changes were signaled within that year.

To characterize the dominant time scales in the measured heat demand, a Fourier spectrum
of the full available 15-minute heat load series is shown in Figure 2. The very-long-period
components above one year should not be interpreted as stationary cycles; they reflect the
expansion of the Munich DHN and the associated increase in annual heat demand. Further
details of this annual development are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. The spectrum also
shows a dominant seasonal component close to one year, mainly attributable to space-heating
demand, and a clear diurnal component around 24 h. Additional peaks at harmonics of the
daily cycle, including 12 h, 8 h, 6 h, and 4 h, indicate that the diurnal load pattern contains
substantial harmonic content rather than being well described by a single sinusoidal component.
The pronounced 12 h component is consistent with two dominant demand periods per day, for
example, morning and evening domestic-hot-water peaks. In contrast, no pronounced weekly
peak is visible, suggesting that the aggregated DHN signal contains no strong strictly periodic
seven-day component.
The data used in the transfer validation originate from the DHN of Flensburg, Germany.
The network has developed historically from 1969 into a city-scale district heating system with
more than 700 km of district heating pipelines and a connection rate of over 90% among private
households. This is the same network analyzed by Obermeier et al.14, discussed in Section 2.4.
Heat is supplied from a central heating plant connected to the DHN. As of 2024, the heat
supply infrastructure comprises six generation units and two large thermal storage tanks.49 The
measured heat load signal reflects the aggregated demand supplied by this city-scale network.

4.2 Configuration Experiments and Benchmark Evaluation
The experimental design comprises configuration diagnostics on selected representative weeks,
full-year benchmarks and external validation, and a multi-year evaluation of MRRC. The config-
uration diagnostics examine context length, temporal resolution, forecast horizon and weather-
covariate choice for both foundation models, with additional model-specific diagnostics where
appropriate. Both models are then evaluated over 2024 against trained AutoGluon baselines and
on the Flensburg data set to assess cross-network transfer. Additional analyses assess sensitivity
to anomaly exclusion, baseline retraining following detected concept drift and weather-forecast
sensitivity, as described in Sections 4.3–4.5. The MRRC configuration and evaluation setup are
presented in Section 4.6.
Although the native temporal resolution of the heat load data is 15 minutes, large thermal
buffer tanks partially smooth the network demand. Consequently, this high temporal resolution
is not strictly required for heat-generation scheduling. Selected experiments therefore also use
hourly aggregated data to assess forecasting performance at a coarser, operationally relevant
resolution.
Candidate weeks are restricted to complete Monday–Sunday weeks without observations
flagged by the screening procedure in Section 3.3. From the remaining weeks, three operating
regimes are selected based on ambient temperature: the coldest week represents winter opera-
tion, the hottest week represents summer operation, and the week with the largest within-week
temperature variability represents transitional operation. The selected winter, transitional, and
summer weeks in 2024 are January 15–21, April 22–28, and August 12–18, respectively. Their
detailed ambient-temperature statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.
These representative weeks are used to study seasonal effects and to compare forecast hori-
zons, context lengths, and temporal resolutions under characteristic operating conditions.
For the principal 2024 benchmarks, the AutoGluon predictors are trained on 2023 target
values and evaluated on rolling 24-hour forecasts in 2024. For a transfer validation on a new
data set, the AutoGluon models are trained from scratch on the corresponding 2023 data before
being evaluated on 2024 data.
Forthefull-yearday-aheadbenchmarks, forecastsareissuedevery24elapsedhours, beginning
at 00:00 Europe/Berlin on 1 January. This corresponds to 00:00 local time during standard
time and 01:00 during daylight-saving time, with each forecast covering 24 elapsed hours. In the
selected-week diagnostics, daily forecasts are instead anchored at local midnight at the beginning
of each selected week.
4.3 Sensitivity to Anomaly Exclusion
To assess whether potentially anomalous targets affect the full-year benchmark conclusions, we
repeat the evaluation after anomaly exclusion. We exclude complete 24-hour forecasts issued on
local dates containing observations flagged by the screening procedure in Section 3.3.
4.4 Baseline Retraining on Detected Concept Drift
To test whether batch retraining triggered by active drift detection improves baseline perfor-
mance, we use KSWIN, which achieved the highest detection accuracy in the benchmark study
of Derzsi et al.9 We replay the Munich drift-detection timestamps supplied by the authors, gener-
ated using the KSWIN parameters specified for the medium-severity expansion scenario, referred
to as EXP-M in Table B.1 of their study.9 AutoGluon models initially fitted on 2020 are eval-
uated over 2021–2024 either as static models without retraining or with batch retraining from
scratch after each KSWIN drift detection on the preceding year of observations, ending strictly
before the detection time. At each forecast issuance, the latest eligible model is used; fitting is
assumed complete before the next scheduled forecast. Each model receives observed history from
its training-window start to the forecast issue time.
13
4.5 Weather-Forecast Sensitivity
To assess sensitivity to weather-forecast uncertainty, retrospective hourly temperature predic-
tions for Munich were obtained from the Open-Meteo Single Runs API using ECMWF IFS
HRES. 51 Each 24-hour heat-load forecast uses one coherent temperature trajectory from the
preceding 12:00 UTC initialization. The archive contains IFS Cycle 49R1 hindcasts, which we
use to assess sensitivity to coherent weather forecasts.
Forecast temperatures are available from 15 March 2024. Requiring a complete 12-week
context and 24-hour horizon yields 208 matched daily forecast starts from 7 June to 31 December
2024. The realized-weather reference uses measured temperatures in both the context and future
horizon. The forecast-weather variants use the same heat-load histories, forecast starts and
targets, but incorporate measured and forecast temperatures as described below.
Measured and forecast temperatures are supplied as separate historical covariates; each his-
torical forecast value comes from the trajectory selected for its corresponding daily forecast
start. Chronos-2 receives only forecast temperature as a future covariate. Because the TabPFN-
TS implementation requires future values for every covariate, both future columns are filled with
the same forecast temperature. These configurations achieved the lowest point-estimate errors
among the tested forecast-weather configurations, as shown in Supplementary Table S2.
4.6 MRRC Configuration and Evaluation
We evaluate the MRRC architecture described in Section 3.5 on the Munich data over 2021–2024
using both TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2 as backbones. The Base Forecaster operates at hourly
resolution, and its predictions are linearly interpolated to the 15-minute grid used for residual
construction and evaluation. Its forecast horizon is 24 hours, with a new forecast issued every
12 hours. Thus, at every point in time, a forecast for at least the next 12 hours is available. The
Residual Forecaster predicts the next two hours at 15-minute resolution and issues a new forecast
every hour, ensuring that a high-resolution forecast for at least the next hour is available.
For the Base Forecaster, the context window is chosen based on the 24-hour forecast results
described in Section 5.1. The base and direct high-resolution forecasts use a common 12-week
context to avoid varying context length alongside resolution and update frequency.
Because residual prediction differs from direct heat-load forecasting, the context-window
results from the main configuration experiments are not directly transferable to the Residual
Forecaster. Residual contexts of 2, 4, 8, 24, 48, 168 and 336 hours are therefore compared
separately for each backbone on the held-out December 2020 selection period, preceding the
2021–2024 evaluation. Selection minimizes the combined MRRC forecast’s CVRMSE. A seven-
day residual context achieved the lowest CVRMSE among the tested context lengths for both
TSFM backbones and was selected for evaluation.
We compare the MRRC Forecaster with two baseline setups. The first is the Base Forecaster
linearly interpolated to 15-minute resolution without the residual correction. The second baseline
is a direct high-frequency, high-resolution predictor: it uses 15-minute resolution, a 13-hour
prediction horizon, and hourly re-issuing. This setup ensures that at least 12 hours of forecast are
available at every time step while matching the short-term resolution of the MRRC Forecaster.
In the following, this setup is referred to as the High Frequency & High Resolution (HFHR)
setup.
Two sets of forecast-quality metrics are reported to reflect the different operational require-
ments. The short-term metrics assess the latest available 15-minute operational trajectory. For
the hourly updated high-resolution and MRRC setups, this corresponds to the first hour after
each forecast update and therefore quantifies how well the most recent forecast tracks the realized
high-resolution load profile. In contrast, the long-term metrics assess the forecast information
available for planning decisions. This long-term evaluation uses the rolling 12-hour energy metric
described in Section 3.2. For the 12-hour energy evaluation, the residual correction is applied
14
during the first two hours; the remaining horizon uses the base forecast.
5 Results
This section presents configuration and seasonal analyses, followed by full-year benchmarks,
external validation and probabilistic forecast quality. It then reports sensitivity to anomaly ex-
clusion, KSWIN-triggered baseline retraining and weather-forecast sensitivity, before comparing
MRRC with Base and HFHR forecasts across 2021–2024.
5.1 Configuration Experiments
First, the required length of historical context data was investigated for the 24-hour forecast task.
Context windows of 1 day, 1 week, 4 weeks, 12 weeks, and 1 year were evaluated for both 15-
minute and hourly forecast resolutions. At hourly resolution, both models achieved their lowest
CVRMSE with a 12-week context. At 15-minute resolution, the best context differed: 12 weeks
for TabPFN-TS and 4 weeks for Chronos-2, as shown in Figure 3. TheR2 and MAE results
follow the same qualitative trend. For TabPFN-TS, the 12-week context achieved a CVRMSE of
11.42%, anR2 of 0.970, and an MAE of 102.6 kW at hourly resolution; at 15-minute resolution, it
achieved a CVRMSE of 15.71%, anR2 of 0.945, and an MAE of 138.7 kW. Extending the recent
context to one year did not further improve the TabPFN-TS point forecast at either resolution.
In this experiment Chronos-2 slightly outperforms TabPFN-TS on every metric for all resolutions
and context window lengths.
The probabilistic score follows the same main pattern as the deterministic metrics. For
hourly forecasts, both models achieved their lowest CRPS with a 12-week context. At 15-
minute resolution, the optimum was 12 weeks for TabPFN-TS and four weeks for Chronos-2.
For TabPFN-TS, the 12-week context achieved a CRPS of 70.7 kW at hourly resolution and
97.3 kW at 15-minute resolution. Thus, the CRPS results support the choice of a moderate
recent context window and do not indicate a probabilistic benefit from using the full one-year
context either.
The corresponding steady-state prediction-call times are shown in the lower panel of Figure 3.
For the 12-week context, the mean time per forecast was 1.136 s and 1.665 s for TabPFN-TS at
hourly and 15-minute resolution, respectively, compared to 0.022 s and 0.028 s for Chronos-2.
Table 2 reports model loading and estimated first-call overhead alongside steady-state inference,
showing shorter model-loading time for TabPFN-TS but lower first-call overhead and inference
time for Chronos-2.

5.3 Full-Year Benchmark and Transfer Validation
The results of the full-year simulations are reported in Table 6. The entries are sorted by
aggregate CVRMSE within each network. On the Munich data set, both foundation models out-
performed the trained AutoGluon baselines across the reported accuracy metrics, with Chronos-2
achieving the lowest aggregate error. To assess whether models perform consistently when trans-
ferred to a different data set, the rank-shift column reports the change in CVRMSE position in
Flensburg relative to the Munich ordering. The relative error metrics were generally lower for
Flensburg than for Munich, especially for the strongest models. Chronos-2, TabPFN-TS, and
TFT remained the three strongest models in both networks, whereas the naive baselines stayed
at the lower end of the ranking. The largest rank changes occurred for AutoETS and ETS, which
lost five CVRMSE positions in Flensburg, and for Feed-forward, Recursive LGBM, and Seasonal
naive, which gained three positions each. Chronos-2 again achieved the lowest aggregate error,
while TabPFN-TS remained ahead of the trained AutoGluon models.
The critical-difference diagrams are shown in Figure 7. For both data sets, TabPFN-TS lies
within the critical difference of Chronos-2. On the Munich data set, the two time-series foun-
dation models form the leading group and outperform all trained AutoGluon baselines in the
daily-rank comparison. In Flensburg, the leading group is broader: TFT, DeepAR, and Auto-
Gluon Ensemble also lie within the critical difference of the foundation-model results. The lower
panels of Figure 7 show the corresponding pairwise daily CVRMSE win rates. Supplementary
Table S8 reports initial AutoGluon fitting times.

5.5 Sensitivity to Anomaly Exclusion
We also investigated the influence of potentially anomalous target observations using the screen-
ing criteria described in Section 3.3. After excluding 24 Munich forecast days containing
time steps identified by these criteria, 342 evaluation days remained. The complete aggregate
CVRMSE ordering was unchanged, with Chronos-2, TabPFN-TS and TFT retaining the first
three positions. Chronos-2 and TabPFN-TS also remained the leading group in the critical-
difference comparison. CVRMSE and MAE decreased for 14 of the 15 models; the naive baseline
was the exception. For TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2, the standard deviation of daily MAE de-
creased from 51.8 to 48.8 kW and from 49.4 to 45.0 kW, respectively. Both foundation models
achieved lower CRPS after exclusion, with Chronos-2 retaining the lower score. No Flensburg
day met the screening criteria. Supplementary Table S4 reports the detailed deterministic results
in Panel A and paired changes after anomaly exclusion for all 15 models in Panel B. Full-year
and anomaly-filtered CRPS values are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

6 Discussion
Chronos-2 achieved stronger full-year deterministic performance and lower CRPS in both
networks, consistent with the benefits of its time-series-specific architecture and pretraining.
TabPFN-TS nevertheless remained within the critical-difference threshold of Chronos-2 in the
daily CVRMSE-rank comparison in both networks. These results show that a model pretrained
entirely on synthetic non-temporal tabular data can support competitive heat-load forecasting
when combined with time-series featurization. Together with the solar-energy results of Liu et
al. 40, this supports the applicability of synthetically pretrained TabPFN models across different
energy-related tasks. Task- and domain-tailored priors may offer further improvements.
The context-length diagnostics in Section 5.1 show that forecast accuracy does not improve
monotonically with context length: longer histories can yield poorer forecasts than shorter ones.
Optimal context length therefore remains application-specific even for foundation models. This
may reflect superimposed demand processes: space heating is strongly seasonal and weather-
driven, whereas domestic hot water is more stochastic and exhibits different, weaker temporal
patterns. Withoutexplicitseparation, longcontextsmayincluderegimesirrelevanttothecurrent
forecast and obscure rather than improve in-context prediction.
The results support the ability of both TSFMs to capture predictable components of higher-
frequency heat-load dynamics at 15-minute resolution. In the four-year comparison, the direct
HFHR setup achieved lower short-term CVRMSE than the interpolated hourly Base Forecaster
for both backbones in every year. Nevertheless, the configuration experiments showed lower fore-
casting errors at hourly resolution. This is likely due to temporal aggregation: hourly averaging
smooths high-frequency dynamics that are difficult to predict at 15-minute resolution. One po-
tential driver of these dynamics is domestic-hot-water draw-off behavior. The exact timing of
individual draw-off events contains a stochastic component and is therefore inherently difficult
to forecast52, whereas broader temporal patterns in domestic-hot-water demand are easier to
capture 53. Additional sources of high-frequency variability may include hydraulic interactions
between buildings54, varying transport delays between the energy hub and consumers, delay-
dependent heat losses, hysteresis-controlled pressure pumps, and other operational effects. The
short-horizon experiments also revealed a setting in which TabPFN-TS outperformed Chronos-
2 in deterministic accuracy: for the 4-hour forecast task at 15-minute resolution, TabPFN-TS
performed better than Chronos-2 when only very short context windows of 4 or 12 hours were
available, as shown in Figure 4. This is consistent with energy-demand forecasting results by
Kim et al.55, who report strong TabPFN performance under short-context conditions, and with
the broader finding that TabPFN can deliver accurate predictions under low data availability11.
Moving from daily day-ahead forecasts to weekly week-ahead forecasts reduced accuracy for
both models, highlighting the importance of matching forecast horizon, update frequency and
context length to the operational task.
The seasonal effects in Section 5.2 provide complementary evidence on how demand compo-
26
sition affects forecast accuracy and uncertainty. The lower winter daily CVRMSE in Figure 5 is
consistent with a larger share of weather-driven space-heating demand, creating a stronger and
more predictable baseload. In summer, the smaller space-heating share and greater relative con-
tribution of stochastic domestic hot water demand are expected to increase the relative forecast
error. The higherR 2 during the transitional period results from greater variation in measured
demand, allowing more variance to be explained despite the relative error not being lowest. The
greater accuracy loss from daily to weekly forecasts in this period may arise because the longer
horizon spans rapidly changing load and weather conditions, making multi-day extrapolation
harder than in the more stable winter and summer periods. The relative interval widths in
Table 5 similarly suggest that relative uncertainty rises as space-heating demand becomes less
dominant and stochastic components gain importance. The transitional-week underprediction
reported in Section 5.2, particularly for Chronos-2, may reflect difficulty adapting to changing
operating conditions. One possible explanation is that the 12-week context window contains
observations less representative of this regime.
The small, model-dependent improvements from additional weather covariates support am-
bient temperature as a parsimonious default for the evaluated forecasting task. This preference
for a compact input set is consistent with Petković et al.56, who favor compact weather-input
sets over large sets of meteorological covariates for heat load prediction. Because the additional
computational cost was small in the tested setting, including further reliably available weather
covariates remains defensible when sufficiently accurate future values are available.
The transfer results in Section 5.3 and Table 6 should be interpreted in light of network scale.
ThelowerrelativeerrorsintheFlensburgvalidationcasemaypartlyreflectaggregation: domestic
hot water demand is comparatively stochastic at the individual-building level but becomes more
predictable as more buildings are connected. Because Flensburg is a city-scale district-heating
system whereas Munich is a smaller mixed-use district, stronger aggregation in Flensburg may
smooth idiosyncratic consumption events and contribute to its lower relative forecasting errors.
Our literature review in Section 2.1 identified retraining under concept drift as a central
challenge in heat load forecasting. The four-year comparison in Section 5.6 shows that the
performance gap between static trained baselines and TSFMs widened over time, whereas the
gap to baselines undergoing KSWIN-triggered batch retraining remained comparatively stable.
This pattern supports our hypothesis that rolling-context in-context learning can accommodate
changing system dynamics without task-specific weight updates. The remaining advantage over
retrained baselines may reflect inherent model differences, the shorter 12-week context compared
with a full training year that may retain more pre-change observations, and the TSFMs’ ability
to identify relevant dynamics within that context. Our experiments do not isolate the relative
contributions of these factors.
As described in Section 5.1, adding calendar-aligned observations from the previous year did
not improve forecasts. However, the 2023 and 2024 data span the ongoing expansion of the
Munich district and associated demand growth. The added block may therefore represent a
different system configuration. Calendar alignment also only approximates physical similarity;
selecting context by ambient temperature or clustered operating conditions may be more mean-
ingful. However, even temperature-matched historical observations may be less representative
if the network configuration or operating conditions have changed. Seasonally relevant context
may thus be more beneficial in stationary district-heating systems.
Using coherent ECMWF temperature hindcasts increased CVRMSE by 7.4% for TabPFN-
TS and 10.1% for Chronos-2 relative to the perfect-weather reference, while CRPS increased
by 8.1% and 10.2%, respectively. These moderate relative increases suggest that both models
retain much of their predictive accuracy under imperfect weather inputs in the evaluated setting.
The extent of deterioration will nevertheless depend on the quality of the weather predictions
available in operation.
For both backbones, TSFM-based residual correction significantly improved short-term
27
CVRMSE, MAE and 12-hour E-CVRMSE relative to the uncorrected Base Forecaster over
2021–2024. This demonstrates the feasibility of using TSFMs as residual forecasters for TSFM
predictions in the evaluated multi-resolution setup. Compared with HFHR, MRRC improved
predictions of integrated heat demand over 12 hours while achieving broadly similar short-term
accuracy. One possible explanation is its separation of forecasting time scales: the hourly
Base Forecaster potentially captures slower variations associated with space heating, consistent
with the dominant low-frequency patterns identified in the spectral analysis, while the Residual
Forecaster addresses faster dynamics, including those associated with domestic hot water. Its
computational savings arise from issuing expensive long-context base forecasts less frequently
and using shorter-context residual forecasts for frequent updates. The MRRC architecture’s
benefit therefore depends on whether operational priorities favor short-term trajectory accuracy,
longer-horizon heat-demand planning, or lower computational cost.
6.1 Limitations
Although the two case studies cover DHNs with substantially different scales and building stocks,
both are located in Germany and are therefore subject to broadly similar seasonal weather
effects. Additionally, configuration selection and MRRC evaluation are restricted to Munich
only. Generalization beyond these case studies therefore remains to be established.
Configuration choices were evaluated on selected operating weeks rather than exhaustively
optimized for every task. Results can depend on the evaluation period: TabPFN-TS’s lower
week-ahead CVRMSE on the selected operating weeks did not persist in the full-year evaluation.
Findings from selected weeks therefore need not generalize to year-round performance.
The principal benchmark assumes perfect weather forecasts by using realized future tempera-
tures. The weather sensitivity analysis uses retrospective hindcasts rather than verified forecasts
delivered operationally in 2024. The sensitivity analysis of weather forecast uncertainty covers
only 24-hour heat-load predictions. Its findings therefore cannot be directly extended to longer
forecasting horizons, for which increasing weather forecast uncertainty may have a larger effect
on heat-load prediction.
KSWIN-triggered batch retraining uses the preceding year of observations and trains the
models from scratch. Consequently, observations preceding a detected change can remain in the
training window; no explicit change-based selection of training data is applied.
Finally, the HFHR setup used as a benchmark in the MRRC experiments was not indepen-
dently optimized for its 13-hour forecast horizon and increased update frequency.
7 Conclusion & Future Work
This work systematically evaluated TabPFN-TS and Chronos-2 for zero-shot probabilistic heat
load forecasting in DHNs. The results demonstrate the suitability of both evaluated founda-
tion models for this task, with Chronos-2 achieving the strongest overall forecast quality and
TabPFN-TS also proving competitive. For hourly 24-hour forecasting, both models achieved
their lowest error among the tested contexts with a 12-week window, whereas shorter contexts
performed better in some high-resolution settings. Ambient temperature alone provided an ef-
fective weather-covariate set.
The experiments show that rolling-context foundation-model forecasting can perform well in
an evolving DHN without repeated task-specific retraining. Both foundation models performed
strongly in the Flensburg validation case and outperformed the trained AutoGluon baselines
in the main full-year comparison. At the same time, the diagnostic experiments showed that
configuration choices remain important: more context data, higher temporal resolution, and
larger weather-covariate sets did not automatically improve performance.
The Multi-Resolution Residual-Correction Forecaster illustrates how residual correction can
28
be adapted to DHN operational requirements. Compared with direct high-frequency, high-
resolution forecasting, MRRC significantly reduced 12-hour E-CVRMSE for both backbones
and required less recorded prediction time, while short-term accuracy remained broadly similar.
Future work on MRRC should address both probabilistic forecasting and further configura-
tion and architecture development. A probabilistic extension requires representing uncertainty
consistently across the hourly base forecast and quarter-hourly residual correction, including be-
yond the two-hour residual horizon. Additionally, configuration and architecture studies could
vary the Residual Forecaster horizon, test multiple residual-correction stages, and compare com-
binations of TSFMs and classical machine-learning models as Base and Residual Forecasters.
The separation of slow load trends and high-frequency deviations could also be evaluated in
other energy-system forecasting tasks.
A second direction for future work is to adapt foundation models more closely to district-
heating heat load forecasting by preconditioning them on real-world time series, simulation data,
or synthetic data containing the relevant dynamics. Task-specific prior data or further weight
adaptation may improve forecast accuracy. However, if target-network data are included in the
prior or used for adaptation, robustness under concept drift must be evaluated separately. For
the trained baselines, future work could investigate whether excluding pre-change training data
or updating existing models through warm-starting or transfer learning improves adaptation and
reduces retraining costs.
Finally, future work should extend forecasts beyond heat demand to mass flow rates as
well as supply and return temperatures. The delivered heat rate is proportional to mass flow
rate and temperature difference, while the efficiency and usability of sustainable sources such
as heat pumps and waste-heat streams depend on the required temperature level. Spatially
distributed multivariate forecasts within the network could further support decentralized heat-
source integration by providing more detailed information for operational planning and control.

S3 Weather-Forecast Configurations and Sensitivity
This comparison examines how measured and forecast temperature histories affect forecasts and
quantifies the change relative to the perfect-weather reference. We compared three ways to
incorporate measured and forecast temperatures into each time-series foundation model. In
Table S2, measured and forecast temperatures are denoted by M and F, respectively. The first
configuration uses forecast temperatures in both the historical context and future horizon. The
second uses a single covariate column containing historical measurements and future forecasts.
The third uses separate measured- and forecast-temperature columns in the historical context.
Chronos-2 receives only forecast temperature over the future horizon. Because the TabPFN-
TS implementation requires future values for both columns, both contain the same forecast
temperature over the future horizon.
All configurations use the same Munich target observations, 12-week context, hourly resolu-
tion and 24-hour horizon, with 208 daily forecast starts from 7 June to 31 December 2024, com-
prising 4,992 hourly predictions. The forecast-weather configurations use coherent retrospective
ECMWF temperature predictions. The M→M configuration corresponds to our perfect-weather
forecast baseline, using measured temperatures throughout, including the future horizon. For
Table S2, each resampled day retains all 24 horizon observations.
