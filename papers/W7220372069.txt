ABSTRACT
As global electricity demand surges due to industrial electrification and digitalisation, peak power 
(PP) management has emerged as a critical challenge in energy-intensive manufacturing, affect-
ing operational costs and grid capacity. PP is defined as the maximum instantaneous total power 
demand across all workstations during the production cycle, and its minimisation is essential for 
reducing demand charges and avoiding grid overloads. This study introduces the balancing and 
scheduling of machining transfer lines as a bi-objective optimisation problem that simultaneously 
minimises cycle time (CT) and PP under precedence, inclusion/exclusion, and technological compat-
ibility constraints. A multi-objective constraint programming (CP) model is formulated and solved 
via a bidirectional ε-constraint method, generating high-quality Pareto sets for small-scale instances. 
To overcome the computational intractability of CP for medium- and large-scale problems, a multi-
objective variable neighbourhood search (MOVNS) enhanced with deep reinforcement learning 
(NN_MOVNS) is proposed, in which a Deep Q-Network adaptively guides neighbourhood operator 
selection throughout the search. Computational experiments on 55 benchmark instances demon-
strate that NN_MOVNS consistently outperforms competing algorithms across all problem scales, 
with improvements becoming increasingly pronounced on larger instances, as confirmed by statis-
tical significance tests. Peak shaving experiments confirm PP reductions ranging from 33% to 73% 
with modest CT increases averaging 5.7%. An industrial-scale test instance of a 148-task automotive 
cylinder head production line achieves an 11.8% CT reduction and a 63.1% PP reduction, lowering 
the peak-to-average power ratio from 2.35 to 1.49.

At the same time, escalating energy prices, power grid

the 21st century. This challenge is reflected in unprece-

than half of national final energy demand and a domi-
nant share of energy-related CO2 emissions Sandalow 
et al. 2022). Moreover, global energy consumption is pro-

tion Administration. 2021), with manufacturing’ s share 
of total electricity consumption expected to nearly double

By contrast, energy cost minimisation via time-of-use

Azizoğlu 2024; Gianessi, Delorme, and Masmoudi 2019), 
which is shown herein to be inadequate when both cycle

ate processing bottlenecks and increase CT . However, 
even if tasks are well balanced, simultaneous scheduling

ing aims to stagger these blocks to smooth the power 
profile; however, without proper task balancing, schedul-
ing alone may result in inefficient use of workstation 
capacity or increased CT . Consequently, the assignment

timing of block execution scheduling must be optimised

with a deep reinforcement learning-based operator selec-
tion mechanism, named NN_MOVNS, is proposed. T o 
the best of our knowledge, no existing approach simulta-
neously addresses CT and PP optimisation in machining 
transfer lines under the full set of precedence, inclu-
sion/exclusion, and technological constraints. This gap 
motivates the following research questions:
RQ1: How can the BSMTL be jointly formulated as 
a bi-objective optimisation problem to simultaneously 
minimise CT and PP under precedence, inclusion/exclu-
sion, and technological compatibility constraints?
RQ2: T o what extent can the proposed NN_MOVNS 
algorithm outperform both exact methods and standard 
metaheuristics in terms of Pareto front quality across 
small, medium, and large-scale instances?
RQ3: What are the practical implications of the 
proposed approach in terms of PP reduction, energy 
redistribution, and peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR) 
improvement in industrial-scale machining transfer 
lines?

2. Literature review
This section provides a structured review of the sci-
entific literature relevant to the problem of minimis-
ing PP and CT in machining transfer lines. Section 2.1 
establishes the foundational concepts of transfer line bal-
ancing. Section 2.2 examines PP considerations in gen-
eral line balancing, drawing transferable insights due 
to the scarcity of transfer line-specific energy studies. 
Section 2.3 identifies the research gap, and Section 2.4 
summarises the main scientific contributions of this 
work.
2.1. Transfer line balancing problem
The Transfer Line Balancing Problem (TLBP) is defined 
as the optimisation of assigning machining operations to 
workstations in an automated production line to min-
imise one or more objectives such as number of stations, 
line cost, or CT , while satisfying precedence constraints, 
technological limitations, and equipment compatibil-
ity requirement (Battaïa, Dolgui, and Guschinsky 2024; 
Lahrichi, Grangeon, et al. 2021). Transfer lines are 
characterised by their serial configuration of worksta-
tions connected by automated material handling sys-
tems, where parts move automatically from one station 
to the next, with each station equipped with specialised 
tools that perform machining operations using single or 
multiple spindle heads (Nourmohammadi et al. 2024). 
Unlike assembly line balancing, which often focuses on 
manual or semi-automated task allocation, TLBPs must 
address distinct features of automated machining sys-
tems, including high automation levels, the capacity for 
parallel processing via multi-spindle heads, equipment 
design decisions that directly influence task times, and 
complex constraints related to tool compatibility and 
task-machine accessibility (Beldar et al. 2025).
According to the comprehensive survey recently car-
ried out by Beldar et al. (2025), most studies on the 
TLBP have focussed on cost-related objectives, particu-
larly minimising line cost (Battaïa, Dolgui, and Guschin-
sky 2023, 2024) and the number of machines (Lahrichi, 
Deroussi, et al. 2021; Lahrichi, Grangeon, et al. 2021). 
While cost considerations dominate much of the research 
due to their direct impact on capital investment, there is a 
noticeable gap in addressing time-related objectives like 
CT minimisation and efficiency-related objectives like 
power or energy efficiency metrics.
Focussing on CT considerations, several key works 
provide valuable insights. Ersin T elemeci and Azizoğlu
(2024) address the Type II TLBP , formulating a mixed-
integer linear programming (MILP) model and a branch-
and-bound algorithm to minimise CT . Nourmoham-
madi et al. (2024) study the balancing and scheduling of 
transfer lines, introducing a MILP model and a multi-
objective optimisation approach to minimise CT and 
total energy cost. Their results highlight how varying 
energy modes influence sustainability, providing insights 
into energy-efficient production strategies for small to 
medium-scale problems. Beldar et al. (2024) propose a 
heuristic approach for balancing transfer lines, incorpo-
rating a wide range of equipment, time, and operational 
factors. A numerical example illustrates the method’ s 
practical applicability and its effectiveness in generating 
feasible solutions for real-world production settings.
Energy considerations, though less prevalent, are gain-
ing traction in TLBP research as sustainability becomes a 
pressing concern. Petersen et al. (2024) address TLBP in 
the context of energy-efficient manufacturing by jointly 
considering CT and energy consumption. A mixed-
integer linear formulation is presented, and a multi-
objective Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II 
(NSGA-II) is developed to handle the problem’ s com-
plexity. Their results demonstrate how different energy 
modes influence sustainability, offering manufactur-
ers actionable insights for energy-efficient production
strategies.
Despite growing interest in energy-related objectives, 
research on incorporating PP into TLBP remains scarce. 
T o better understand how such considerations can be 
incorporated, it is necessary to look beyond transfer lines 
and examine the broader body of line balancing research, 
where PP has recently emerged as a distinct objective 
alongside CT , cost, and energy metrics.

2.2. PP considerations in line balancing
PP in line balancing is defined as the maximum instanta-
neous electric power demand during the production pro-
cess. Unlike traditional assembly line balancing problems 
that focus primarily on balancing workload and minimis-
ing CT or number of stations (Battaïa and Dolgui 2022), 
PP considerations introduce additional scheduling and 
sequencing complexities, as overlapping high-energy 
tasks can cause peak demand that elevate operational 
expenses and risk outages (Delorme et al. 2023; Delorme 
and Gianessi 2024). PP minimisation in line balanc-
ing problems must address the temporal distribution of 
energy-intensive tasks, often requiring integrated assign-
ment and scheduling decisions to smooth power profiles 
while respecting precedence constraints, takt time, and 
workstation limits.
Focussing on PP considerations, several key works 
provide valuable insights. Lamy, Delorme, and Gianessi
(2020) examine a variant of the simple assembly line bal-
ancing problem with PP minimisation (SALB3PM), for-
mulating a model that integrates task sequencing without 
idle times to minimise PP . Their metaheuristic approach 
demonstrates that sequencing decisions can minimise 
peaks in paced lines. Delorme and Gianessi (2024) 
study PP minimisation in reconfigurable parallel-serial 
lines with crossover, introducing an integer program-
ming model and matheuristic that consider workstation 
resource allocation and task scheduling to minimise PP .
Building on these foundations, Gianessi, Delorme, 
and Masmoudi (2019) deal with SALB3PM, present-
ing an integer linear program that minimises PP 
through integrated assignment and scheduling. Fang 
et al. (2020) explore multi-objective optimisation in 
mixed-model multi-robotic disassembly lines, minimis-
ing CT , peak workstation and total energy consump-
tion under interval processing times. Their evolution-
ary simulated annealing algorithm yields uniformly dis-
tributed Pareto-optimal solutions, highlighting the trade-
offs between energy metrics in robotic systems. Simi-
larly, Z. Zheng, Cherif, and Shibasaki (2024) propose a 
maximum satisfiability-based model for the SALB3PM, 
efficiently handling large instances by encoding power 
constraints and achieving robust performance across 
benchmarks.
In disassembly contexts, Fang et al. (2019) tackle 
many-objective optimisation for mixed-model disassem-
bly lines with multi-robotic workstations, balancing CT , 
energy consumption, and robot usage. Their problem-
specific evolutionary algorithm provides insights into 
convergence-diversity trade-offs, with computational 
experiments showing superior performance over rep-
resentative methods. H. Ming, Liu, and Pham (2019) 
address uncertain processing times in multi-robotic dis-
assembly lines, developing an MILP model that min-
imises CT , PP , total energy consumption, and haz-
ardous task costs, validated through illustrative exam-
ples. Z. Zhang et al. (2025) consider multi-robot destruc-
tive disassembly line balancing under component non-
disassemblability, optimising CT , energy consumption, 
and profit with an improved multi-objective water cycle 
algorithm.
Further advancements in reconfigurable systems are 
evident in Delorme and Gianessi (2022), who min-
imise PP in parallel-serial lines with crossover, propos-
ing an integer linear program that integrates bal-
ancing and scheduling for an energy-efficient sys-
tem. Their approach demonstrates substantial peak 
reductions through resource reconfiguration. Delorme 
et al. (2023) introduce a decoder for permutation-based 
heuristics in SALB3PM with earliest starting dates, opti-
mally splitting task sequences to minimise peaks, with 
preliminary tests confirming efficiency in large instances. 
Taken together, these works confirm that PP-oriented 
line balancing and scheduling is an active and growing 
research area, but one that has not yet been extended to 
multi-spindle block-based machining transfer lines.
2.3. Research gap
Although prior research on PP minimisation in assem-
bly, reconfigurable, and disassembly lines offers valu-
able conceptual and methodological foundations, these 
studies focus on line structures and decision variables 
that differ significantly from those in machining transfer 
lines. Specifically, BSMTL combines characteristics that 
are not jointly addressed in the existing literature, includ-
ing parallel multi-spindle processing within blocks, syn-
chronised station operations through automated transfer 
mechanisms, and strict equipment- and compatibility-
related constraints. When considered under a single 
objective, the problem relates to two distinct research 
streams: if only CT is minimised, it connects to 
transfer line balancing studies in multi-spindle set-
tings (Ersin T elemeci and Azizoğlu 2024) and balancing-
and-scheduling formulations for mono-spindle flexible 
transfer lines (Nourmohammadi et al. 2024); if only 
PP is minimised, it relates in spirit to PP-oriented 
balancing-and-scheduling models for simple assem-
bly lines (Gianessi, Delorme, and Masmoudi 2019) 
and mono-spindle reconfigurable lines (Delorme and 
Gianessi 2022). However, none of these works simulta-
neously address multi-spindle block-based transfer lines, 
joint balancing and scheduling decisions, and PP as an

objective. In both mono-spindle and multi-spindle trans-
fer lines, all stations operate simultaneously, contribut-
ing to system-level PP . However, in mono-spindle lines, 
only one task is executed at a time within each sta-
tion, so power demands do not accumulate within a 
station. In contrast, in multi-spindle block-based lines 
all tasks within a block execute simultaneously, caus-
ing their power demands to sum instantaneously. Block 
composition therefore becomes itself a PP-determining 
decision, an additional source of complexity that does 
not arise in mono-spindle settings. Treating CT and PP 
independently is thus insufficient: CT-only approaches 
do not control how block composition generates instan-
taneous power peaks, while PP-only approaches may 
inflate CT by creating workload imbalances or scheduling 
bottlenecks. Since the task-to-block assignment deter-
mines which power demands accumulate simultane-
ously and the block execution sequence determines when 
those peaks occur, both decisions must be optimised 
jointly. This reveals a methodological gap in the litera-
ture and motivates the bi-objective models and solution 
approaches developed in this work.
2.4. Our main scientific contributions
In this work, we address the BSMTL problem, charac-
terised by the joint optimisation of PP and CT under 
precedence, inclusion/exclusion, and technological com-
patibility constraints. The contributions of this study are 
classified under three categories:
(i) Theoretical contributions: A multi-objective CP 
model is formulated and solved via a bidirectional ε-
constraint method (Bérubé, Gendreau, and Potvin 2009), 
generating high-quality Pareto sets for small-scale
instances. This formulation establishes a rigorous math-
ematical foundation for the joint CT–PP optimisation 
in machining transfer lines, a problem class that has 
received limited formal treatment in the literature.
(ii) Methodological contributions: A MOVNS
algorithm is proposed, featuring a two-phase construc-
tive heuristic for initialisation: in the first phase, tasks 
are assigned to workstations with integrated constraint 
handling to ensure feasibility, while in the second phase, 
blocks are created on each workstation to establish a 
high-quality starting solution. The MOVNS framework 
is further augmented with a neural network-based oper-
ator selection mechanism (NN_MOVNS) that employs 
a Deep Q-Network (DQN) to dynamically adapt search 
operators during optimisation, significantly improving 
exploration capabilities over static operator selection 
strategies.
(iii) Managerial contributions: The practical implica-
tions of the proposed approach are assessed using two 
metrics, the Peak Shaving Index (PSI) and the Energy 
Redistribution Index (ERI), commonly used in demand 
response contexts to assess and mitigate demand peaks. 
These metrics provide operations managers with action-
able tools to quantify and communicate the benefits of PP 
reduction in energy-intensive manufacturing systems.
3. Problem description
A machining transfer line composed of a finite set 
of workstations M = {1, . . . , NM} is considered, where 
each workstation k ∈ M contains a limited number of 
blocks. A set of tasks O = {1, . . . , NT} must be executed 
on the line. Each task i ∈ O is characterised by a deter-
ministic processing time T ai and a power demand Tpi. 
The tasks are also subject to technological and organi-
sational constraints. Precedence relations (prei,j) specify 
that task i must be assigned no later than task j in the 
line sequence. W orkstation eligibility restrictions (teci,k) 
indicate whether task i can be processed on workstation 
k. Compatibility rules further restrict how tasks can be 
grouped: inclusion constraints (inci,j) require two tasks 
to be assigned to the same workstation; workstation-
level exclusion constraints (exci,j) prevent two tasks from 
being assigned to the same workstation; and block-level 
exclusion constraints (exc_bi,j) prevent two tasks from 
being assigned to the same block.
The assignment of tasks is performed in two stages. 
First, tasks are grouped into blocks, where the total num-
ber of blocks across the line is |B| =Nb · NM, with Nb
blocks allocated to each workstation. The set of block 
indices belonging to workstation k is formally defined as:
R(k) = {(k − 1) · Nb + 1, (k − 1)
· Nb + 2, . . . , k · Nb}
This partitioning ensures that every block q ∈ B belongs 
to exactly one workstation, and that the Nb blocks 
on each workstation are indexed consecutively without 
overlap. For instance, with NM = 3 workstations and 
Nb = 2 blocks per workstation, the total block set is 
B = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, partitioned as R(1) = {1, 2}, R(2) =
{3, 4}, and R(3) = {5, 6}, so that workstation 1 operates 
blocks 1 and 2, workstation 2 operates blocks 3 and 4, and 
workstation 3 operates blocks 5 and 6. Within a block, all 
tasks are executed simultaneously; thus, the processing 
time of a block is equal to the maximum processing time 
among its assigned tasks. The number of tasks per block 
is limited by a technological upper bound Mtb. Second, 
the blocks of each workstation are scheduled sequentially, 
and the workstation processing time is equal to the sum of 
its active block durations. Since all workstations operate 
in parallel, the CT of the transfer line is determined by the

The following modelling assumptions underlie the

• Processing times T ai and power demand values Tpi are 
deterministic. Stochastic variability in task durations

• The number of workstations NM, the maximum num-

• All inclusion, exclusion, block-level exclusion, and

3.1. CP model
T o generate high-quality Pareto sets for small-scale 
instances of the BSMTL, a multi-objective CP model 
is developed. CP is selected as the modelling frame-
work because the BSMTL is inherently a disjunctive 
scheduling problem: it combines task-to-block assign-
ment, block formation, and block execution schedul-
ing under precedence, inclusion/exclusion, and techno-
logical compatibility constraints. CP handles this class 
of problems through global constraints and constraint 
propagation, without the auxiliary binary variables and 
big-M linearizations that MILP formulations of dis-
junctive problems typically require, and which tend 
to weaken LP relaxations and slow convergence. The 
scheduling component is represented compactly through 
interval decision variables and global constraints, which 
have dedicated propagation algorithms. An equivalent 
MILP formulation would require binary sequencing vari-
ables for every pair of blocks, leading to quadratic 
growth in model size. Recent works on integrated line 
balancing and scheduling problems confirm that CP 
models outperform state-of-the-art MILP formulations 
in objective value and solution time for this class of 
problems (Kheirabadi et al. 2025; Nourmohammadi 
et al. 2025; C. Zheng et al. 2025; C. Zheng, Li, and Pon-
nambalam 2025). The CP model is solved using IBM CP 
Optimiser and its solutions serve as reference Pareto sets 
for validating the proposed NN_MOVNS metaheuristic. 
For completeness, an equivalent MILP formulation of the 
BSMTL is provided in Appendix 1.

Despite its effectiveness on small-scale instances, the CP 
model becomes computationally intractable as problem 
size grows. The main source of complexity is the coupled 
assignment-and-scheduling structure of the BSMTL. 
Each task can potentially be assigned to multiple blocks, 
represented by the optional interval variables Yi,q, while 
satisfying precedence, inclusion/exclusion, technological 
eligibility, and block-capacity constraints. As the num-
bers of tasks, workstations, and blocks increase, the num-
ber of feasible task-to-block assignments grows rapidly. 
Moreover, each feasible assignment induces additional 
timing decisions for block start and end times, as well as 
time-indexed power calculations over the planning hori-
zon through actt,q,i, blkq,t, machk,t, and syst. Since the 
CP model is solved repeatedly within the ε-constraint 
framework to generate reference Pareto sets, the overall 
computational effort increases substantially with instance 
size. As a result, the exact CP approach becomes imprac-
tical for medium- and large-scale instances within the 
imposed time limit, which motivates the development

4. Solution method
The proposed NN_MOVNS algorithm builds on the 
variable neighbourhood search (VNS) framework, which 
has demonstrated strong and consistent performance in 
constrained combinatorial optimisation, notably in line 
balancing (Meng, Tang, and Zhang 2022; Zhao, Tang, 
and Zhang 2022; Zhao et al. 2024) and scheduling (J.-
K. Li et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026), outperforming 
population-based methods on highly structured prob-
lem instances. The block-based structure of the BSMTL, 
combined with its dense constraint landscape covering 
precedence, inclusion/exclusion, and technological com-
patibility constraints, makes it particularly well-suited to 
neighbourhood search, where problem-specific opera-
tors can directly exploit the feasibility structure.
The algorithm, described in Algorithm 1, comprises 
an initialisation stage followed by a main iterative loop 
that integrates MOVNS-based shaking and local search, 
DQN-guided operator selection, and a learning mech-
anism. The initialisation stage generates an initial fea-
sible solution using a constructive heuristic, and con-
currently prepares the Pareto archive for storing non-
dominated solutions and the DQN agent with its expe-
rience replay buffer. The main iterative loop operates 
until a time-based termination criterion is met. At the 
start of each iteration, a diversification step periodically 
reseeds the working solution from the Pareto archive to 
prevent premature convergence. A shaking operator is 
then applied to perturb the current solution before local 
search proceeds. The selection of a local search oper-
ator is randomised during an initial warm-up period 
and is subsequently guided by the DQN agent via an ε-
greedy policy based on a five-dimensional state vector. 
The application of the selected operator yields a new solu-
tion, which is then verified for feasibility and evaluated 
against the two objectives. A multi-component reward 
function assessing Pareto expansion, diversity improve-
ment, and per-objective gain is computed and, together 
with the observed state transition, stored in the experi-
ence replay buffer (Liu et al. 2025). The Pareto archive is 
updated accordingly, and the DQN network is periodi-
cally retrained via mini-batch sampling from the buffer, 
allowing the agent to improve its operator selection pol-
icy throughout the search. Upon reaching the termi-
nation criterion, the algorithm outputs the final Pareto 
archive. The subsequent sections provide detailed formu-
lations for each of these components.

4.1. Feasible solution generation using constructive 
heuristic
The constructive heuristic generates an initial feasible 
solution through a multi-step process that ensures tasks 
are assigned to blocks on different workstations while 
respecting all problem constraints. The steps are as 
follows:
(1) T ask ordering: In this step, the initial sequence of 
tasks is determined using the Ranked Positional 
W eight method (Helgeson and Birnie 1961), which 
prioritises tasks based on their cumulative positional 
weights (the sum of a task’ s processing time and 
all its successor task times) to establish an efficient 
preliminary order.
(2) Initial task assignment: Distribute tasks to all work-
stations following the order determined in the pre-
vious step. Initially, each workstation is treated 
as a candidate location for each task, forming 
a workstation–task eligibility matrix. Subsequent 
constraint-filtering steps progressively remove infea-
sible task–workstation combinations before the final 
unique assignment is constructed.
(3) Inclusion constraint implementation: For task pairs 
requiring co-location, identified by incij > 0, the 
heuristic enforces that tasks i and j can only remain 
feasible for workstations where both tasks can be 
jointly assigned. If either task is infeasible or unavail-
able on a workstation, the paired task is also marked 
infeasible for that workstation.
(4) Exclusion constraint implementation: For task pairs 
prohibited from co-location (identified via exclusion 
matrix excij > 0), if both tasks are assigned to the 
same workstation, retain one task based on priori-
tisation and mark the other as infeasible (−1) for 
that workstation. Additionally, mark the retained 
task as incompatible (−1) on any workstation that 
initially designates both tasks together, maintaining 
exclusivity constraints.
(5) T echnological constraint implementation: For tasks 
requiring specific workstations (identified via tech-
nological matrix tecik = 1), ensure assignment only 
to technologically capable workstations by removing 
the task from any incompatible workstation, main-
taining technological compatibility requirements.
(6) Final assignment: Assigns tasks to workstations to 
balance workload while satisfying all constraints. A 
threshold is calculated as total task time divided 
by workstation count multiplied by the coefficient 
β = 2.7. For each workstation (except the last), 
tasks are assigned from the prioritised list only if 
prerequisites are completed on the same or ear-
lier workstations. Assignment continues until the 
threshold is reached. When exceeded, assigned tasks 
are marked infeasible (−1) on remaining worksta-
tions, and unassignable tasks are marked infeasible 
for the current workstation. Exception: tasks requir-
ing specific workstations are assigned regardless of 
threshold violations.
(7) Block creation and task-to-block assignment: This 
step groups each workstation’ s tasks into discrete 
blocks while satisfying exclusion constraints. The 
algorithm first generates a set number of empty 
blocks per workstation (Nb). Then, for each work-
station, it iterates through its tasks and tries to assign 
each one to an existing block. Before placement, 
it checks the block exclusion matrix (exc_bij) to 
ensure no conflicts with tasks already exist in that 
block. If there is a conflict (exc_bij = 1), the task is 
skipped for that block. The search continues until 
a suitable block is found or a new one is created if 
needed. Each block can contain up to a maximum 
number of tasks (Mtb), ensuring balanced workload
distribution.
(8) CT calculation: This step computes the execution 
times for each workstation and determines the over-
all CT of the system. For each workstation, the 
algorithm processes the blocks sequentially, calcu-
lating the execution time of each block as the max-
imum task duration among all tasks within that 
block (since tasks within a block execute in paral-
lel). The workstation’ s total CT is computed as the 
sum of all its block execution times, representing 
the sequential execution of blocks on that worksta-
tion. The algorithm tracks the start and end times for 
each block to provide detailed timing information. 
The final system CT is determined as the maxi-
mum CT across all workstations, representing the 
bottleneck workstation that determines the overall 
system performance. This step provides the founda-
tion for evaluating one of the primary optimisation 
objectives.
(9) Power demand calculation: This step determines the 
power demand profile for each block across all work-
stations to support the second optimisation objec-
tive. For each workstation, the algorithm processes 
each block and calculates the total power demand 
as the sum of power requirements of all tasks 
within that block. Since tasks within a block execute 
simultaneously, their power demand are additive. 
The algorithm handles various edge cases, includ-
ing empty blocks (assigned zero power demand) 
and validates the integrity of the task assignments.

4.2. Shaking operators
The proposed MOVNS uses two distinct shaking opera-
tors (SO), SO1 and SO2, to perturb the current solution 
and help avoid local optima. In the shaking phase, SO1 is 
applied first; if it fails to produce a feasible solution within 
As = 20 attempts, SO2 is applied instead with the same 
attempt limit. If neither operator yields a feasible solu-
tion, the algorithm retains the current solution, thereby 
ensuring that a feasible solution is always maintained.
SO1 (Block shift neighbourhood). A shifting process 
takes place wherein a block is randomly selected from 
a randomly chosen workstation and is then moved to 
a new position either earlier or later within the same 
workstation’ s block sequence. The feasibility of this move 
depends on ensuring that all constraints, including prece-
dence relationships, are satisfied after the shift.
SO2 (Intra-workstation block split). A splitting process 
occurs wherein a block with at least two tasks is ran-
domly selected from a chosen workstation, and a subset 
of tasks from this block is moved to another block on 
the same workstation. The target block may be an exist-
ing one or an empty block. The move is only feasible if 
the target block has enough capacity to accommodate the 
additional tasks and all relevant constraints are satisfied.
4.3. Neighbourhood operators
This section briefly explains the structure of the neigh-
bourhood operators used in the local search phase of 
NN_MOVNS.
N1 (Inter-workstation task swap). A swapping process 
takes place wherein two different workstations are ran-
domly selected, and from each workstation, a non-empty 
block is chosen at random. Subsequently, a task from 
each selected block is picked randomly and exchanged 
between the blocks. The feasibility of this move depends 
on ensuring that all constraints are satisfied after the 
swap.
N2 (Intra-workstation adjacent block task swap). A 
swapping process occurs wherein a single worksta-
tion is randomly selected, and from this workstation, 
two adjacent non-empty blocks are chosen at random. 
Subsequently, a task from each of these blocks is picked 
randomly and exchanged between them. The feasibility 
of this move depends on ensuring that all constraints are 
satisfied after the swap.
N3 (T ask relocation across workstations). A moving 
process takes place wherein two distinct workstations are 
randomly selected from the set of available workstations. 
From each workstation, a block is chosen at random, with 
the source block on the first workstation having at least 
one task and the target block on the second workstation 
having available capacity. Subsequently, a task from the 
source block is randomly picked and moved to the target 
block on the second workstation. The feasibility of this 
move depends on satisfying all problem constraints after 
the task is moved.
N4 (T ask relocation within a workstation). A moving 
process occurs wherein a single workstation is randomly 
selected from the set of available workstations. On this 
workstation, two distinct blocks (not necessarily adja-
cent) are chosen at random, with the source block having 
at least one task and the target block having available 
capacity. Subsequently, a task from the source block is 
randomly picked and moved to the target block on the 
same workstation. The feasibility of this move depends 
on satisfying all problem constraints after the task is 
moved.
N5 (T ask insertion across blocks). A relocation process 
takes place wherein a task is randomly selected from a 
non-empty block on a workstation, and an attempt is 
made to insert it into blocks on other workstations that 
have available capacity. The target blocks are considered 
in a randomised order to promote exploration diversity. 
The move is accepted only if all problem constraints are 
satisfied, with the first feasible insertion being accepted.
N6 (T ask relocation from higher CT to adjacent lower 
CT workstation). A shifting process takes place wherein a 
pair of adjacent workstations is randomly selected from 
the set of available workstations, and the workstation 
with the higher CT is identified as the source, while the 
other is the target. From the source workstation, the last 
task of the last non-empty block is chosen, and it is moved 
to the start of the first block of the target workstation if 
capacity allows. The feasibility of this move depends on 
satisfying all the constraints of the problem after the task 
is shifted.
N7 (T ask move between adjacent blocks on single work-
station). A moving process occurs wherein a single work-
station is randomly selected from the set of available 
workstations, and from this workstation, two adjacent 
blocks are chosen at random, with the source block hav-
ing at least one task and the target block having available 
capacity. Subsequently, a task from the source block is 
randomly picked and moved to the target block on the

4.4. Key components of the NN_MOVNS framework
The proposed NN_MOVNS algorithm builds on tradi-
tional MOVNS by incorporating a DQN to guide the 
adaptive selection of neighbourhood operators. Unlike 
conventional MOVNS frameworks, which apply oper-
ators in a fixed or random order, this static approach 
can limit the algorithm’ s ability to escape local optima 
and explore the solution space effectively (Kalatzanton-
akis, Sifaleras, and Samaras 2025). By using deep rein-
forcement learning, the proposed method continuously 
learns which operators perform best, allowing it to adjust 
dynamically between exploration and exploitation based 
on accumulated search experience rather than relying 
on preset rules. This integration of learning mechanisms 
into neighbourhood search is supported by a growing 
body of evidence in the scheduling and manufactur-
ing optimisation literature, where reinforcement learn-
ing has been shown to improve operator selection in 
VNS (Kalatzantonakis, Sifaleras, and Samaras 2025; P . Li 
et al. 2023), graph neural networks have been combined 
with neighbourhood search for combinatorial schedul-
ing (T . Guo et al. 2025), and deep reinforcement learning 
has been applied to resilient production control in com-
plex manufacturing environments (Leng et al. 2024; Liu 
et al. 2025). The key components of the NN_MOVNS 
framework, as outlined above, are detailed in the follow-
ing subsections
4.4.1. DQN architecture and state representation
The DQN integrated into NN_MOVNS employs a multi-
layer perceptron architecture to approximate the action-
value function that maps search states to the expected 
cumulative rewards of each neighbourhood operator (F . 
Ming et al. 2024). The architecture consists of an input 
layer that receives five-dimensional state feature vectors, 
two hidden layers with sixty-four neurons each using rec-
tified linear unit activations, and an output layer with 
eight neurons representing the Q-values of the eight 
local search operators. The DQN training configuration 
was determined through the parameter tuning proce-
dure described in Section 5.2, and the final settings are 
reported in Table 4. These include the optimiser, learn-
ing rate, discount factor, batch size, exploration parame-
ters, and training frequency. Experience replay is used to 
reduce the correlation between successive samples, while 
reward clipping bounds the magnitude of temporal-
difference updates and improves numerical stability. T o 
examine whether a more advanced deep reinforcement 
learning architecture was required, preliminary tests were 
also conducted with a Double DQN variant (Y . Zhang 
et al. 2023), which decouples action selection from target-
value estimation to reduce overestimation bias. Across a 
representative subset of instances, Double DQN did not 
produce consistently better Pareto fronts, while increas-
ing the training overhead due to the additional target-
network evaluation. Given the compact state represen-
tation with five features and the limited action space 
of eight neighbourhood operators, the overestimation 
bias addressed by Double DQN was not observed to 
be a dominant performance-limiting factor. Therefore, 
the standard DQN architecture was retained as the most 
parsimonious and computationally efficient alternative.

4.4.2. Action space and neighbourhood operators
The action space in the reinforcement learning frame-
work comprises the eight local search operators over 
which the DQN agent learns a selection policy. These 
operators, {N1, N2, N3, N4, N5, N6, N7, N8}, perform
small-scale structural adjustments aimed at solution 
intensification, preserving the integrity of solutions while 
exploring promising regions near the current search posi-
tion. The formal action space is defined as:
A = {N1, N2, N3, N4, N5, N6, N7, N8}
The two shaking operators, SO1 and SO2, are not part 
of the learned action space. Instead, they are applied as 
a mandatory perturbation step at every iteration prior 
to local search, independently of the DQN’ s decision. 
This design reflects the classical VNS principle of separat-
ing diversification (shaking) from intensification (local 
search): the DQN governs the intensification phase only, 
adaptively selecting which local structure to exploit given 
the current search state, while the shaking phase ensures 
structural diversity unconditionally.

4.4.5. Adaptive exploration through warm-up phase

learning.

Complementing the reinforcement learning-guided
operator selection, NN_MOVNS incorporates explicit 
diversification through periodic Pareto set sampling. At 
regular intervals of ten iterations, the current working

complementary exploration channel independent of the 
learned operator preferences, thereby mitigating poten-

5.1.1. Competing algorithms and implementation
T o evaluate the proposed NN_MOVNS, we compare 
it with four competing approaches: the CP model 
described in Section 3.1, the baseline MOVNS, and two 
representative population-based multi-objective meta-
heuristics, namely NSGA-II (Fang et al. 2020; Petersen 
et al. 2024) and multi-objective particle swarm opti-

search paradigms that are commonly used in multi-
objective manufacturing and energy-aware scheduling 
problems. NSGA-II represents an evolutionary search 
framework based on non-dominated sorting, elitist selec-
tion, crossover, and mutation, whereas MOPSO repre-

are updated through individual and collective learn-
ing mechanisms. Their inclusion therefore provides a 
meaningful comparison with algorithms that differ sub-
stantially from VNS in both population management 
and search dynamics, allowing the effectiveness of the 
proposed learning-guided neighbourhood search to be 
assessed against recognised multi-objective optimisation 
baselines.
All four metaheuristics are implemented using the 
same block-based solution representation, objective eval-
uation procedures, constraint-checking mechanisms, 
and computational-time stopping criterion. This com-

parison focuses on the search behaviour of the algorithms 
rather than differences in representation, evaluation, or 
feasibility handling.

The implementation details of the four metaheuris-
tics are summarised below. All tuned and fixed parameter 
values are consolidated in Table 4.
5.1.1.1. NN_MOVNS (proposed). NN_MOVNS is the 
learning-guided VNS algorithm described in Section 4. 
At each iteration a shaking operator (SO1 or SO2) diver-
sifies the current solution, after which one of the eight 
local search operators N1–N8 is applied. The operator is 
selected by a DQN-guided ε-greedy policy. A warm-up 
phase covering 20% of the runtime precedes the learning-
guided phase, during which operators are selected uni-
formly at random.
5.1.1.2. MOVNS. MOVNS serves as the baseline VNS 
method. It employs the same shaking and neighbour-
hood operators as NN_MOVNS but does not use the 
DQN-guided operator selection mechanism. This com-
parison therefore isolates the contribution of the learning 
component.
5.1.1.3. NSGA-II. The NSGA-II implementation uses 
non-dominated sorting and crowding-distance selec-
tion (Petersen et al. 2024). Offspring are generated via 
two crossover operators (workstation-level and task-
level) and five mutation move types (task relocation 
across workstations, task swap between blocks, within-
workstation block reordering, block movement between 
workstations, and multi-move perturbation). Because the 
BSMTL is highly constrained, infeasible offspring are 
passed through a repair procedure. If repair fails, the 
parent solution is retained.
5.1.1.4. MOPSO. The MOPSO implementation main-
tains an external archive of non-dominated solutions. 
At each update, a guiding solution is selected from this 
archive using crowding distance (L. Guo et al. 2025). 
Since the BSMTL is a discrete problem, the standard con-
tinuous velocity update is replaced by a discrete mech-
anism: the inertia component applies a random muta-
tion, the cognitive component moves the particle toward 
its personal best solution, and the social component 
moves it toward the selected archive solution. Infeasible 
solutions produced by these updates undergo the same 
repair procedure as in NSGA-II. T o counteract stagna-
tion, turbulence mutation and periodic swarm reseeding 
are applied.
5.1.1.5. Computational effort. Although all four meta-
heuristics share the same instance-dependent time 
limit, the single solution-based and population-based 
methods allocate this budget differently. NN_MOVNS 
and MOVNS follow a sequential iterate-and-improve 
scheme: each iteration perturbs the incumbent solution, 
applies a single neighbourhood operator, checks feasi-
bility, and updates the approximation set. NSGA-II and 
MOPSO instead process entire populations at each gen-
eration, applying crossover, mutation, or velocity updates

5.1.2. Performance metrics
A rigorous and multi-faceted evaluation of algorithmic 
performance is conducted using three complementary 
quality indicators (Beldar et al. 2026): the relative hyper-
volume (HVrel), the inverted generational distance plus 
(IGD+), and the coverage metric. This set of indica-
tors enables a holistic assessment of convergence quality, 
solution diversity, and pairwise dominance relationships 
within the generated Pareto fronts.
The HVrel indicator quantifies the volume of the 
objective space dominated by a solution set relative to a 
reference point, thereby capturing both convergence and 
spread. The IGD+ metric calculates the average distance 
from points on the reference Pareto front to their nearest 
solution in the obtained approximation front, measur-
ing how well the generated front covers and conforms 
to the best-known trade-off surface. Finally, the coverage 
metric provides a direct pairwise comparison between 
two solution sets by quantifying the fraction of solutions 
in one set that are dominated by solutions in the other. 
T ogether, these three indicators provide a comprehensive 
evaluation of each algorithm’ s performance.
5.2. Parameter tuning
The performance of metaheuristic algorithms is sensi-
tive to their control parameters. Therefore, the Taguchi 
method (Mahmoodi et al. 2024) was employed to config-
ure the parameters of all four metaheuristics. An orthog-
onal array design was used to explore each algorithm’ s 
parameter space efficiently, reducing the number of 
experimental runs while capturing the main effects of 
individual factors. For all algorithms, a representative 
subset of 15 instances covering the three size categories 
(small, medium, and large) was used as the tuning 
testbed, with the mean IGD+ value as the response vari-
able and the signal-to-noise (SN) ratio under the ‘smaller 
is better’ criterion used to identify robust settings.
For NN_MOVNS, an L18 orthogonal array with seven 
factors at three levels was employed. The tuned factors 
are the learning rate, the initial and minimum ε-greedy 
values, the decay factor, the discount factor, the replay 
buffer capacity, and the batch size. Figure 2 shows the 
corresponding main effects plots. In these plots, red cir-
cles indicate the selected tuned values, while dashed 
lines indicate the overall mean. For most factors, both 
the mean response and SN ratio led to the same pre-
ferred level. The same Taguchi procedure was applied to 
MOVNS, NSGA-II, and MOPSO. For brevity, only the

7.2. Peak shaving and energy redistribution across 
problem scales
Peak shaving is critical in industrial facilities, where elec-
tricity costs are heavily influenced by demand charges 
based on maximum power demand. Unlike residential 
users, where fixed capacity charges dominate, industrial 
users face contractual limits and subscription agreements 
that make PP reduction directly tied to cost efficiency and 
operational compliance. In this context, NN_MOVNS 
reduces PP primarily resulting in energy redistribution. 
Energy redistribution refers to reshaping the power–time 
profile such that the same total electrical energy is 
consumed, but high instantaneous power demands are 
reduced by spreading energy usage more evenly over the 
operating period. Task execution is rescheduled to avoid 
simultaneous high-power events, resulting in lower peaks 
while preserving the integral (total energy) of the power 
profile.
T o quantify the peak reduction achieved by NN_
MOVNS, the PSI is defined as the percentage reduction 
in maximum PP:
PSI =
(PPmax initial) − (PPmax NN_MOVNS)
(PPmax initial) × 100
A higher PSI indicates a greater reduction in PP .
T o directly measure the degree of load reshaping 
attributable to energy redistribution, the discrete ERI is 
defined as:
ERI =
∑︁ max(0, Pinitial[k] − PNN_MOVNS[k])1t∑︁ Pinitial[k]1t
Here, the numerator represents the energy removed from 
time instants where the optimised profile is lower than 
the initial profile (i.e. energy shaved from high-demand 
periods), while the denominator is the total electrical 
energy over the cycle. Therefore, ERI ∈ [0, 1], where 
ERI = 0 indicates no redistribution (the profiles coincide 
in the sense of no shaved energy), and larger ERI values 
indicate a stronger redistribution effect, meaning more 
energy has been shifted away from high-power instants 
and spread across the operating horizon.
Figure 6 presents peak shaving results for six represen-
tative instances spanning small, medium, and large prob-
lem scales. Table 13 reports the corresponding PSI and 
ERI values, complementing the visual trends observed in 
the figure. PP reductions range from 33.33% to 73.17%, 
with problem size influencing the extent of improvement. 
Small-scale cases (T11, T18) achieve notable reductions 
of 46.03% and 53.57%, respectively, despite fewer tasks 
available for rescheduling. Even in these compact sys-
tems, NN_MOVNS eliminates sharp peaks in the ini-
tial schedules, confirming that meaningful peak shaving 
can be obtained even under limited scheduling flexi-
bility. Medium-scale cases (T30, T38) show moderate 
improvements of 40.68% and 33.33%, respectively. Their 
initial power curves contain multiple local peaks that are 
smoothed in the optimised profiles, although the achiev-
able improvements are less pronounced than in larger 
systems due to fewer degrees of freedom for redistribu-
tion. Large-scale cases (T45, T54) achieve the strongest 
results, with PP reductions of 70.27% and 73.17%,
respectively.
Consistent with these results, the proposed method 
yields notable ERI values across all test problems, with 
particularly high percentages in T45 and T54, reaching 
35.28% and 31.53%. These results indicate that a substan-
tial portion of the high power demand in large machining 
transfer lines is temporally redistributed over the operat-
ing cycle, leading to significantly smoother power profiles 
without altering the total energy consumption per cycle. 
However, these improvements can slightly increase CT 
(by an average of 5.7%, with a maximum of 12.7% in T30), 
the reductions in PP and the associated redistribution of 
energy outweigh the relatively small CT increases.

posed peak shaving strategy on an industrial manufac-

a high-voltage (HV)/medium-voltage (MV) transformer. 
In the initial operating scenario (orange power pro-

systems, or additional process equipment, without violat-
ing grid constraints or transformer limits. Importantly, 
this reduction is achieved through temporal redistribu-
tion of power demand rather than energy curtailment, 
preserving the total energy required per production cycle 
while substantially alleviating peak loading at the feeder 
and grid interface levels.

7.4. Findings and managerial insights
This subsection synthesises the key findings of the study 
by explicitly addressing the three research questions 
posed in the Introduction, and by drawing out their 
practical implications for production and energy man-
agement.
The first research question (RQ1) asked how the 
BSMTL can be jointly formulated as a bi-objective opti-
misation problem that simultaneously minimises CT and 
PP under the full set of precedence, inclusion/exclusion, 
and technological compatibility constraints. The pro-
posed CP model answers this by integrating task-to-
block assignment, block formation, and block execu-
tion scheduling within a unified framework that captures 
the temporal interaction between task assignments and 
system-level power demand (Section 3.1). The formula-
tion confirms that CT and PP cannot be optimised in iso-
lation, since the assignment of tasks to blocks determines 
which power demands accumulate simultaneously, while 
the block execution sequence determines when those 
28 
 P. BELDAR ET AL.
peaks occur. Optimising either dimension independently 
therefore leaves the other uncontrolled, as illustrated by 
the contrasting solutions in Figure 1. For production 
managers in automotive and aerospace manufacturing, 
this implies that energy and scheduling decisions must 
be treated jointly rather than sequentially, because facil-
ities that balance and schedule as separate steps may 
be systematically generating avoidable peak demand and 
inflated electricity charges.
The second research question (RQ2) examined
whether a MOVNS enhanced with deep reinforcement 
learning can outperform both exact methods and stan-
dard metaheuristics in terms of Pareto front quality 
across small, medium, and large-scale instances. The 
computational experiments confirm that NN_MOVNS 
consistently outperforms the baseline MOVNS across 
all three performance indicators and all problem scales, 
with the advantage becoming increasingly pronounced 
as instance size grows and reaching an 88.4% reduc-
tion in mean IGD+ on large instances, a difference 
confirmed as statistically significant by the Wilcoxon 
signed-rank test (Tables 8, 9, and 11). While CP pro-
duces exact or near-exact Pareto sets on small instances, it 
becomes intractable at medium and large scales, and both 
VNS-based methods significantly outperform NSGA-II 
and MOPSO, confirming that the block-based neigh-
bourhood structure is better suited to the BSMTL than 
population-based search. For production planners and 
operations engineers, these results indicate that learning-
enhanced metaheuristics offer a robust and scalable alter-
native to exact solvers, capable of delivering high-quality 
CT and PP trade-off solutions within realistic computa-
tion times even for industrial-scale problems.
The third research question (RQ3) focussed on the 
practical implications of the approach in terms of PP 
reduction, energy redistribution, and PAPR improve-
ment. As reported in Sections 7 and the peak-shaving 
analysis above, NN_MOVNS delivers consistent PP 
reductions across all problem scales, with the accom-
panying ERI values confirming that these reductions 
are achieved through temporal redistribution of energy 
demand rather than curtailment, so that the total energy 
consumed per production cycle is preserved. Large-scale 
instances benefit most, owing to their greater schedul-
ing flexibility, and the industrial-scale test instance 
further demonstrates the same behaviour at realis-
tic problem size. For manufacturers facing electricity 
demand charges, transformer capacity limits, or site-level 
grid constraints, these findings confirm that intelligent 
rescheduling via NN_MOVNS is a practically viable peak 
shaving tool. It lowers energy costs and improves opera-
tional compliance without sacrificing production conti-
nuity, while the released electrical capacity can be safely 
reallocated to auxiliary production lines or utility systems 
within the same manufacturing site.
8. Conclusions
This study addressed the balancing and scheduling of 
machining transfer lines (BSMTL), in which cycle time 
(CT) and peak power (PP) are simultaneously min-
imised under precedence, inclusion/exclusion, and tech-
nological compatibility constraints. Three main contri-
butions were made. First, a multi-objective CP model 
was developed and solved via a bidirectional ε-constraint 
method, generating high-quality Pareto sets for small-
scale instances and establishing a rigorous mathemat-
ical foundation for the joint CT–PP optimisation in 
machining transfer lines. Second, a MOVNS algorithm 
with a two-phase constructive heuristic was proposed to 
address the scalability limitations of the CP model for 
medium- and large-scale problems. Third, the MOVNS 
framework was augmented with a DQN-based operator 
selection mechanism (NN_MOVNS) that dynamically 
adapts search behaviour during optimisation, improv-
ing both convergence and Pareto front diversity over the 
static baseline.
Computational experiments across 55 benchmark 
instances confirm that NN_MOVNS consistently outper-
forms the baseline MOVNS and both population-based 
methods across all performance indicators and problem 
scales, with the advantage widening on larger instances 
and confirmed as statistically significant by Wilcoxon 
signed-rank tests. On the industrial-scale test instance of 
a 148-task automotive cylinder head production line, it 
reduced peak power by 63.1% through temporal energy 
redistribution rather than curtailment, releasing substan-
tial grid capacity for reallocation to other site loads while 
keeping the total energy per cycle unchanged.
Beyond the BSMTL problem, the proposed NN_
MOVNS framework can generalise to other production 
line types and combinatorial optimisation settings, pro-
vided that its problem-specific components are appropri-
ately redefined. The archive-guided Pareto search struc-
ture and the DQN-based adaptive operator selection 
mechanism are line-type independent and require no 
structural modification. For assembly lines, the block-
based solution representation would be replaced by a 
station-task assignment encoding, and the neighbour-
hood operators would be redesigned to act on task 
reassignment and within-station sequencing rather than 
block formation. For reconfigurable manufacturing sys-
tems, an additional configuration-selection layer would 
need to be incorporated into the solution encoding and 
feasibility checks to account for varying machine con-
figurations across production runs. Regarding objective

extensions, incorporating a third objective such as total 
energy consumption, workload imbalance, or line cost 
would require extending the robjective component of the 
DQN reward function to cover the additional dimen-
sion, adjusting the Pareto dominance check accordingly, 
and updating the DQN state vector to encode the new 
objective value. The overall learning architecture would 
otherwise remain unchanged. More broadly, applying the 
DQN-guided operator selection to other combinatorial 
problems such as flexible job shop scheduling, vehicle 
routing, or supply chain network design requires defin-
ing a meaningful state representation, a set of comple-
mentary neighbourhood operators, and a reward signal 
reflecting solution quality improvement.
Several limitations of the present study should be 
acknowledged. First, task processing times and power 
demand values are assumed to be deterministic and 
constant; real-world variability due to tool wear, mate-
rial inconsistencies, or machine conditions is not cap-
tured. Second, power demand values for the benchmark 
instances were estimated from manufacturers’ catalogs 
rather than measured directly on physical systems, which 
may not fully reflect operational variability. Third, the 
CP model becomes intractable for medium- and large-
scale instances, limiting its use to small-scale validation. 
Fourth, the study focuses on a single-product, single-
cycle setting; multi-product transfer lines with mixed 
precedence structures are outside the current scope. 
Fifth, the DQN reward weights (w1 = 0.8, w2 = 0.9, 
w3 = 0.3) were determined through preliminary experi-
mentation and may require recalibration for significantly 
different problem structures.
Future research could extend this work in sev-
eral directions. Incorporating stochastic factors such as 
machine breakdowns, processing time variability, and 
real-time energy pricing would better reflect indus-
trial uncertainties. Additional sustainability objectives, 
including total energy consumption, carbon emissions, 
and renewable energy utilisation, could also be consid-
ered. Extending the framework to multi-product settings 
and hybrid exact–metaheuristic schemes may further 
broaden its applicability. Furthermore, combining the 
complementary strengths of the CP and MILP formula-
tions through decomposition schemes such as Benders 
decomposition or Dantzig–W olfe reformulation could 
yield more scalable exact methods for larger instances. 
Moreover, generative AI techniques, such as diffu-
sion models or AI-generated content frameworks (Leng 
et al. 2025), could be integrated into the initialisa-
tion or neighbourhood design phases of NN_MOVNS 
to improve convergence speed and Pareto front diver-
sity in dynamic production environments. Future exten-
sions could also explore federated learning frameworks 
in which the DQN operator selection policy is trained 
collaboratively across multiple production sites with-
out sharing sensitive operational data, enabling privacy-
preserving peak shaving and energy redistribution in 
distributed, multi-plant manufacturing networks.

The objective functions (A1) and (A2) minimise CT and PP , 
respectively. Constraint (A3) guarantees each task is assigned 
to exactly one block. Constraint (A4) enforces precedence 
relationships between tasks. Constraint (A5) prevents incom-
patible tasks from being assigned to the same block. Con-
straints (A6) and (A7) enforce workstation inclusion and exclu-
sion rules for task assignments, respectively. Constraint (A8) 
ensures tasks are assigned only to technologically compatible 
workstations. Constraint (A9) limits the number of tasks per 
block to Mtb.
Constraint (A10) suppresses phantom start times for unas-
signed task–block pairs. Constraint (A11) anchors the first 
block on each workstation to start at time zero. Con-
straints (A12) and (A13) define the block start time BSq via 
big-M linearisation. Constraint (A14) defines the block end

time BEq as the latest task completion within the block. Con-
straints (A15) and (A16) define the block activity indicator aq, 
which equals 1 if and only if block q contains at least one task. 
Constraints (A17) and (A18) enforce contiguous sequential 
scheduling of active blocks on the same workstation, eliminat-
ing idle time between them. Constraint (A19) prevents gaps 
in block usage by requiring earlier blocks to be filled before 
later ones. Constraints (A20) and (A21) set the duration of any 
inactive block to zero by equating its start and end time.
Constraints (A22)–(A29) linearise the activity indicator 
ut,q,i, which equals 1 if and only if task i is assigned to block 
q and is being processed at time t. This is achieved via two 
auxiliary binaries: w1 t,q,i (not-yet started) and w2 t,q,i (already fin-
ished), whose combined logic forces ut,q,i = 1 exactly over the 
active window si,q ≤ t < si,q + T ai. Constraint (A30) computes 
the power contribution of each active task as Tpi · ut,q,i. Con-
straints (A31), (A32), and (A33) aggregate power bottom-up 
through the task, block, workstation, and system levels. Con-
straint (A34) determines the PP as the maximum system power 
across all time slots. Constraint (A35) computes the CT as 
the maximum completion time of any assigned task across all 
blocks.
Appendix 2. Sensitivity analysis of reward 
function weights
T o assess the sensitivity of NN_MOVNS to the reward function 
weighting coefficients (w1, w2, w3), five weight configurations 
were evaluated on five representative instances drawn from 
the main experimental testbed: T4 (JACKSON, 11 tasks), T14 
(BUXEY , 29 tasks), T19 (GUNTHER, 35 tasks), T22 (KIL-
BRID, 45 tasks), and T25 (KILBRID, 45 tasks). Each config-
uration was executed ten times under the same time limits 
and parameter settings used in the main experiments, with 
only the reward weights varied. The five configurations are: 
C1 (baseline, w1=0.8, w2=0.9, w3=0.3), C2 (equal, 0.5, 0.5, 
0.5), C3 (objective-heavy, 0.3, 0.3, 0.9), C4 (diversity-heavy, 0.5, 
1.0, 0.1), and C5 (Pareto-heavy, 1.0, 0.5, 0.1). Table A1 reports 
the mean HVrel and IGD+ values across the ten independent 
runs.
The baseline configuration C1 achieves the best mean HVrel
and the lowest mean IGD+ across all five tested instances. 
This consistently strong performance supports the baseline 
reward-weight configuration adopted in the main computa-
tional experiments.
Configurations that preserve relatively high weights on the 
Pareto expansion and diversity components exhibit only mod-
erate performance reductions. Under C4 (diversity-heavy), 
HVrel decreases by 0.8% on T4 (from 0.988 to 0.980), by 1.2% 
on T14 (from 0.918 to 0.907), by 0.9% on T19 (from 0.992 to 
0.983), by 0.9% on T22 (from 0.993 to 0.984), and by 1.4% 
on T25 (from 0.956 to 0.943). Under C2 (equal weights), the 
reductions are slightly larger but remain below 3% across all 
instances. Under C5 (Pareto-heavy), the largest HVrel reduc-
tion is 2.5% on T25 (from 0.956 to 0.932). These results indi-
cate that the algorithm retains competitive performance under 
the moderate weight perturbations examined in this analysis. 
Among these configurations, C4 is consistently the second-
best, which aligns with the multi-objective design rationale: 
prioritising solution spread along the Pareto front yields bet-
ter approximation quality than emphasising archive cardinality 
or objective improvement alone.
In contrast, configuration C3 (objective-heavy, w3=0.9) 
produces the largest performance degradation across all five 
instances. On T4, IGD+ increases from 1.100 to 2.150 
(+95.5%). On T14, IGD+ rises from 2.860 to 5.480 (+91.6%). 
On T19, IGD+ increases from 1.021 to 2.340 (+129.2%). On 
T22, IGD+ rises from 2.129 to 4.970 (+133.4%). On T25, IGD+
increases from 1.373 to 3.280 (+138.9%). The correspond-
ing HVrel reductions range from 3.8% on T19 (from 0.992 to 
0.954) to 5.7% on T25 (from 0.956 to 0.901). This outcome 
is consistent with the multi-objective nature of the problem: 
when single-objective improvement is excessively prioritised, 
the DQN agent learns to favour operators that improve one 
objective at the expense of Pareto front coverage and diversity, 
resulting in narrower and less well-distributed approximation 
sets.
Overall, the analysis confirms that the selected weight con-
figuration (0.8, 0.9, 0.3) provides an effective balance among the 
three reward components. The algorithm tolerates moderate 
deviations without substantial quality loss, but heavily skew-
ing the reward toward direct objective improvement is detri-
mental, reinforcing the design rationale of prioritising archive 
expansion and solution spread in the multi-objective search 
context.
