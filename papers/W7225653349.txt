Abstract
Visual anomaly detection plays an important role in industrial inspection, edge 
monitoring and visual quality control, particularly where abnormal samples are 
rare, heterogeneous, and often unavailable during training. Recent deep anomaly 
detection approaches have achieved high classification performance by leveraging 
pretrained backbones, memory banks, nearest-neighbor search, synthetic anomaly 
generation, or auxiliary discriminators. However, these methods often increase 
parameter count, floating-point operations (FLOPs), memory usage, update size, and 
energy consumption, which complicates deployment on cost-constrained industrial 
or edge hardware. This study introduces PILAR (Pyramidal Inhibitory Lightweight 
Autoassociative Reconstruction), a compact reconstruction-based architecture for 
anomaly detection. PILAR employs a pyramidal autoassociative encoder-decoder with 
local receptive fields, a spatial bottleneck, pure-pyramid reconstruction, and lateral 
inhibition to promote local competition within feature maps. The model is trained 
only with normal samples, and the anomaly score is obtained from reconstruction 
residuals. Thus, during inference, PILAR does not require class prototypes, covariance 
matrices, support vectors, density parameters, nearest-neighbor databases, or 
discriminator states. PILAR is evaluated on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, 
MVTec AD, and VisA datasets using direct image reconstruction, whole-image 
RGB reconstruction, patchwise RGB reconstruction, and Feature-PILAR-FCN, a fully 
convolutional feature-space variant that uses compact PILAR heads to reconstruct 
selected frozen feature maps. In the evaluated implementation, these maps are 
taken from ResNet18 layer 2 and layer 3 prior to residual scoring. The proposed 
detector reaches 98.86 ± 0.04% Image-AP and 92.63 ± 0.05% AUPRO@30 on MVTec 
AD over five seeds; the unchanged industrial configuration reaches 92.53 ± 0.07% 
Image-AUROC on VisA. Compared with the evaluated ResNet18 configurations of 
SimpleNet and GLASS, Feature-PILAR-FCN retains 35.6% fewer inference parameters 
and achieves 5.2–5.6 times their throughput at batch size 16 on the same Apple 
M4 Max GPU in FP32, despite higher single-image latency. These results indicate 
that PILAR can be used as a low-state anomaly detector when memory footprint 
and computational cost are relevant constraints. They also show that direct RGB

1 Introduction
Visual anomaly detection has motivated research for various practical applications in 
industrial quality control  [ 1]. Recent proposals include early defect detection in mass 
fabric production  [ 2], visual inspection of metal-stamped components  [ 3], anomaly 
localization applied to manufacturing [ 4], and the use of robots for inspection of plas -
tic injection-molded parts [ 5]. Together, these studies indicate that automated anomaly 
detectors are simplifying the process of defect detection in object, texture, and pro -
duction-line contexts. Typically, visual anomaly detection is formulated as a one-class 
problem, where a model is trained on normal data and assigns higher anomaly scores to 
samples that deviate from established normal patterns.
In the context of one-class anomaly detection, autoencoder-based neural networks [ 6] 
have become a common alternative because they can learn the inner structure of normal 
samples and measure deviations by reconstruction residuals or prediction errors. Dif -
ferent variants have been proposed for this purpose. Recurrent autoencoders have been 
used for non-periodic time series  [ 7], while convolutional and regression-augmented 
models have been applied to industrial multivariate streams and benchmark anomaly-
detection datasets  [8, 9]. These methods are useful because they can model nonlinear 
relationships that are difficult to capture with purely statistical forecasting or classical 
machine learning techniques. However, this advantage depends on the data and on the 
experimental protocol. Comparative studies involving conventional methods, machine 
learning models, and deep neural networks show that no single family of methods is 
consistently superior across all scenarios  [ 10, 11]. Therefore, autoencoders should be 
regarded as a flexible, high-performing design pattern whose effectiveness depends on 
the representation, data regime, and deployment constraints.
In IoT, streaming, and industrial monitoring environments, selecting an appropriate 
method requires balancing detection performance against resource constraints. This 
balance must consider accuracy, execution time, memory usage, and model state, as 
these factors all affect whether a detector can be used in practice. Simpler models tend 
to be attractive in this scenario because they are easier to store, update, and execute, 
while neural and ensemble-based methods may require more complex feature extrac -
tion or additional deployment optimization [ 12, 13]. For this reason, recent studies have 
investigated different ways to control this trade-off, including accuracy-memory config -
urations  [12], memory-efficient isolation forest variants  [ 14], and compact time-series 
detectors based on depthwise separable convolutions or memory scheduling  [ 15]. In 
embedded and edge applications, these aspects are decisive, since hardware limitations, 
latency requirements, and energy consumption may prevent the use of a detector that 
performs well in offline experiments but is too costly for continuous operation [16].
PILAR, a Pyramidal Inhibitory Lightweight Autoassociative Reconstruction detector, 
is proposed for deployment in cost-constrained environments. The architecture is based 
on two foundational concepts: AutoAssociative Pyramidal Neural Networks [ 17], which 
demonstrate that combining pyramidal receptive fields with autoassociative memory 
industrial detection requires a more informative representation, which in this work is 
obtained from frozen feature maps.

enables one-class visual recognition with implicit feature extraction, and Lateral Inhibi -

local inhibitory competition can enhance visual classification while maintaining a small 
stored state compared to support-vector-based classification in the evaluated context. 
PILAR follows the same direction and adapts these ideas to anomaly detection. The 
local pyramidal encoding reduces the number of dense connections, the autoassociative 
reconstruction provides the residual score using only normal samples, and the lateral 
inhibition introduces competition among neighboring responses.
Building on earlier pyramidal and feature-reconstruction approaches, PILAR trans -
forms the pyramidal hierarchy into a narrow multi-channel spatial bottleneck with a 
map-to-map decoder and reconstruction-only residual scoring, eliminating dense latent 
expansion and any external memory or classifier state at inference. Therefore, the infer -
ence stage depends only on the trained network weights and on simple score-normaliza-
tion statistics. No prototype set, covariance matrix, support-vector collection, density 
model, memory bank, or nearest-neighbor index is required. If direct RGB reconstruc -
tion is not sufficiently informative, the same compact head can be used to reconstruct 
frozen feature maps, keeping the anomaly score based on reconstruction residuals while 
using a more robust visual representation.
The principal contributions of this study are fourfold: The remainder of this paper 
is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews one-class anomaly detection, autoencoder-
based methods, industrial feature-space detectors, and the pyramidal autoassociative 
and inhibitory neural network background that motivates PILAR. Section 3 presents the 
PILAR architecture, reconstruction objective, and feature-space fully convolutional net -
work (FCN) variant. Section 4 describes the datasets and evaluation metrics. Section 5 
reports results on MVTec AD, VisA, CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST and MNIST, and the 
runtime-state and cost analysis. Section 6 discusses deployment regimes, failure modes, 
and computational-cost implications, followed by limitations and conclusions.
1. We propose PILAR, a compact pyramidal autoassociative anomaly detector that 
combines local receptive-field encoding, lateral inhibition, a spatial bottleneck, pure-
pyramid reconstruction, and residual-based anomaly scoring. The model is trained 
only with normal samples and does not require prototypes, covariance matrices, 
support vectors, density models, memory banks, nearest-neighbor indexes, or 
discriminator states at inference time.
2. We introduce Feature-PILAR-FCN, a fully convolutional feature-space variant 
in which compact PILAR heads reconstruct selected frozen feature maps. In the 
evaluated implementation, these maps are taken from ResNet18 layer 2 and layer 3, 
producing dense residual maps for industrial anomaly localization while keeping the 
anomaly score based only on reconstruction residuals.
3. We evaluate PILAR under multiple operating regimes, including Strict grayscale 
PILAR, RGB-only PILAR, Patchwise RGB PILAR, and Feature-PILAR-FCN. The 
experiments cover MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, MVTec AD, and VisA, 
including Image-AUROC, Image-AP , Pixel-AUROC, AUPRO@30, category- and 
subtype-level review-budget analyses, class-wise localization, pairwise confusion, and 
component-ablation analyses when applicable. The local industrial comparison also 
characterizes retained inference state, single-image and batch execution, and fixed-
budget training cost.

2 Related work
One-class anomaly detection has been traditionally addressed by methods that estimate 
the region occupied by normal samples. One-class support vector machines and support 
vector data description are representative examples of this idea, since both methods try 
to define a boundary around the normal data distribution [ 19, 20]. More recently, deep 
one-class models have followed a similar principle in a learned feature space, mapping 
normal samples to a compact representation [ 21]. Other approaches combine this rep -
resentation learning step with probabilistic modeling, as in deep autoencoding Gaussian 
mixture models, in which the learned representation is associated with a density-estima-
tion mechanism [22].
Autoencoder-based anomaly detection is usually based on the reconstruction error. 
The model is trained using normal samples, and a test pattern is considered more anom-
alous when it cannot be properly reconstructed. This formulation is attractive because 
the training procedure does not require abnormal examples, and the inference stage 
produces a direct residual score. However, if the decoder has excessive capacity, abnor -
mal samples may also be reconstructed with low error, thereby reducing the separation 
between normal and abnormal samples.
Pyramidal representations and reconstruction of pretrained features have been 
explored for anomaly detection. Mishra et al. aggregate deep pyramidal representations 
in an encoder–decoder framework  [ 23], while DFR reconstructs multiscale regional 
features extracted from a pretrained network  [ 24]. MemAE constrains reconstruc -
tion through a learned memory of normal patterns [ 25]. These approaches motivate a 
complementary question: whether a compact pyramidal reconstruction head with local 
receptive fields and lateral inhibition can balance detection accuracy, localization qual -
ity, and inference cost. The contribution examined here is the reconstruction head that 
combines channel projection, input-position weighting shared across overlapping local 
windows, lateral inhibition, and map-to-map decoding. The architectural claim there -
fore concerns this constrained head and its measured accuracy–cost trade-off, rather 
than pyramidal representations or pretrained-feature reconstruction in isolation.
Earlier pyramidal neural-network models provide the architectural basis for PILAR. 
AAPNet combined receptive fields, a pyramidal topology, and autoassociative memory 
for one-class visual pattern recognition, using reconstruction from implicitly extracted 
features as the class-consistency signal  [ 17]. LIPNet introduced lateral inhibition into 
a pyramidal receptive-field network, showing that local inhibitory competition can 
improve visual classification and reduce stored state and classification time in compari -
son with support-vector-based classification in its face-detection experiment [18].
Recent one-class image benchmarks also show that the experimental protocol sig -
nificantly influences the interpretation of results. Some methods are evaluated in a 
strict normal-only setting, where the detector is trained only on samples from the 
normal class. Other methods use additional information, such as auxiliary outlier

Fernandes Discover Artificial Intelligence (2026) 6:1829 
examples, pretrained representations, dense projections, or nearest-neighbor scoring 
mechanisms  [26, 27]. Although these strategies can improve the separation between 
normal and abnormal samples, they also change the assumptions made during train -
ing and inference. Therefore, methods evaluated under different protocols should con -
sider not only the accuracy values but also the training data, representation, and scoring 
mechanism.
Industrial visual anomaly detection is frequently evaluated using the MVTec AD 
benchmark [28]. In this scenario, several methods use pretrained feature representations 
rather than reconstructing or comparing images directly in RGB space. PaDiM models 
the distribution of nominal patch embeddings with Gaussian statistics [ 29], while Patch-
Core stores representative nominal patch features and detects anomalies by nearest-
neighbor distances in this feature space [ 30]. Flow-based methods such as CFLOW-AD 
and FastFlow estimate feature likelihoods with conditional or two-dimensional normal -
izing flows  [ 31, 32]. Teacher–student and distillation-based methods follow another 
direction: STFPM compares feature pyramids produced by a pretrained teacher and a 
student network  [ 33], EfficientAD combines efficient teacher–student anomaly maps 
with an autoencoder component  [ 34], and recent reverse-distillation variants such 
as MRD further improve MVTec accuracy by refining teacher–student feature trans -
fer [35]. Uninformed Students learns discriminative teacher–student embeddings [ 36], 
while DeSTSeg combines denoising student–teacher learning with segmentation guid -
ance  [37]. Mean-shifted contrastive loss adapts pretrained representations through a 
contrastive objective [38]. SimpleNet and DRAEM use synthetic anomaly information in 
different ways: SimpleNet trains a discriminator on perturbed pretrained features [ 39], 
whereas DRAEM learns a discriminative reconstruction embedding from synthetically 
generated surface anomalies [40]. GLASS combines gradient-ascent-guided feature syn -
thesis with image-level texture synthesis  [ 41]. These methods show the importance of 
representation choice for industrial anomaly detection. However, these methods retain 
different inference mechanisms, including stored features, nearest-neighbor search, den-
sity models, discriminators, or teacher–student feature comparison. Synthetic anomaly 
generation belongs to training in SimpleNet and GLASS; both are memory-bank-free 
at inference, like PILAR. This distinction is important when the objective goes beyond 
obtaining high detection accuracy and also includes reducing the information that must 
be stored and processed during inference.
3 PILAR method and architecture
PILAR (Pyramidal Inhibitory Lightweight Autoassociative Reconstruction) is a recon -
struction-based anomaly detector that models the structure of normal visual patterns. 
The model accepts either an image or a feature map extracted from a frozen backbone as 
input, depending on the reconstruction domain specified in the experiment. In the Strict 
and RGB-only configurations, the reconstruction target is the image itself, whereas in 
the Feature-PILAR configuration, the target comprises a set of frozen feature maps. In 
both scenarios, the input representation is processed by a pyramidal encoder consisting 
of local receptive-field layers. The responses produced by this encoder are laterally inhib-
ited inside each feature map, generating a compact spatial representation. A pyramidal 
decoder reconstructs the selected target, and the anomaly score is derived from the

reconstruction residual. This general architecture supports direct image reconstruction, 
patchwise image reconstruction, and fully convolutional feature-map reconstruction.

principle inside each feature map, where the inhibited response depends on the activa -

3.4 Feature-PILAR-FCN for natural and industrial images
The RGB versions of PILAR are reconstruction-domain ablation baselines, designed 
to keep the model small while reconstructing the image directly. This configuration is 
appropriate when the abnormality produces visible changes in the pixel domain. How -
ever, industrial defects are not always expressed in this way. Scratches, imprints, missing 
parts, small texture variations, and local geometric changes may produce weak residuals 
when the model reconstructs only RGB values [ 43]. In these cases, the reconstruction 
error can be affected by illumination, texture, or alignment variations, even when these 
variations are not related to a real defect. Therefore, a more informative representation 
can be useful before applying the compact autoassociative reconstruction model.

macro averages over the 15 MVTec or 12 VisA categories.

FPR curve is interpolated at FPR 0.30, and the area up to this limit is normalized by 0.30.
The computational cost is evaluated using trainable parameters, total inference param-
eters, float32 weight storage, and estimated FLOPs. Measured latency and throughput 
on the same Apple MPS device are reported separately from these model-scale quanti -

6 Discussion
6.1 Architectural contribution and comparison with lightweight anomaly detectors
PILAR shares two elements with existing anomaly-detection approaches: reconstruc -
tion-based scoring and, in Feature-PILAR-FCN, pretrained feature representations. 
However, these elements do not completely define the proposed model. Conventional 
reconstruction-based detectors generally rely on an encoder-decoder trained to repro -
duce normal samples, while several feature-space approaches introduce an additional 
mechanism to model or compare the nominal representation. PaDiM estimates feature 
distributions, PatchCore stores nominal features for nearest-neighbor search, flow-
based methods learn feature densities, and teacher-student or discriminative approaches 
obtain the anomaly signal from feature discrepancies or learned discrimination. PILAR 
follows a different organization. The anomaly detector itself is an inhibited pyramidal 
autoassociative model: feedforward connections are restricted to local receptive fields,

both the image and feature-space configurations.
The pyramidal structure is mainly related to the capacity and state of this reconstruc -
tion model. The controlled experiments in Table 15 illustrate this operating point. Strict 
grayscale PILAR uses 3,670 parameters and reaches an AUC of 0.947 on MNIST, com -
pared with 0.928 for the 215,601-parameter CAE and 0.949 for the 952,400-parameter 
MLP-AE. On Fashion-MNIST, PILAR reaches 0.876, below the CAE and MLP-AE val -
ues of 0.898 and 0.910, respectively. These results do not demonstrate that pyramidal 
connectivity alone improves detection accuracy. Instead, they show that the constrained 
architecture can retain useful one-class reconstruction performance with a substantially 
smaller model state. This is the main role of the local pyramidal organization in the low-
cost configuration.
Lateral inhibition has a more directly isolated effect. As shown in Table 16, remov -
ing inhibition reduces the mean AUC from 0.876 to 0.863 on Fashion-MNIST and from 
0.947 to 0.936 on MNIST. The reduction is small but consistent across both controlled 
datasets. The full-category MVTec ablation has smaller, mixed effects, including a slight 
AUPRO decrease with inhibition. Moreover, using radial inhibition in all layers gives 
0.878 and 0.949, respectively, slightly above the selected baseline. These results are con -
sistent with the expected role of local competition described in Sect.  3.2: inhibition is 
not responsible for the small model state, but it modifies the representation produced 
within that constrained architecture and contributes to its detection performance.
The feature-space experiments also clarify which improvements should not be attrib -
uted only to the pyramidal structure or lateral inhibition. Moving the reconstruction 
target from pixels to frozen features increases CIFAR-10 AUC from 0.574 to 0.812 and 
Fashion-MNIST AUC from 0.876 to 0.936. On MVTec AD, the RGB-only configura -
tions reach approximately 0.696–0.697 mean Image-AUROC, whereas Feature-PILAR-
FCN reaches 0.969. The pretrained representation is therefore an important part of the 
improvement on more difficult visual domains. In this configuration, the feature extrac -
tor provides a more discriminative representation, while PILAR provides the com -
pact autoassociative reconstruction and residual-scoring mechanism applied to that 
representation.
This distinction also explains how PILAR differs from lightweight feature-space detec -
tors. The objective is not to replace the feature extractor with a smaller representa -
tion, but to keep the anomaly-detection stage structurally simple. Feature-PILAR-FCN 
requires only the frozen extractor, the PILAR reconstruction heads, and score-normal -
ization constants at inference. It adds no nominal feature memory, nearest-neighbor 
index, density model, synthetic-anomaly discriminator, or separately trained student 
network. The local comparison uses the same ResNet18 and input size for PILAR, 
SimpleNet and GLASS, while retaining their different training objectives and budgets. 
SimpleNet and GLASS also avoid nearest-neighbor memory banks, but retain an adap -
tor and discriminator at inference. PILAR instead reconstructs normal feature maps 
and retains normalization statistics. These are architectural differences whose effects on 
accuracy, state and runtime require separate measurements; the compact head does not 
establish overall superiority.

6.2 Where the method is most useful
The evaluated application is ranking-assisted visual inspection. Images from a known 
product category can be processed in batches, and an operator reviews the highest-scor -
ing images within an available review budget. The same-device throughput results are 
relevant to this setting, whereas single-image latency is more relevant when each item 
requires an immediate decision. Automated rejection is a different operating require -
ment: it requires a threshold validated for the target defect prevalence and the costs of 
false acceptance and false rejection. Neither Image-AUROC nor Image-AP alone estab -
lishes that threshold.
The strongest current use case is industrial anomaly detection under a constrained 
compute budget. The RGB-only reconstruction-domain ablations represent low-cost 
versions of the model. They achieve good results on some MVTec classes, such as hazel -
nut, wood, screw, tile, and toothbrush, but their mean Image-AUROC of approximately 
0.696–0.697 indicates that direct RGB reconstruction is insufficient for a general MVTec 
detector. Feature-PILAR-FCN gives a better compromise for this scenario. It keeps 
the trainable anomaly model compact, with 1.55M PILAR parameters, and reaches 
0.969 mean Image-AUROC, 0.989 Image-AP , 0.965 mean Pixel-AUROC, and 0.926 
AUPRO@30 on MVTec AD.
The result is not state of the art in mean Image-AUROC, but the ranking and local -
ization metrics are useful complementary evidence. Feature-PILAR-FCN reaches 
98.86 ± 0.04% Image-AP and 92.63 ± 0.05% AUPRO@30 without a memory bank, near -
est-neighbor search, synthetic-anomaly discriminator, or flow-based density model. 
The local comparisons assess whether that reconstruction-only choice provides a useful 
accuracy–cost operating point; the absence of a memory bank alone does not distin -
guish it from SimpleNet or GLASS.
The review-budget curves in Figure 6 provide an operational interpretation of the 
image-level scores in the local comparison. T o retrieve 90% of anomalous images, PILAR 
requires reviewing 67.46 ± 0.25% of the test images, compared with 75.22 ± 1.62% for 
SimpleNet and 70.94 ± 1.40% for GLASS (Table 6). These results support ranking-
assisted review under the evaluated MVT ec AD test composition, but do not directly 
estimate the workload of a production inspection system.
The subtype-level review-budget analysis further identifies practical niches where 
Feature-PILAR-FCN may be more suitable for ranking-oriented screening. This suitabil -
ity should be judged using both the raw review fraction, which describes the manual-
review burden in the benchmark setting, and the normalized review efficiency, which 
reflects how effectively the model prioritizes a subtype relative to the subtype-specific 
oracle minimum and exact finite-sample random expectation. Several lead, insulation, 
and breakage subtypes reach the oracle minimum review fraction, indicating favorable 
ranking behavior in the evaluated score distribution. However, the method is not equally 
effective for all defect types, and a small raw review fraction can partly reflect a small 
subtype-specific anomalous set. Deployment decisions should therefore consider not 
only the category, but also the expected anomaly profile of the target inspection task.
The class-wise results also show where the method is more appropriate. For bottle, 
carpet, hazelnut, leather, metal nut, transistor, and wood, Feature-PILAR-FCN achieves 
Image-AUROC values of 0.990 or higher. The Pixel-AUROC and AUPRO@30 results are 
also strong for several categories, including leather, toothbrush, screw, capsule, zipper, 
Page 35 of 46
Fernandes Discover Artificial Intelligence (2026) 6:1829 
bottle, carpet, grid, and hazelnut. These results indicate that the method can be useful 
for selected industrial inspection tasks, especially when the application requires a com -
pact model and can tolerate the accuracy–cost trade-off. In this case, the objective is 
not to replace the most accurate detectors, but to provide a simpler alternative with a 
reduced inference state.
The CIFAR-10 and Fashion-MNIST results show another possible use of the proposed 
model. When a pretrained feature extractor can be used, but the scoring stage must 
remain simple, Feature-PILAR-FCN becomes more appropriate than the strict pixel-
reconstruction versions. Strict RGB PILAR and Strict grayscale PILAR are useful mainly 
when the model size must be extremely small. In the grayscale configuration, for exam -
ple, the stored weights require only about 15 KB.
When the use of a truncated ResNet18 is acceptable, Feature-PILAR-FCN improves 
the detection performance while keeping the anomaly head compact. The model 
requires about 12.5 MB of weights in the CIFAR-10 and Fashion-MNIST configuration, 
but it does not store prototypes, covariance matrices, support vectors, or nearest-neigh -
bor feature memories. On Fashion-MNIST, the AUC increases from 0.876 to 0.936, 
which is close to the DPAD result of 0.937. On CIFAR-10, Feature-PILAR-FCN reaches 
0.812 AUC. This value is higher than the DPAD average of 0.745, although it remains 
below the results reported by methods that use Outlier Exposure or transformation-
based training.
6.3 Where the method fails
On MVTec AD, some of the remaining errors are not caused by the absence of localized 
responses in the anomaly map. The screw class is the clearest example. Feature-PILAR-
FCN obtains 98.57 ± 0.05% Pixel-AUROC but 87.99 ± 0.84% Image-AUROC. This indi -
cates that the model often highlights regions related to the defect, but the image-level 
score can still overlap with the scores obtained for difficult normal samples. The prev -
alence-normalized subtype analysis confirms difficult cases in screw thread_side, 
screw manipulated_front, cable cable_swap and zipper fabric_interior 
(Table 7). These examples suggest that some errors involve part correspondence, subtle 
changes, alignment variation, and aggregation into a single image-level score; they do 
not establish these factors as causal explanations.
The RGB-only PILAR variants also indicate the limitation of direct image reconstruc -
tion. These models can be useful in simpler scenarios and in selected MVTec classes, 
but the results show that pixel reconstruction alone is not sufficient for broad industrial 
anomaly detection. The weakest cases include metal nut, cable, carpet, and transistor, 
where the abnormality is not well separated by the RGB residual. For this reason, Fea -
ture-PILAR-FCN is used as the main MVTec configuration. The RGB-only variants are 
better interpreted as minimal-cost baselines, showing how far the compact autoassocia -
tive head can go without using a pretrained feature representation.
On CIFAR-10, the main difficulty of Feature-PILAR-FCN occurs in classes with large 
semantic and visual variability. Cat is the weakest class, with a mean AUC of 0.649, and 
it also presents a pairwise inversion when frog is used as the anomaly class. Bird and 
dog also remain below the macro mean. These results indicate that the feature residual 
is able to capture part of the class-conditional appearance regularity, but it is still lim -
ited when the normal class has large internal variation or when anomalous classes are

visually similar. The confusions involving airplane–ship and truck–ship also show that 
background and global shape may affect the reconstruction residual.
On Fashion-MNIST, shirt is the main difficult class. Feature-PILAR-FCN improves the 
result for this class, but the overall Fashion-MNIST result is still below the GEO value of 
0.940 reported in the literature, although it is close to the DPAD result.
VisA-specific ranking and residual diagnostics are discussed with the corresponding 
results in Sect. 5.3.
6.4 Computational cost and deployment implications
Computational cost is relevant to continuous inspection, but parameter and operation 
counts do not predict backend execution time or energy consumption. The same-device 
measurements separate batch-1 latency from batch-16 throughput; neither quantity 
can be inferred from nominal GPU peak FP32 performance. Energy consumption was 
not measured. Model conversion and quantization, as studied by Arch-Net  [ 50], and 
architecture conversion through progressive volume distillation in PVD-AL  [ 51], offer 
complementary ways of reducing deployment cost. PILAR evaluates a compact detec -
tor directly; conversion and NeRF distillation are not treated as anomaly-detection 
baselines.
The CIFAR-10 and Fashion-MNIST Feature-PILAR-FCN configuration costs 0.984 
GFLOPs per image at 128 × 128 resolution. At 100,000 images per day, this is about 
9.84 × 1013 FLOPs per day, much higher than Strict grayscale PILAR but far lower than 
the 512 × 512 MVT ec Feature-PILAR-FCN configuration. This illustrates a practical scal-
ing rule: the PILAR head can remain small, but feature-map resolution and backbone 
choice dominate the computational component of deployment cost.
This discussion must be interpreted with some caution. In applications where false 
negatives have a high cost, such as safety-critical industrial inspection, the compact 
model should not be used as a replacement for a more accurate detector without addi -
tional validation. In these cases, PILAR is more appropriate as an initial screening stage, 
as a compact detector for selected classes, or as a mechanism to decide when a more 
expensive model should be applied. Therefore, the reduction in computational cost is 
useful only when the loss in accuracy is acceptable for the target application.
6.5 Interpretation and fairness of cross-method comparisons
The comparisons with external anomaly-detection methods should be interpreted 
according to the level of experimental control available for each quantity. Table 3 distin-
guishes literature-reported accuracy values from our measurements. The local experi -
ment evaluates ResNet18 PILAR, SimpleNet and GLASS at the same resolution and 
with common metrics. The compared methods retain different training objectives, syn -
thetic-anomaly policies and training budgets. Both local baselines use 160 epochs and a 
checkpoint fixed before test evaluation.
Within this context, the performance differences are important for defining the oper -
ating point of PILAR. Image-AUROC, Image-AP and localization metrics describe dif -
ferent aspects of the score distributions. The five-seed local results, retained state and 
same-device timing must therefore be considered together, without attributing all differ-
ences to architecture alone.
Page 37 of 46
Fernandes Discover Artificial Intelligence (2026) 6:1829 
Computational comparisons require a related but distinct qualification. Parameter 
count and FLOPs provide useful indicators of model scale and computational demand 
because they are less dependent on a particular execution platform than measured 
latency. However, different anomaly-detection architectures distribute their inference 
requirements differently. PatchCore, for example, stores nominal feature embeddings 
and performs nearest-neighbor search, while PaDiM stores feature-distribution sta -
tistics; other approaches rely on teacher-student, flow-based, or discriminative com -
ponents. Parameter count therefore provides a useful measure of model scale, while a 
broader deployment comparison should also consider FLOPs and method-specific 
inference state. In PILAR, the inference state consists of the network weights and score-
normalization constants, without an additional memory bank, nearest-neighbor index, 
density model, or discriminative scorer.
Measured runtime is even more implementation dependent. All inference timings use 
the same Apple MPS device, precision, batch sizes and CPU-input/CPU-output scope. 
Hardware architecture, software backend, kernel implementation, memory traffic, and 
operator efficiency can still affect execution time, so this comparison characterizes 
the evaluated implementation and device. It does not predict a cross-hardware speed 
ranking.
Taken together, Tables 3 and 20 position PILAR within an accuracy-cost space rather 
than defining a single ranking across methods. The reported results characterize the 
state, accuracy and execution trade-offs of the evaluated local configurations. The 
parameter, FLOP , inference-state, and hardware-specific runtime analyses should there-
fore be viewed as complementary dimensions for characterizing this operating point.
6.6 Limitations
The review-budget comparison uses local PILAR, SimpleNet and GLASS scores; other 
literature methods are outside that comparison. The MVTec AD category sizes and 
anomaly prevalence also differ substantially, and the test sets contain a higher frac -
tion of anomalous images than many production streams. For this reason, Recall@5%, 
Recall@10%, and the review fractions at high recall are benchmark-specific quanti -
ties. They describe rankings on the MVTec AD test sets and should not be transferred 
directly to real-world defect prevalence or used as a standalone estimate of manual-
inspection workload.
The subtype-level MVTec AD analysis has additional limitations. Raw subtype review 
fractions depend on subtype prevalence and sample count, because each subtype-spe -
cific evaluation contains all normal images from the corresponding category but only 
the anomalous images from that subtype. The lowest raw-review results involve sub -
types with only ten anomalous examples. Normalized review efficiency reduces this 
prevalence effect by comparing the observed review fraction with the oracle minimum 
and the exact finite-sample random-ranking expectation for that subtype, but it does 
not eliminate uncertainty caused by small subtype test sets or by the fixed MVTec AD 
test composition. The subtype analysis should therefore be interpreted as benchmark-
specific evidence, not as a production prevalence or workload guarantee. Finally, sub -
type-level review-budget curves are reported only for Feature-PILAR-FCN, because 
the subtype diagnostic is scoped to the proposed method; the common three-method 
screening comparison is category-level.

6.7 Scientific positioning
PILAR does not achieve state-of-the-art mean Image-AUROC on MVTec AD. However, 
its Image-AP and AUPRO@30 results show that the compact feature-reconstruction 
head is competitive in ranking and localization metrics. In this configuration, Feature-
PILAR-FCN reaches 96.91% Image-AUROC, 98.9% Image-AP , 96.49% Pixel-AUROC, 
and 92.63% AUPRO@30 on MVTec AD, using 4.33M total parameters and no memory 
bank. The local ResNet18 comparisons characterize this operating point while preserv -
ing the methods’ different training objectives and training costs. The same feature-
reconstruction principle also improves the results on other one-class image benchmarks, 
reaching 81.24% AUC on CIFAR-10 and 93.61% AUC on Fashion-MNIST. This indicates 
that the feature-reconstruction version is not limited to industrial defects.
For this reason, the comparison with the literature should consider the protocol used 
by each method. Normal-only models without a backbone, methods based on frozen 
pretrained features without auxiliary anomalies, and OE-assisted methods trained with 
external anomaly examples should be analyzed separately. The RGB-only PILAR variants 
remain useful as very low-cost alternatives for simpler screening tasks, but the feature-
space version is the main configuration when better detection performance is required.
7 Conclusion
This paper presented PILAR, a Pyramidal Inhibitory Lightweight Autoassociative 
Reconstruction model for cost-constrained visual anomaly detection. The model com -
bines a pyramidal autoassociative architecture, lateral inhibition, and reconstruction-
based anomaly scoring. The objective was to evaluate whether a compact reconstruction 
head can be useful in anomaly detection when the inference state and the computational 
cost are important constraints.
The main result was obtained with Feature-PILAR-FCN on MVTec AD. In this 
configuration, frozen ResNet18 layer 2 and layer 3 feature maps are reconstructed by 
compact PILAR heads. The model obtained 0.969 mean Image-AUROC, 0.989 Image-
AP , 0.965 Pixel-AUROC, and 0.926 AUPRO@30, using 4.33M total inference param -
eters, 17.32 MB of float32 weights, and 15.96 GFLOPs per image under the common 
weighted-operation scope. These values characterize the repeated industrial experiment; 
the local SimpleNet and GLASS comparison assesses the corresponding accuracy and 
inference costs, using final checkpoints and a common evaluation protocol. Therefore, 
Feature-PILAR-FCN occupies a useful accuracy–cost region and performs well on sev -
eral MVTec classes while avoiding memory banks, nearest-neighbor search, synthetic-
anomaly discriminators, and flow-based density estimators. Both Image-AUROC and 
Image-AP summarize rankings; the review-budget analysis provides the direct evidence 
for screening, while automated rejection still requires separate threshold validation. The 
MVTec AD review-budget analysis further shows that Feature-PILAR-FCN prioritizes 
anomalous images more effectively than the RGB-only PILAR variants, reducing the 
macro review fraction required for 90% anomaly recall from approximately 85% to 67%. 
The subtype-level review-budget analysis further shows that screening suitability var -
ies substantially across defect types, even after finite-sample normalization for subtype 
composition.

The unchanged industrial configuration reaches 92.53 ± 0.07% Image-AUROC and 
90.30 ± 0.14% AUPRO@30 on VisA, showing that performance differs across industrial 
datasets.
The experiments with CIFAR-10 and Fashion-MNIST also confirm the importance of 
the reconstruction domain. In CIFAR-10, the direct RGB version obtained 0.574 mean 
one-vs-rest AUC. When the reconstruction was moved to frozen feature maps, the result 
increased to 0.812 over five seeds, and no score-orientation inversion was observed. A 
similar behavior was obtained in Fashion-MNIST. Strict grayscale PILAR reached 0.876 
AUC, while Feature-PILAR-FCN reached 0.936. This value is close to the DPAD result, 
although the two methods follow different protocols.
The RGB-only variants gave a different view of the model. Their average result on 
MVT ec AD was lower, with approximately 0.696–0.697 macro Image-AUROC, showing 
that direct RGB reconstruction is not enough for general industrial anomaly detection. 
Even so, these variants were not uniformly poor. Depending on the spatial formulation, 
good class-specific results were obtained for hazelnut, wood, screw, tile, and toothbrush. 
These results characterize the RGB-only models as reconstruction-domain ablation 
baselines with category-dependent performance. For broader coverage, especially on 
industrial and natural images, the use of pretrained feature maps becomes necessary.
The conclusion is not that PILAR dominates modern anomaly detection methods. 
The main conclusion is that a compact autoassociative head with lateral inhibition can 
provide a practical low-state anomaly detector when it is combined with an appropri -
ate representation. Strict grayscale PILAR shows the smallest operating condition of the 
model, while Feature-PILAR-FCN shows that the same reconstruction principle can be 
extended to more difficult visual anomaly detection problems when the additional cost 
of a frozen backbone is acceptable.

Image-AUROC (percent) is measured against all normal images of the same category, then averaged across five seeds. 
Parentheses give distinct anomalous-image counts. A dash denotes an empty stratum; category coverage differs across 
columns
A stratum’s AUROC is the mean pairwise normal rank of its anomalous images, with 
half credit for ties. The normal-score reference is unchanged across a category’s size 
strata. Mask extent is measured relative to the whole evaluation image; category, subtype, 
appearance and annotation practice remain potential confounders.
For capsules, the residual maximum lies outside a four-pixel dilation of the annotated 
defect in 55.40 ± 3.91% of anomalous images; for macaroni2 the fraction is 44.60 ± 1.52%. 
For macaroni2, top-1% map pixels recover 73.16 ± 1.17% of ground-truth pixels, but only 
4.12 ± 0.09% of selected pixels lie inside the annotation. Low precision partly follows from 
tiny target regions and diffuse feature maps; it does not prove that every outside-mask 
response is spurious.
The post-hoc sensitivity analysis varies the aggregation fraction on 256 × 256 anomaly 
maps. At this resolution, top-1% aggregation yields 92.514% Image-AUROC on VisA, 
compared with 92.532% when scoring the 512 × 512 maps used in the main benchmark. 
T op-0.1% aggregation at 256 × 256 yields 91.998%. The main benchmark uses top-1% 
aggregation at 512 × 512 throughout.
