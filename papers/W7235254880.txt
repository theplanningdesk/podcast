Abstract
Accurate time series forecasting with exogenous inputs is critical across domains,
including energy and retail, yet modern deep learning models often overfit to corre-
lations that do not generalize under shifts in these inputs. We propose SLiDE, a
Koopman-inspired architecture for forecasting with exogenous inputs that imposes
structure on latent temporal dynamics. SLiDE approximates nonlinear system
evolution using a learned linear operator in a latent space, combining (i) a history
encoder that reconstructs the current latent state from past targets and inputs with
(ii) a shared linear rollout driven by future exogenous variables. By enforcing a
common linear recurrence across encoding and prediction, SLiDE aligns how past
information is encoded with how future states evolve, reducing parameterization
and preventing reliance on spurious correlations in the input history. Empirically,
SLiDE achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on real-world benchmarks, including
electricity price forecasting and retail demand, while maintaining a computational
footprint comparable to lightweight MLP architectures. SLiDE is especially ef-
fective at generalizing to shifted exogenous inputs, reducing MSE by 20% on
electricity price samples with out-of-range exogenous values and MAE by 30%
in a synthetic transfer setting where the same dynamical system is driven by an
unseen exogenous process. Ablations confirm the importance of both the structured
history encoder and the linear latent rollout, suggesting that structured latent linear
dynamics provide a useful inductive bias for forecasting with exogenous inputs.

and Watson, 2003], healthcare [Harutyunyan et al., 2019], transportation [Vlahogianni et al., 2014],

2022]. These inputs are often forecasted, noisy, or generated by changing external processes, making
robustness to shifted exogenous inputs important for deployment. Modern deep forecasting models
incorporate exogenous information through cross-attention [Wang et al., 2024], graph encoding [Li
et al., 2026], or MLP feature stacking [Das et al., 2023]. While effective, they typically rely on flexible

Koopman-inspired modeling provides a useful architectural bias. For autonomous nonlinear systems,
Koopman theory represents nonlinear evolution as linear dynamics over observables, i.e., scalar-
valued functions of the state. Learning nonlinear lifting with linear latent transport may be easier
to learn than a nonlinear latent transition directly. Recent applications such as Zhang et al. [2025]
suggest that this provides a suitable bias for the forecasting setting.
Motivated by this, we propose SLiDE (Structured Linear Dynamics with Exogenous inputs), a
lightweight Koopman-inspired architecture for multi-step forecasting with exogenous inputs. Rather
than implementing an exact finite-dimensional Koopman model, SLiDE uses learned latent linear
evolution as a practical forecasting bias. It reconstructs a forecast-start latent state from past targets
and inputs, then rolls it forward with future-known exogenous variables through two constraints: a
lag-invariant recurrent encoder and a shared linear rollout with additive exogenous forcing.
SLiDE achieves strong forecasting performance while remaining computationally efficient. Across
electricity price and retail demand benchmarks, it attains the best average rank with cost comparable
to lightweight MLP architectures. Its advantage is clearest under shifted exogenous inputs, including
out-of-range (OOR) values and unseen test-time input processes. Figure 1a shows shifted-input
examples from EPF-DE and Favorita. In EPF-DE, the future wind forecast falls outside the training
range, and SLiDE tracks the ground-truth price while the baseline deviates. In Favorita, the number

Our contributions are as follows: We introduce SLiDE, a lightweight architecture for multi-step
forecasting with exogenous inputs that replaces unconstrained feature fusion with a Koopman-inspired
latent recurrence. Its key constraints are a lag-invariant recurrent encoder for forecast-start state
reconstruction and a shared linear rollout with additive exogenous forcing. Empirically, SLiDE
achieves state-of-the-art performance across electricity price, retail demand, and nonlinear dynamical-
system benchmarks, with the largest gains under shifted exogenous inputs. The code is publicly
available.3
2 Related work
Deep learning has become a dominant paradigm for time series forecasting, and due to its practical
importance, incorporating exogenous variables is an active area of research. Approaches to forecasting
with exogenous variables include attention-based architectures such as TFT and TimeXer [Lim
et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2024], lightweight feedforward models such as TiDE, TSMixer, and N-
BEATSx [Das et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023, Olivares et al., 2023, Oreshkin et al., 2020], and
graph-based models such as GCGNet [Li et al., 2026]. In contrast to attention-, graph-, and MLP-
based approaches that primarily rely on flexible feature fusion, SLiDE imposes a constrained input-
driven latent dynamics model, which we show improves accuracy and efficiency while promoting
generalization under shifted input processes.
A related line of work studies structured latent models with inputs from the perspective of nonlinear
system identification and control, including Koopman-Subnet [Iacob et al., 2021], CT-Subnet [Bein-
tema et al., 2023], and MamKO [Li et al., 2025], the latter combining Koopman-style latent operators
with Mamba-based sequence modeling [Gu and Dao, 2024]. These models are related in spirit to our
work in that they impose structure on latent dynamics, but they are primarily motivated by modeling,
identification, or control rather than exogenous multi-step forecasting. SLiDE deviates from exact
Koopman theory where necessary, incorporating deliberate design choices that make the technique
specifically suitable for forecasting with known future exogenous inputs.
More broadly, SLiDE is related to Koopman-with-input works [Proctor et al., 2018, Korda and Mezi´c,
2018], Koopman-inspired forecasting models that do not incorporate exogenous variables [Zhang
et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2023], and structured state space models such as S4 and Mamba [Gu et al.,
2022, Gu and Dao, 2024]. Additional discussion of related work is provided in Appendix I.
3 Problem setting
We consider a discrete-time target series of real-valued observations yτ ∈ Rny . At forecast origin k,
we observe the past target trajectory y[k] = [y1, . . . , yk], where [k] denotes the index set [1, . . . , k],
and the past exogenous trajectory u[k] = [u1, . . . , uk] for an input uτ ∈ Rnu that affects the target
but is not affected by it. We also assume access to the values available at time k for the next H
forecast steps, denoted by uk,[H] = [uk,1, . . . , uk,H ]. The double index indicates forecast origin k
and lead time h ∈ [H]. These future exogenous values may themselves be forecasts and need not
coincide with the values that are later observed. For example, the value available at origin k for
lead time two, uk,2, may differ from both the realized value uk+2 and from a later updated forecast
uk+1,1.
The forecasting task is to generate a point estimate from the conditional distribution of the target
random variable Yk,[H] = [Yk,1, . . . , Yk,H ] = [Yk+1, . . . , Yk+H ]:

4 Background: Koopman theory
We model the observed time-series as a nonlinear system with state variable x ∈ Rnx at time index
τ, and a nonlinear transition map f : Rnx × Rnu → Rnx :
xτ +1 = f (xτ , uτ +1) , (2a)
yτ = o(xτ ) . (2b)
We assume that the model state is (in general) not fully observable. Instead, we observe an output
y ∈ Rny obtained from a nonlinear output map o : Rnx → Rny .
The Koopman operator framework [Koopman, 1931, Brunton et al., 2022] provides a means of
representing nonlinear dynamical systems as evolving linearly in an infinite-dimensional lifted state.
Let ϕ : Rnx → R be a scalar measurement function that is an element of an infinite-dimensional
Hilbert space H. For the corresponding zero-input autonomous system, define f0(x) := f (x, 0).
The linear infinite-dimensional Koopman operator K : H → H acts on the measurement functions
and satisfies
Kϕ = ϕ ◦ f0, ∀ϕ ∈ H , (3)
or equivalently Kϕ(xτ ) = ϕ(f0(xτ )). In practice, we approximate H by a finite dimensional
subspace Hnz ⊂ H with Hnz = span {ϕj }nz j=1 and associated approximate operator Knz .
When there are inputs u, it becomes more complicated to preserve the linearity inherent to the
Koopman framework. Following Iacob et al. [2024], we introduce the concept of a lifted state
z ∈ Rnz where zτ = Φ(xτ ) = [ϕ1(xτ ), . . . , ϕnz (xτ )] . We assume that Φ ◦ f0(·) ∈ span{Φ},
implying that there exists a matrix A ∈ Rnz ×nz such that
Φ ◦ f0 = AΦ. (4)
We also assume that our output map o can be exactly represented by a linear combination of the
observables Φ, so:
o(·) = CΦ(·) , with C ∈ Rny ×nz . (5)
While this can be achieved by including the output functions among the observables, requiring
their span to also be Koopman-invariant is generally restrictive in finite dimensions. Under these
assumptions, Iacob et al. [2025] show that there exists an exact finite-dimensional Koopman form
zτ +1 = Azτ + B(zτ , uτ +1)uτ +1 (6)
where A ∈ Rnz ×nz and B(zτ , uτ +1) is, in general, a nonlinear state- and input-dependent map.
SLiDE uses the input-dependent Koopman form as architectural motivation and adopts a practical
forecasting-oriented parameterization of the input effect.
5 Methodology
In SLiDE, a structured encoder maps past targets and exogenous inputs to a forecast-start latent state,
which a shared linear operator W rolls forward using additive forcing from future-known inputs (see
Figure 2). A nonlinear output map maps the latent trajectory back to the target space, and multiple
block forecasts are summed. We now proceed with the design principles, first motivating them based
on Koopman theory, and then introducing the important adjustments for forecasting success.

One challenge of implementing this model is determining the initial condition of the lifted state
zk. Iacob et al. [2025] introduce a reconstructability concept and discuss its relationship with
local observability. The observability map Λz,m can be used to compute the lifted state at the start
of a window from m consecutive output values and the corresponding inputs. Relying on this, a
reconstructability map Πz,k can be derived to determine the initial condition zk from k past (lagged)
outputs y[k] and the k − 1 inputs that follow the first observation, given sufficiently large k. We
provide the derivation in the context of our method in Appendix A.
In order to estimate this model in practice, we parameterize Aθ , Bθ , Cθ . We collect these parameters
in θ ∈ Θ ⊆ Rnθ . Since an analytical solution for Πz,k is impractical, we introduce a learned
approximation Πψ z,k with parameters ψ ∈ Ψ ⊆ Rnψ , which additionally receives u1 for alignment
(Appendix A). All parameters are learned jointly with the goal of minimizing a general risk function
R(yk,[H], ˆyk,[H]).
In practice, the future values uk,h available at forecast origin k allow us to estimate future lifted
states ˆzk,h using the same indexing. Our estimation approach for multi-step forecasting is then:
ˆzk = Πψ z,k(y[k], u[k]) , (8a)
ˆzk,h = Aθ ˆzk,h−1 + Bθ uk,h , with initialization ˆzk,0 := ˆzk , (8b)
ˆyk,h = Cθ ˆzk,h, h ∈ {1, . . . , H} . (8c)
5.2 Defining a structure on the reconstructability map
Beyond establishing its theoretical existence, prior work gives little guidance on the practical form of
Πz,k and typically treats it as a generic black-box encoder, implemented as a dense MLP learned
directly from data [Iacob et al., 2025]. For forecasting, this approach has a number of practical
challenges: a dense MLP ignores the sequential structure of the past trajectory, its parameterization
grows with the available context length, and its computational cost can dominate the relatively
lightweight latent rollout. We therefore impose additional structure on the learned encoder Πψ z,k,
introducing a forecasting-oriented inductive bias for reconstructing the forecast-start latent state:
5
Lag-invariant parameterization. We constrain Πψ z,k to have the same parameterization ψ ∈ Ψ ⊆
Rnψ for any positive k. The parameter search space Ψ is fixed as a design choice and does not
grow with the number of lagged values k. This prevents the model search space from exploding
and reduces model memory cost. Furthermore, it allows for a single ψ to be learned from training
samples with varying amounts of past data available.
Encoder Koopman update. The exact reconstructability map is recursively related to the latent
dynamics: reconstructing zk from history can be viewed, informally, as reconstructing an earlier
latent state and propagating it forward. Motivated by this structure, we impose a recurrent latent
update inside the encoder. In particular, we define
Πψ z,k(y[k], u[k]) = ˆzk (9a)
= Aψ ˆzk−1 + Bψ uk + Dψ yk (9b)
= Aψ Πψ z,k−1(y[k−1], u[k−1]) + Bψ uk + Dψ yk (9c)
for some linear matrix Aψ and MLPs Bψ and Dψ . This recurrence requires the encoder to learn not
just a final estimate ˆzk, but a trajectory of estimates that is updated with the same linear structure as
the underlying Koopman system. In this context, y is modeled as an additive input term alongside u,
helping to provide the information required to guide the trajectory of ˆz towards a final estimate ˆzk.
Parameter sharing. In order to limit the parameterization of our model and align the dynamics inside
the encoder with those used in the forecast horizon, we impose complete parameter sharing by setting
Aψ = Aθ and Bψ = Bθ . This imposes a strong heuristic for the encoder to follow the dynamics of
the Koopman model. However, the inclusion of the Dψ yk term allows for some differences, so that
the known lagged values y[k] can influence the reconstruction of ˆzk.
5.3 Model definition
Pre-processing: Patching. For long input histories or forecast horizons, we may optionally apply
non-overlapping patching to the target and input sequences, reducing the effective sequence length
while increasing the per-step target and input dimensions. The exact transformation is given in
Appendix B. After patching, we reuse the notation y and u for the transformed target and input
sequences.
Implementation of the Koopman operator. We implement the Koopman operator approximation
as a matrix Aθ = Aψ := W ∈ Rnz ×nz , with parameter sharing as imposed in Section 5.2. For the
input maps Bθ = Bψ and Dψ , we approximate the terms
Bθ uτ ≈ MLPu(uτ ) = pτ (10a)
Dψ yτ ≈ MLPy (yτ ) = dτ (10b)
The MLPs have much more representational ability than a fixed matrix but are less general than
B(zτ , uτ )uτ , since the MLP output does not vary with ˆzτ . In time series forecasting tasks, the
external input provided to the model may be noisy or uncertain. This choice provides a practical
compromise. By alleviating restrictions on the input term, we can better match the complexity of
real-world data. The resulting forecast rollout then becomes
ˆzk,h = W ˆzk,h−1 + pk,h, for h ∈ {1, . . . , H}. (11)
Definition of the output map. Although the idealized formulation uses ˆyk,h = Cθ ˆzk,h, we
implement the output map as the nonlinear function MLPˆy (ˆzk,h). This added expressivity helps
compensate for approximation error in the Koopman-inspired model.

Block structure. We impose a block structure on the entire forecasting model by decomposing
the lifted state into N non-interacting subspaces. Each SLiDE block b ∈ {1, . . . , N } is intended to
capture different dynamics of the target system. We consider our full lifted state z to be separable into
N sub-states zb ∈ Rnz,N where z = [z1⊤ , . . . , zN ⊤ ]⊤ and nz,N = nz N . Each subspace is initialized

which allows zb to evolve independently through time. A separate output MLP is used to obtain
ˆyb k,[H], with the block forecasts summed to obtain the final output: ˆyk,[H] = PN b=1 ˆyb k,[H].
6 Experimental results
In this section, we evaluate SLiDE along three axes: (1) Forecasting performance on real-world and
synthetic benchmarks; (2) Generalization under shifted exogenous inputs, including out-of-range
real-world samples and the Bouc–Wen transfer setting; and (3) Efficiency, assessing the tradeoff
between predictive performance and computational cost.
Benchmarks. We evaluate SLiDE on real-world datasets with heterogeneous covariates, including
future-known, past-only, and static variables. In SLiDE, future-known covariates are treated as
exogenous inputs u, past-only covariates are modeled jointly with the target history, and static
covariates are embedded and concatenated repeatedly to the input signal u.
The Electricity Price Forecasting (EPF) benchmark [Lago et al., 2021] consists of five datasets
from different electricity markets and is a standard benchmark for forecasting with future-known
covariates [Li et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2024, Olivares et al., 2023]. The Favorita sales dataset [Cook
et al., 2021] targets large-scale retail demand forecasting with promotions and holidays known in
advance. We additionally consider the synthetic Bouc-Wen hysteretic system [Schoukens and Noël,
2017, Noël and Schoukens, 2020], a nonlinear dynamical model with exogenous forcing. We use
it both as a controlled forecasting task and as a test of generalization under shifted input processes.
In the Multisine setting, models are trained and evaluated on a random-phase multisine forcing
trajectory. In the Transfer setting, models are trained on a sine-sweep forcing process and evaluated
on a multisine forcing process at test time. Appendix F.1 provides additional dataset details.
Metrics. Performance is measured by Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Squared Error (MSE)
for the EPF benchmark and the Bouc-Wen system. For the Favorita sales dataset, we report the Root
Mean Squared Logarithmic Error (RMSLE). To assess statistical significance, we perform pairwise
one-sided tests on forecast-error differences between models. We use the Diebold–Mariano test for all
datasets except Favorita, for which we use a Wilcoxon test on per-series error differences. Appendix H
provides additional details and bootstrapped confidence intervals. To compare computational cost,
we report the mean inference time and the trainable parameter count for each model.
Baselines. We compare SLiDE against two baseline groups: competitive forecasting architectures
that support exogenous information, and related Koopman- or system-identification-inspired models.
The forecasting baselines are TSMixer [Chen et al., 2023], TiDE [Das et al., 2023], TimeXer [Wang
et al., 2024], GCGNet [Li et al., 2026], and DLinear [Zeng et al., 2023] as a simple linear baseline.
We also include SKOLR [Zhang et al., 2025], a related Koopman-inspired model that cannot use
future-known covariates to show the performance lift from auxiliary covariates. To position SLiDE
relative to structured latent-dynamics approaches, we compare against Koopman-Subnet [Iacob et al.,
2021], CT-Subnet [Beintema et al., 2023], and MamKO [Li et al., 2025], which are conceptually
relevant Koopman-with-input or system-identification baselines, though not originally proposed for
the exogenous forecasting setup studied here. Appendix F.2 provides additional context.
Training Methodology. To ensure a fair comparison, we select the hyperparameters for all baselines
using the same random-search protocol with a fixed budget and patience. For each model, we select
the hyperparameter configuration that achieves the lowest validation loss. Each model is then trained
five times with different random seeds and the results are averaged. Additional details on the search
spaces and training settings are provided in Appendix F.2.

6.1 Key results
Accuracy. SLiDE is a consistent top performer on the real-world datasets, ranking among the
top two in eight out of eleven instances. On the synthetic Bouc-Wen benchmark, it achieves the
strongest overall performance across forecasting architectures in the multisine task and the strongest
performance overall in the transfer setting. While the related system identification and control
baselines achieve top performance on the Bouc-Wen multisine task, they are not competitive with
the forecasting architectures in general, achieving at best an average rank of 5.80. We report the
benchmark results in Table 1. Overall, SLiDE significantly outperforms each baseline multiple times
while being significantly outperformed at most twice (see Figure 3a).
Shifted Exogenous Inputs. SLiDE performs particularly well under shifts in the exogenous inputs.
On the real-world side, we study out-of-range (OOR) exogenous inputs, defined as test samples for
which at least one future-known exogenous value lies outside the range observed during training.
We focus our analysis on EPF-DE as it contains the largest number of such samples among the EPF
datasets. Figure 1b summarizes the aggregate results: on OOR EPF-DE samples, SLiDE reduces
MSE by 20% relative to the strongest baseline. We observe a similar pattern on Favorita, where we
observe a clear separation from the baselines on a small subset of OOR samples (see Appendix C).
On the synthetic side, we use the Bouc-Wen transfer setting, where the test-time input process differs
from training. In this setting, SLiDE reduces MAE by 30% relative to the next best model. These
results support the view that SLiDE generalizes better under shifted exogenous inputs.
Efficiency. We compare mean inference time against average benchmark rank in Figure 3b. SLiDE
has inference cost similar to TSMixer, a model designed for computational efficiency, while be-
ing considerably faster than TimeXer and GCGNet. Marker size reflects the number of trainable
parameters. Thus, SLiDE’s superior performance does not come at a substantial computational cost.

7 Conclusion and Limitations
In this work, we introduced SLiDE, a structured architecture for exogenous multi-step forecasting
inspired by Koopman-style latent linear modeling. SLiDE combines a structured lag-invariant
recurrent encoder for reconstructing the forecast-start latent state with a shared linear latent rollout
that propagates this state under additive exogenous forcing. Empirically, SLiDE achieves state-of-the-
9
art performance across real-world energy and retail benchmarks while remaining computationally
efficient. Its advantage is particularly clear when future exogenous inputs shift beyond the training
regime, where it outperforms the considered baselines on both real-world and synthetic tasks. Overall,
our results suggest that carefully structured latent-state estimation together with a shared linear latent
rollout is an effective design principle for forecasting with exogenous inputs.
Limitations. Our focus is on forecasting accuracy together with computational and memory effi-
ciency. SLiDE inherits the strengths but also the modeling assumptions of Koopman-style latent
representations. Its suitability may depend on how well a forecasting problem can be described by an
approximately linear latent dynamics model. This could be more restrictive for systems with richer
latent structure, for example, those not well captured by low-dimensional Koopman approximations
or involving continuous spectra, which we do not study systematically here. As exogenous inputs
directly influence the latent rollout, missing, noisy, or misaligned inputs affect forecast quality. Our
experiments with missing or noisy inputs (see Appendix D.3) suggest that SLiDE is relatively robust
compared to the considered baselines. Moreover, we only evaluate a limited set of shifted-input
scenarios, namely out-of-range covariates with a limited number of samples per dataset and a syn-
thetic transfer setting. Extending the analysis to a wider range of covariate shifts and exploring more
adaptive latent operators, for example, context-dependent variants as in Li et al. [2025], are valuable
directions for future work.

B.1 Characterizing the departure from the exact Koopman form
The exact Koopman form has a state- and input-dependent input map
zτ +1 = Azτ + B(zτ , uτ +1)uτ +1. (21)
However, as detailed in Iacob et al. [2025], it may be desirable to trade accuracy for simplicity of the
model, e.g. by choosing a suitable form of the input function B in Equation (7a). In our case, we do
not only choose the input map to be state-independent, but instead of explicitly parameterizing the
matrix-valued map B, our implementation directly parameterizes the resulting whole input forcing
term as MLPu(u) ∈ Rnz . The following characterizes what this input-decoupled model retains
relative to the exact form.
The input-decoupled form is exact if there exists a finite-dimensional lifting Φ for which the effect of
the input on the lifted dynamics is independent of the lifted state
Φ(f (x, u)) = AΦ(x) + g(u). (22)
This structure is analogous to a Hammerstein model in the lifted coordinates, where a static nonlinear
transformation of the input drives otherwise linear latent dynamics. If, instead, the exact lifted
input forcing depends on both z and u, as permitted by the general Koopman form B(z, u)u, the
state-independent forcing g(u) constitutes an approximation.
When the input-decoupled form is not exact, we can characterize the error induced specifically by
removing the latent-state dependence from the input forcing. Let B(z, u)u denote the exact lifted
forcing and let g(u) denote its state-independent approximation. We assume that their mismatch is
uniformly bounded over the relevant domain D of lifted states and inputs:

B.2 Patching
For time series with a long history k and forecast horizon H, we may optionally pre-process the target
time series by patching together consecutive observations. We define a patch length P to transform
the target time series to have k′ = k/P past values and H ′ = H/P future values. For ypatch τ ∈ RP ·ny
and τ ∈ {1, . . . , k′ + H ′}, we define:
ypatch τ ≜ [yP (τ −1)+1, . . . , yP τ ]⊤ . (38)
Within each patch, the consecutive time steps and channels of y are flattened into a space RP ·ny .
This removes the sequential relationship of consecutive observations within the patch, treating it
instead as simultaneous observations of a higher-dimensional variable. The range of defined time
indices is reduced by a factor of P . This same patching mechanism is then analogously applied for
the input sequence u.
We begin by reframing our target time series from y ∈ Rny to ypatch ∈ RP ·ny following the patching
process described in Equation (38). The transformed target variable is equivalent to some other target
y′, with a different forecast horizon H ′ = H/P and dimension n′ y = P · ny . Our model treats ypatch
as the variable of interest for forecasting, and we can subsequently obtain y by inverting the patching
process.

D.2.2 Explicit block diversity
While Figure 7 suggests that different blocks learn distinct temporal patterns, Figure 9 shows that their
eigenvalue spectra largely overlap. To investigate whether explicitly enforcing diversity improves
performance, we added a diversity loss that penalizes pairwise squared cosine similarity between block
operators under the Frobenius inner product, following the cosine-similarity diversity regularization
of Wortsman et al. [2021]. We evaluated weights w=1 and w=10 across N ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8}.
The effect on performance is negligible, with the mean relative performance change across dataset–
metric pairs ranging from −0.36% to +0.02% across the tested configurations. Thus, explicitly
encouraging block diversity does not yield systematic performance improvements in the considered
experiments.

D.3.1 Missing exogenous inputs

D.3.2 Noisy future exogenous inputs
We note that our real-world benchmarks already contain errors in future exogenous inputs by con-
struction: the future-known covariates in all five EPF datasets are themselves day-ahead forecasts,
not realized values. The EPF results therefore already evaluate the models using future exogenous
variables that contain realistic forecast uncertainty.
To additionally characterize robustness in a controlled way, we ran an input corruption experiment.
We perturb only the future exogenous inputs in the horizon with Gaussian noise (standard deviation
scaled to 50% of each input’s standard deviation), keeping past values unperturbed. Each model is
re-trained with its original hyperparameters on unperturbed past and noisy future inputs. We omit
Favorita from this experiment since its future-known covariates (holidays, promotions, calendar
features) are deterministically known in advance, so corrupting them would not correspond to a
realistic uncertainty scenario.
SLiDE maintains its top average rank under this noisy regime. Notably, on Bouc–Wen Multisine,
SLiDE now outperforms Koopman-Subnet and CT-Subnet, which outperform SLiDE on the task
without noise addition but show particularly large relative degradation when the future forcing is
corrupted. Results are reported in Table 7.

Bouc-Wen Hysteretic System. The Bouc-Wen benchmark [Schoukens and Noël, 2017, Noël and
Schoukens, 2020] provides two noiseless datasets, originally intended for system identification: (a) a
random phase multisine input dataset containing a steady state period of 8192 timesteps and (b) a
sine-sweep input dataset in which the sine input signal increases frequency in a frequency band from
20 to 50 Hz. This dataset contains about 153,000 timesteps.
We use this benchmark as a controlled test bed for forecasting with exogenous inputs and define the
following two forecasting tasks:
1. Multisine: We employ a 70%/10%/20% split on dataset (a) and extract samples using a
stride (step size) of 1.
2. Transfer: We train on the sinesweep dataset (b) of which we hold out the last 15% as a
validation set and evaluate on the multisine dataset (a). Samples are extracted using a stride
of 24. This setting is designed to test generalization under a shift in the exogenous forcing
process.
For both tasks, we optimize the mean absolute error loss, use a context length of 288 steps, and a
forecast horizon of 144 steps. Both datasets are standardized using mean and standard deviation
statistics computed from the respective training sets, and performance is reported in standardized
units.

F.2 Additional information on baselines and training methodology
We consider two groups of baselines: competitive forecasting architectures with exogenous-variable
support, and related structured latent-dynamics baselines. For each baseline, we summarize the
model class, the implementation used in our experiments, and any adaptations required to match our
covariate setting.
TimeXer. The TimeXer model [Wang et al., 2024] is a Transformer-based architecture that is specifi-
cally designed for forecasting with exogenous variables. As the original implementation assumes
that endogenous and exogenous sequences have the same length, it cannot directly accommodate
future-known covariates beyond the historical context. We therefore use the implementation from Li
et al. [2026], which modifies the exogenous-input embedding to support future-known covariates in
the forecasting setting studied here. However, this version is not able to utilize past-only covariates
without further modifications, which only affects the oil price variable in the Favorita task.

TSMixer. The Time-Series Mixer (TSMixer) [Chen et al., 2023] is an MLP-based architecture
built around mixing operations along the time and feature dimensions. We use the implementation
from Neuralforecast [Olivares et al., 2022] to run our experiments. TSMixer natively incorporates

GCGNet. The Graph-Consistent Generative Network (GCGNet) [Li et al., 2026] is a recently
proposed graph-based time series forecasting model that is specifically designed for forecasting tasks
with exogenous variables. We use the original implementation to run baseline experiments. On the
Favorita dataset, GCGNet exhibited unstable (internal) variance estimates; we therefore clamp these
values to a bounded interval in order to stabilize training on this dataset.
TiDE. Time-series Dense Encoder (TiDE) [Das et al., 2023] is an MLP-based encoder-decoder
architecture. We use the implementation from Neuralforecast [Olivares et al., 2022] to run our
experiments. TiDE natively incorporates future-known, past-only and static covariates.
SKOLR. Structured Koopman Operator Linear Recurrent Neural Network (SKOLR) [Zhang et al.,
2025] is a Koopman-based architecture that is structurally related to our approach. However, it is not
able to incorporate future-known exogenous covariates. We use the original implementation and treat
the past-only and the past portion of future-known covariates as additional channels of the input and
run SKOLR in the non-channel-independent mode.
DLinear. DLinear [Zeng et al., 2023] is a simple linear architecture that includes a seasonal
decomposition. We use the implementation from DARTS [Herzen et al., 2022], which natively
incorporates past-only, future-known and static covariates.
Koopman-Subnet. Koopman-Subnet [Iacob et al., 2021] is a neural Koopman-with-input architecture
originally developed for nonlinear system identification. It learns a lifted latent representation
together with a generic MLP-based encoder and a state-dependent input map. We use the original
implementation and adapt the inputs to our forecasting setting. Relative to SLiDE, Koopman-Subnet
uses a more generic MLP encoder and a state-dependent input map,
CT-Subnet. CT-Subnet [Beintema et al., 2023] is an architecture developed from a continuous-time
system-identification perspective. We include it because it is structurally close to our setting in that it
learns a latent state from observed history, but it differs from SLiDE in several key respects: it uses a
generic MLP encoder, models nonlinear latent dynamics rather than a shared linear latent transport
operator, and is motivated by continuous-time identification rather than discrete-time exogenous
forecasting. We evaluate CT-Subnet using its original implementation.
MamKO. MamKO [Li et al., 2025] is an adaptive Koopman-style architecture inspired by Mamba [Gu
and Dao, 2024] and designed for modeling and control. Unlike SLiDE, which keeps the latent
transition fixed and uses the full history to reconstruct a forecast-start latent state, MamKO uses
historical context to generate operator-related components while obtaining the initial latent state
from only the last target value through an MLP. We include MamKO as a conceptually relevant
baseline because it represents an alternative design in which context modulates the latent operator
itself rather than improving latent-state estimation at rollout start. We evaluate MamKO using its
original implementation. In the original MamKO formulation, the input at time t drives the state
transition from t to t + 1. We additionally evaluate the model with the complete exogenous input
sequence shifted forward by one time step. This aligns the forecast covariates with the predicted
timestamps and includes the final forecast covariate row in the rollout. Changes that noticeably exceed
the reported standard deviation across seeds are improvements in EPEX BE MAE and MSE and
EPEX FR MAE, and a further deterioration on the Bouc–Wen transfer task. The overall conclusions
and discussions of MamKO remain qualitatively unchanged. We do not evaluate shifted Subnet
variants as these architectures support direct feedthrough from ut to yt.

Categorical static embedding. To allow the use of categorical static variables, we adopt the same
learnable static embedding scheme for all baselines. For the baselines that do not natively incorporate
static variables (TimeXer, GCGNet, SKOLR), we provide an optional mechanism that repeats the
embedding and concatenates it to the inputs at each time step. We treat the inclusion of static
information as a binary hyperparameter during tuning, allowing these models to leverage the static
features if beneficial without forcing a non-native design choice.
Instance stationarization. Differences in instance stationarization can substantially influence
forecasting accuracy [Kim et al., 2022]. To isolate the contribution of each method, we fix the
instance stationarization protocol across all baselines for a given dataset, choosing the setting that
performed best across baseline models. In particular, we apply instance stationarization to both
endogenous and exogenous inputs for the EPF-BE and EPF-FR datasets, while applying it only
to the endogenous input signal for the remaining EPF datasets and Favorita. We do not apply
any instance stationarization to the Bouc-Wen tasks. An exception is Koopman-Subnet, which
occasionally diverged at test time, without additional stationarization,in some settings. For this model,
we also apply instance stationarization to exogenous inputs on EPF-DE, and to both endogenous and
exogenous inputs on the Bouc–Wen transfer task.
Hyperparameter optimization. For all baseline models, we define the hyperparameter search
spaces based on the ranges reported in the original papers and list them for the EPF datasets in
Table 10. We use a random search with a fixed budget of 30 trials per model and dataset. Each
configuration is evaluated on the validation set using early stopping with a patience of five epochs,
and the configuration with the best validation performance is selected as the final model setting. The
final baseline metrics are reported as averages over five independent runs with different random seeds.
For the ablation experiments, we use a smaller random-search budget of 8 trials centered around the
best configuration of the base model.

G Performance of Koopman/system-identification baselines across
benchmarks
We observe that Koopman-Subnet, CT-Subnet, and MamKO perform most favorably in settings
that closely resemble classical system identification, with compact dynamics and directly observed
forcing signals. This is visible in the Bouc–Wen experiments, where the methods perform strongly on
Multisine, but degrade substantially under the distribution shift of the Transfer task and when future
exogenous inputs are perturbed.
To test whether this trend extends beyond the original benchmarks, we additionally evaluate on the
real-world UCI Bike Sharing dataset [Fanaee-T, 2013] and a synthetic Duffing oscillator driven by
band-limited Gaussian forcing.

H Statistical evaluation
Statistical Test Setup. We assess statistical significance using one-sided pairwise tests. For all
datasets except Favorita, we apply the one-sided Diebold–Mariano (DM) test on the time series of
error differences per sample averaged over seeds. We set the truncation of the variance estimate to
the horizon divided by the step size between test samples. We implemented the DM test in Python
following the implementation of the R forecast package [Hyndman and Khandakar, 2008]. As each
Favorita test sample corresponds to a different product group predicted over the same time range,
we instead use a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-product-group error differences, again
averaged over seeds. We deem results significant at p < 0.05.
Bootstrap Confidence Intervals. In addition, we report 95% confidence intervals of the loss
differential (again averaged over the five seeds) obtained via stationary bootstrap applying automatic
block-length selection depending on autocorrelation in Table 12. The bootstrap confidence intervals
show that SLiDE’s advantage is broad but not uniform, with the strongest and most statistically stable
gains appearing on EPEX-DE and especially on the Bouc–Wen transfer task.

I Extended related work
Deep Learning for forecasting with exogenous variables. Deep learning has become an important
paradigm for time series forecasting, and explicitly incorporating exogenous variables to capture ex-
ternal drivers of temporal dynamics into neural forecasters remains an active research area. DeepAR
represents an early recurrent approach that conditions probabilistic forecasts on past observations
together with covariates [Salinas et al., 2020]. Subsequent work has adopted transformer-based archi-
tectures for forecasting with exogenous information. The Temporal Fusion Transformer [Lim et al.,
2021] introduced attention and gating mechanisms together with explicit handling of static and time-
varying covariates. The TimeXer architecture [Wang et al., 2024] further explored transformer-based
forecasting and includes covariates with a cross-attention mechanism. A parallel line of work explored
30
simpler feedforward designs that remain competitive for forecasting with exogenous variables. The
Time-series Dense Encoder (TiDE) [Das et al., 2023] adopts a lightweight encoder–decoder formula-
tion. TSMixer [Chen et al., 2023] is another all-MLP architecture that uses mixing operations across
time and channels. N-BEATSx [Olivares et al., 2023] extends the N-BEATS architecture [Oreshkin
et al., 2020] to forecasting with exogenous covariates. Recently, GCGNet [Li et al., 2026] has been
proposed as a generative, graph-based model that explicitly targets the future-known covariate setting.
In contrast to attention-, graph-, and MLP-based approaches that primarily rely on flexible feature
fusion, SLiDE imposes a constrained input-driven latent dynamics model, which we show improves
accuracy and efficiency while promoting generalization under shifted input processes.
Koopman with inputs and controls. The Koopman operator is extensively used to describe
dynamical systems. Some works within this community consider additional inputs, such as (Extended)
Dynamic Mode Decomposition with control (DMDc/EDMDc) [Proctor et al., 2016, Korda and Mezi´c,
2018] and Koopman-with-input formulations [Proctor et al., 2018, Kaiser et al., 2021]. These methods
were developed primarily for system identification and control, where inputs are typically treated
as observed, externally specified control or forcing signals and, in control applications, as directly
manipulable. In contrast, forecasting applications involve exogenous variables that act as external
drivers rather than controls and are often partially observed. Beyond classical approaches with hand-
chosen lifting functions, a substantial body of work has studied learning Koopman observables directly
from data using neural networks. Takeishi et al. [2017] and Yeung et al. [2019] both learn nonlinear
lifting functions and then fit linear dynamics in the lifted space. The first is more explicitly motivated
by learning invariant subspaces for DMD and spectral analysis, while the second is framed as deep
dictionary learning to avoid manually designing EDMD observables. Related deep operator learning
approaches have also been proposed in molecular kinetics, including time-lagged autoencoders
and VAMPnets [Mardt et al., 2018, Wehmeyer and Noé, 2018]. Lusch et al. [2018] developed
parsimonious, interpretable neural representations of Koopman eigenfunctions by embedding the
dynamics on a low-dimensional manifold using an autoencoder architecture. A central difficulty
is constructing or learning suitable lifted observables in the presence of inputs, particularly while
preserving causal structure [Selby and Asada, 2021]. Recent neural system-identification approaches
with inputs, such as Koopman-Subnet [Iacob et al., 2021] and CT-Subnet [Beintema et al., 2023],
show that structured latent models can be effective for nonlinear dynamics. Adaptive Koopman-style
architectures such as MamKO [Li et al., 2025] use historical context to modulate latent operators,
combining Koopman-inspired modeling with Mamba [Gu and Dao, 2024]. However, these methods
are primarily motivated by system identification or control rather than multi-step forecasting with
exogenous variables. Our work builds on Koopman-with-input formulations by shifting the emphasis
from lifting construction to forecasting architecture. Treating the lift as a learnable component lets
us focus on two key design questions for exogenous forecasting: how to reconstruct a forecast-start
latent state from past context, and how to roll that state forward under future-known exogenous
drivers. This yields a structured forecasting architecture that turns Koopman-inspired latent modeling
into a practical multi-step prediction design.
Koopman-inspired forecasting and structured linear sequence models. Recent work has adapted
the ideas from Koopman theory of learning a latent space in which nonlinear dynamics evolve approx-
imately linearly to forecasting settings. Autoencoder architectures have been proposed to learn latent
representations for Koopman-inspired sequential data forecasting [Azencot et al., 2020]. Spectral
approaches have likewise been explored for forecasting quasi-periodic dynamical systems [Lange
et al., 2021]. The Koopman Neural Forecaster [Wang et al., 2023] is a Koopman-based architecture
designed for time series that exhibit temporal distribution shifts. Koopa [Liu et al., 2023] addresses
nonstationarity by decomposing time series into time-varying and time-invariant components. In
parallel, renewed interest in linear recurrent dynamics provides a complementary perspective. Struc-
tured linear RNN models can match or exceed more complex recurrent baselines on long-context
problems [Orvieto et al., 2023], while SKOLR [Zhang et al., 2025] provided an explicit connection
between a linear recurrent architecture and Koopman theory. More broadly, state-space models such
as S4 [Gu et al., 2022] and Mamba [Gu and Dao, 2024] show that structured latent linear dynamics
can serve as highly effective sequence backbones. Hybrid approaches such as MamKO [Li et al.,
2025] further connect this state-space modeling perspective with Koopman-style latent operators.
Unlike such general-purpose or control-oriented sequence models, SLiDE is designed specifically for
exogenous multi-step forecasting. In particular, the Koopman forecasting and structured sequence
models considered above do not jointly address the setting in which heterogeneous future-known
exogenous covariates explicitly drive the latent dynamics throughout the forecast horizon.
