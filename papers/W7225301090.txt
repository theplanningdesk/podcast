1. Introduction
Supply chains are complex, integrated and syn -
chronised processes that address the acquisition, 
transformation and distribution of materials, often in-
cluding concurrent processes and/or subcontracting. 
One of the most impacting aspects on a Supply Chain 
(SC) pertains to supplier selection and order alloca -
tion since it involves a wide range of variables with 
a multitude of combinations that must be optimized 
to attain competitive advantages over competitors [1]. 
This study introduces an innovative framework for order allocation and scheduling in the 
textile industry, addressing the complex challenges of multi-objective production planning 
across diverse processes, multiple outsourcing entities, and interconnected production de-
partments. The proposed approach uses advanced genetic algorithms to deliver efficient and 
sustainable scheduling solutions, integrating a state-of-the-art production process simulator. 
A core innovation of this research is a novel chromosome representation, complemented 
by refined genetic operators and a heuristic pruning and selection mechanism. Green sup -
plier criteria are integrated through a lexicographic scoring mechanism that governs alloca -
tion tie-breaking, ensuring that sustainability preferences are structurally embedded in the 
decision process without displacing primary economic objectives. The production process 
simulator is designed to replicate real-world industrial dynamics, significantly boosting the 
model’s accuracy and practical applicability. The study pioneers advanced methodologies, 
including a gendered genetic algorithm and a hybrid genetic algorithm, specifically devel -
oped to optimize scheduling in multi-supply chain and open shop environments. These tech-
niques improve computational efficiency and promote sustainable practices by optimizing 
resources, reducing waste and minimizing production costs. Validation using real industrial 
data underscores the model’s capability to outperform conventional practices, demonstrating 
higher performance relative to the company's prior manual scheduling practice, mostly in 
terms of operational efficiency, cost-effectiveness and sustainability. The framework offers 
substantial potential for practical implementation, providing robust tools to enhance market 
competitiveness and support greener and resilient production strategies within the textile 
industry and beyond.

2. Literature Review 
Today’s competitive environment has led indus -
try to diversify their sources of material and services 
while pursuing low cost, high quality, flexibility and 
specialized manufacturers in a global and increas -
ingly competitive market [8]. Outsourcing has be-
come the modus operandi for many manufacturing 
companies, from textile to automotive industry, since 
it provides flexibility, improved overheads, cost re -
duction and access to highly specialized services and 
technologies. 
2.1 Supplier Selection and Order Allocation
The selection of suppliers is a complex decision-
making process that involves qualitative and quanti -
tative criteria in a broad multi-criteria scenario [9]. 
Traditionally supplier selection evolved from tradi-
tional criteria e.g., cost, quality, lead time, response 
rate, to cumulative green criteria e.g., eco transporta-
tion, packing, management, environment and social 
impact [10], [11]. Among all criteria cost, quality and 
delivery time are the most prevalent [5], [11], [12] and 
there is registered patterns pertaining to the increased 
use of multi-criteria approaches [13]-[16]. All these 
criteria are often underpinned as being the most im-
portant factors but there are other aspects that may 
interfere and sometimes condition the overall choice 
of suppliers, such as supplier availability or response 
capacity. The latest aspects may interfere in schedul-
ing, handling or inventory costs, leading to changes 
in suppliers’ selection to comply with delivery times. 
Supplier selection is considered a crucial factor 
in purchasing or subcontracting of services since it 
might contribute to the reduction in operational costs 
and improve thus total SC performance [11], [17]. 
An acceptable cost or quality are no longer enough 
to determine appropriate suppliers [11], other fac-
tors such as lead time, responsiveness, warranties, ca-
pacity, among others, have to be taken into account 
[18]. Further, diversification as well as specialization 
of suppliers demands that an increased number of 
small batches have to be allocated to different sup -
pliers, hence increasing the complexity of the SC 
[19]. Since outsourcing has become widely adopted, 
governance mechanisms must be improved as well 
as the selection of suppliers and the prioritization in 
allocations, hence requiring improved frameworks to 
support such intertwined and time-consuming job. 
Some research from the literature, shows different 
frameworks integrating supplier choices and order -
ing decisions under uncertainty. The study in [20] 
provide a broad review of order allocation models 
and solutions to improve SC processes. Models were 
systematically classified by decision criteria (e.g. cost, 
quality), supplier characteristics and SC structure, 
highlighting the gaps and research opportunities.

2.2 Scheduling and Optimization Approaches
SC optimization can be categorized according to 
nine main categories: SC network, facility location, 
supply planning, production planning, distribution 
planning, inventory planning, capacity planning, lot 
sizing and supplier selection [3]. Some of these topics 
are studied on an isolated way like: capacity planning 
[22], SC network [23], [24], and the facility location 
[25]. However, the trend and the majority of the stud-
ies are focused on integrated approaches such as: the 
integration of production, distribution and inventory 
planning [26], the integration of lot sizing and suppli-
er selection and the integration of supply and distri-
bution planning [27]. Almost every decision made in 
SCM is affected by supplier evaluation and selection 
[28]-[30]. Besides the supplier selection, the lot sizing 
problem [31], which deals with sourcing decisions, is 
also among the most important challenges that orga -
nizations are facing, and actually, some researchers 
tried to combine the supplier selection and order al-
location problems to align and analyse the available 
strategies [32]-[35]. 
A common optimization problem is finding the 
optimal selling price and lot-size of multiple prod-
ucts in multiple periods while selecting the best 
suppliers. This problem faces several constraints, 
namely, the budget limitation, storage and supplier 
capacity [36], [37].
The problem with scheduling, rescheduling and 
supplier selection is thus a multi-objective problem 
that should be handled in a dynamic way, considering 
that the realistic SCs are not immune to disruption. 
There are few combined research addressing simul -
taneously outsourcing and disruption and indeed this 
is quite common in industrial operations where a 
large number of suppliers are involved giving place 
to long and complex SCs [38]-[40]. Most studies ad-
dress machine rescheduling or otherwise outsourcing 
selection ignoring most often that these two problems 
gain complexity when dealt in conjunction [38]. Re-
cent work has further reinforced this challenge: inte-
grated formulations combining sustainable supplier 
selection, order allocation, and production schedul -
ing under supply disruption have been shown to sig -
nificantly outperform isolated approaches [41], while 
rescheduling frameworks capable of accommodating 
new order arrivals in real time – whether through 
bounded-disruption algorithms [42] or digital twin-
enhanced Genetic Algorithms (GA) [43] – represent 
the current frontier of dynamic scheduling research. 
Further, green supplier selections add a significant 
number of constraints that dynamically affect SCs 
and thus limit the use of traditional optimization ap-
proaches.
Optimization techniques have been used to op -
erate SC processes effectively handling complexity 
while integrating procurement, production, distribu-
tion and demand planning [3]. There are many op-
timization techniques used in SC processes such as 
Linear Programming [24], [44]-[46], Multi-Objective 
Programming [23], [47], Stochastic Programming 
[48] and Heuristics [25]-[27], [49]. Each category has 
techniques associated with it, and some of the most 
common [18], [19] are: the Analytic Hierarchy Pro -
cess [11], [50]-[52], Technique for Order Preference 
by Similarity to Ideal Solution [11], [15], [53]-[55], 
Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory 
[16], [18], [27]. 
Further, new research reinforces the use of search 
techniques, such as the use of quantum annealing, 
for larger problems compared to classical meta -
heuristics, since parameter selection complexities 
urge further exploration [56], [57]. These approach -
es have proved useful in a wide variety of problems, 
although their applicability is often hindered by the 
non-deterministic nature. The majority of the deci-
sions in SCM belong to the class of non-deterministic 
problems [58], and thus heuristic methods have been 
applied to improve decisions. These methods define 
a completely new approach on optimization and are 
characterized by their search algorithm for poten -
tial solutions while searching for the overall optimal 
one, testing and evaluating solutions along the way,

further supporting the appropriateness of simulation-

In dynamic market conditions and manufactur -
ing contexts, customer requirements, operational 
resources, and supply channels are prone to swift 
alterations, thereby requiring the implementation of 
agile and adaptive methodologies. Proficient SCM 
serves a crucial function in mitigating these variances, 
empowering organizations to react expeditiously to 
changing consumer preferences and market dynam -
ics [60]-[62].
A realistic SCM is normally a multi-objective prob-
lem and as such, it points to a Pareto optimal solution 
set being hardly attainable the entire Pareto set ex -
cept for the simplest cases. The use of GA is in these 
cases extremely useful since it can be easily adapted 
to find optimum multi-objective solutions [63], [64] 
and, thus produce multiple Pareto optimum solu -
tions. GA can simultaneously search solutions in dif-
ferent parts of the solution space and hence handle 
different objectives in a single run and explore the 
Pareto frontiers without requiring limiting or scaling 
objectives. GA have been used in a multitude of opti-
mization problems [65]-[68] and results demonstrate 
their ability and flexibility in handling complex prob-
lems without the numerous limitations of traditional 
techniques [69]. 
Authors in [70] propose an optimization model 
that considers supplier selection, inventory decisions 
and inbound logistics under stochastic demand. GA 
are used to select suppliers, whereas, analytic formu-
las are described for base-stock inventory levels, with 
discrete-event simulation updating lead times under 
supply disruption. The case-based simulations have 
shown that selection criteria weights should depend 
on item characteristics and that mitigation strategies’ 
effectiveness varies with disruption patterns.
Further, dynamic scheduling is fundamental since 
it can manage uncertainties by making real-time deci-
sions, adapting to variable conditions, enhancing the 
ability to both optimize economic and environmental 
outcomes, while contributing to a more sustainable 
environment [71]-[73]. [74] presented a two-stage 
Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) model 
for supplier selection and order allocation under dis-
ruption risks. The model includes both mean and 
Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) in the objective 
(a Mean–CVaR approach) to reflect a risk-averse 
decision-maker. Therefore, this robust-optimization 
framework allows for ambiguous probability distribu-
tions of supplier disruptions, linking supplier selec -
tion with order quantities, giving managers a way to 
deal with supply disruptions. 
Authors in [75] develop a robust SC design mixed 
integer linear programming (MILP) model that intro-
duce new integrator “risk measures” and apply ro -
bust optimization to account for uncertainty. Tested 
on randomly generated data and a real European 
aerospace case, the model helps quantify the trade-
off between integrator benefits and their potential 
failure costs. 
This study’s key methodological contribution is 
embedding integrators as decision variables in sup -
plier selection, with a robust formulation to guide 
original equipment manufacturers’ tier decisions un -
der uncertainty.
In a different scientific approach, authors in [76] 
apply graph neural networks (GNN) to recommend 
alternative suppliers in large SCs. A GNN of a major 
automotive supply network and train was defined to 
capture relationships (buyer–supplier links, disrup -
tions, and other variables). The model suggests sub -
stituting suppliers when disruptions occur. This study 
contributes to a data-driven, AI-based framework for 
dynamic supplier selection under disruption, demon-
strating the value of machine learning in SC decision 
support. 
In [77], authors take a game-theoretic approach to 
sourcing decisions under risk. In their research, the 
model was applied considering two competing com -
panies, choosing between a risky (cheaper) and a re -
sponsible (costlier) supplier, under different consum-
er segments and product-line strategies. Results have 
shown that factors like cost differences, product-line 
expansion and dual-sourcing options affect whether 
companies source from the “responsible” supplier. 
The model highlights how demand segmentation 
(e.g. green consumers) can induce sustainable sup -
plier choices. Yet, the methodological contribution is 
analytical, i.e., a strategic framework linking product 
design and sourcing diversification to supplier selec -
tion in competitive markets.
A fuzzy multiple criteria decision making 
(MCDM) framework for circular economy-oriented 
supplier selection demonstrated how it can quantify 
“circularity” in supplier management. This work con-
tributes to a novel set of circularity metrics and an 
advanced fuzzy decision process, emphasizing envi -
ronmental and resource reuse criteria in supplier se -
lection [78]. Sustainable supplier selection to multi-ti-
er supply chains through an hybrid model combining 
fuzzy sets, stepwise weight assessment ratio analysis 
(SWARA) and Bayesian networks also being studied 
[79]. The study highlights how sustainable SC strat -

3. Materials and Methods
A description of the problem to be handled is 
described in this section in detail to set the scenario 
for the development of the optimization framework. 
The problem concerns the outsourcing of jobs to a 
multitude of small companies that must be managed 
by a manufacturer and hence fulfil its needs while 
optimizing costs and delivery time subject to several 
constraints, e.g. outsourcing capacity, production ca-
pacity, supplier selection criteria, piecewise cost, qual-
ity, to name a few. The main manufacturer is a big 
corporation that outsources frequently some of the 
orders they get both to suppress shortage in available 
resources and when specialized jobs are required. 
The main manufacturer must handle several hundred 
orders per month and hence is urged to provide an 
optimum response since disruption or badly planned 
outsourcing may threaten the company’s sustainability 
and thus leverage its competitive advantage.

manufacturing environment, companies increasingly 
rely on hybrid production models where internal 
capabilities are supplemented through outsourcing. 
This strategy is driven by economic efficiency, respon-
siveness, and product diversity. However, outsourc -
ing introduces complexities in supplier selection, 
capacity management, quality assurance, and sched -
uling under uncertainty. The problem addressed in 
this work revolves around the allocation and schedul-
ing of manufacturing orders, each composed of mul-
tiple items, to a portfolio of third-party suppliers. The 
key operational challenge lies in selecting feasible,

The primary goal of the company is to optimize 
outsourcing decisions by minimizing overall costs 
and reducing the makespan or time horizon required 
to complete all orders, while ensuring that deliveries 
are made within their respective deadlines and that 
each order is allocated to a supplier with the nec -
essary capabilities. However, the complexity of the 
problem extends well beyond these core objectives. 
Disruptions, changing supplier availability, and the 
arrival of new orders occur unpredictably, requiring 
continuous monitoring and dynamic rescheduling. 
Order commissioning must typically be executed 
with a one-week lead time, as stipulated in supplier 
agreements to allow for production preparation. 
Moreover, upon receiving an order request, the 
manufacturer has on average a four-day window to 
assess its feasibility and profitability before accep-
tance, though this may vary by contract. In certain 
instances, orders are declined either due to insuffi -

advantageous future opportunities, particularly when 
current suppliers are near capacity.

approach to multi-objective optimization: economic 
objectives are optimized first, and sustainability cri-
teria govern tie-breaking. Further developments may 
elevate this component to a full third objective within 
a Pareto framework, particularly as richer ESG audit

of the problem (with multiple items per job, variable 
lead times, and heterogeneous suppliers), traditional 
exact methods are computationally infeasible. There-
fore, we implement a GA tailored to the structural 
characteristics of the problem. Each chromosome

The proposed representation is designed to ac-
commodate the complexity and variability of real-
world manufacturing scheduling. It supports: (i) vari-
able numbers of items per order; (ii) heterogeneous 
suppliers with distinct capabilities and processing 
times; and (iii) context-specific constraints such as 
feasibility based on supplier capabilities Vs, delivery 
deadlines, and available capacity. To address these 
challenges, the GA integrates domain-specific op-
erators that closely mimic real-world rescheduling 
actions. The crossover operator swaps supplier allo-
cations for subsets of orders between two parent solu-
tions, but only when such swaps respect all feasibility 
conditions, including supplier capability, available 
time, and deadlines. 
The mutation operator reassigns an individual or-
der from one supplier to another, selected at random, 
and similarly only applies the change if all constraints 
are satisfied. These operators differ from generic en-
codings by being semantically meaningful, enabling 
more realistic and interpretable evolutionary steps 
such as job reallocation or re-commissioning due to 
disruptions.

plier ranking index into a scalar fitness value. This 
approach was chosen for its computational simplicity

ments, where a single ranked solution set must be

ruptions or evolving supplier conditions. Comple-
menting the problem formulation and supplier moni-
toring framework outlined previously, this scheduling 
strategy leverages the flexibility and efficiency of a 
GA tailored for dynamic order allocation. The goal 
is twofold: to minimize outsourcing costs and shorten 
the production makespan. Given the inherent vari-
ability and interdependence of scheduling variables, 
even small deviations in delivery times can cause cas-
cading failures and financial penalties. As such, the 
proposed approach emphasizes responsive, adaptive 
decision-making capable of continually integrating 
new information as operational conditions evolve.
The proposed GA follows the fundamental prin -
ciples of natural selection, beginning with the genera-
tion of an initial population and iteratively evolving 
this population through selection, crossover, and mu-
tation operations. The evolutionary process converg-
es toward a near-optimal solution by favouring the 
survival and reproduction of fitter individuals. Unlike 
conventional encodings, typically fixed sequences of 
tuples that define operations, machines, jobs, or cells 
[80], often inflexible and poorly suited to real-world 
scenarios involving variable substructures, the model 
presented here adopts a context-sensitive and struc -
turally mutable representation. Specifically, each 
chromosome is a variable-length nested structure: at 
the top level, genes correspond to suppliers; within 
each supplier gene, a variable-length list of assigned 
orders is maintained; and within each order, the item 
set and quantities are carried as metadata. This three-
level hierarchy – supplier, order, item – is structur -
ally distinct from flat assignment vectors (e.g., a fixed 
array where position i encodes the supplier for job 
i), because it naturally accommodates variable num -
bers of orders per supplier and variable numbers of 
items per order without requiring padding or length 
normalization. As a direct consequence, crossover 
and mutation operate at the semantically meaning -
ful supplier-assignment level rather than at arbitrary 
positional offsets, substantially reducing the rate of 
infeasible offspring compared to generic encodings 
applied to equivalent problem instances. 
Individuals are structured according to supplier-
job allocations, where each supplier may be assigned 
a variable number of jobs, and each job may contain 
a different number of items, reflecting realistic indus-
trial settings. This flexible design enables meaningful 
implementation of crossover and mutation opera -
tions that directly mirror physical scheduling actions. 
Crossover involves the exchange of supplier-job al-
locations between compatible individuals, while mu -
tation consists of relocating a job from one supplier 
to another within the same individual, provided the 
recipient supplier has the required capabilities. This 
approach not only increases the feasibility of gener -
ated individuals but also avoids the limitations associ-
ated with traditional crossover operators, which often 
produce a high proportion of unfit solutions when 
applied to complex representations [81]. The entire 
scheduling framework is implemented in Julia, a 
high-performance programming language developed 
at MIT, specifically chosen for its numerical efficien-
cy and flexibility [82]. This implementation avoids 
the limitations imposed by commercial simulation 
platforms, which often rely on rigid, predefined 
structures that restrict problem-specific adaptations. 
Notably, some prior works claim to introduce novel 
GA-based approaches, but they depend heavily on 
generic solvers like LINGO [83], which are inade -
quate for modelling the kind of dynamic, constraint-
intensive environment addressed here.
The heuristic pruning mechanism operates as 
a feasibility filter applied after crossover and muta -
tion. Specifically, any offspring individual is evaluat -
ed against three hard constraints before entering the 
population: (i) each order must be assigned to exactly 
one supplier possessing the required capabilities (Ri 
⊆ Vs); (ii) the total assigned processing time must 
not exceed the supplier's monthly capacity Ts; and 
(iii) the projected execution date must not exceed the 
order deadline LEDo. Individuals violating any of 
these conditions are discarded rather than penalized, 
ensuring the population remains entirely feasible 
throughout evolution. This pruning incurs O(|O|·|I|) 
per individual per generation, which is negligible rela-
tive to fitness evaluation. Importantly, pruning does 
not systematically disadvantage greener suppliers: in-
feasibility is determined solely by capability, capacity, 
and deadline constraints, which are independent of 
the αs score.
To ensure responsiveness, the algorithm reruns 
the scheduling procedure whenever a new order is 
received, adjusting supplier availability in real-time. 
Job commissioning – currently performed manually 
– is already embedded into the workflow and can be 
automated in future versions. Each commissioning 
event updates the monthly availability of the selected 
supplier by deducting the duration required for the

Overall, the proposed framework offers a prac -

in distributed manufacturing environments. By re -

4. Results
The following test results were obtained with the 
information contained in three main tables within 
an SQL database: “Orders”, “Suppliers” and “Sup -

information pertaining to each order to be sched -

status and due date. Table “Suppliers” contains the 
information pertaining to each of the available sup -

and scoring indexes related to economic, social and 
environmental criteria. In table “Supplier Capacity” 
is registered the capacity of suppliers to manufacture

the database and 119 jobs/orders to be scheduled.

109. There were 20 different registered items in the 
supplier, and, on average, each supplier was able to 
manufacture 6 of these items, mainly household, e.g., 
bedding, tablecloths and towels.

5. Discussion
5.1 Framework contributions to SC 
optimization 
The experimentation's results offer valuable in-
sights into the efficiency and effectiveness of the pro-
posed approach to order allocation and scheduling 
within the textile industry. Drawing upon data from 
three primary tables within an SQL database – “Or -
ders”, “Suppliers” and “Supplier Capacity” – the 
study conducts comprehensive analyses, providing 
a robust foundation for its findings. By tracking the 
evolution of the heuristic search through 1500 itera -
tions of optimization, utilizing a population-based ap-
proach with varying mutation probabilities, the study 
illustrates the impact of mutation probability on con-
vergence rates. Figures 2 and 3 showcase this evolu -
tion, highlighting that higher mutation probabilities 
lead to faster convergence, as evidenced by Figure 3's 
progression compared to Figure 2. 
Similarly, Figure 4 underscores how lower muta-
tion probabilities result in slower convergence rates. 
Moreover, the investigation delves into the effect of 
population size on convergence rates, as depicted 
in Figures 5, 6, and 7. Here, larger population sizes 
demonstrate faster convergence, yielding optimized 
solutions in fewer iterations. However, lower muta-
tion probabilities contribute to slower convergence, 
as evidenced in Figure 7. Figure 8 offers an overview 
of the overall influence of mutation probability and 
population size on efficiency. Notably, both param -
eters significantly impact convergence rates, with mu-
tation probability exhibiting a higher impact. 
Optimal scheduling solutions are attained with a 
population size of 180 individuals or more and a mu-
tation probability of approximately 30%. Normalized 
efficiency metrics further enhance the understanding 
of the performance gains achieved through optimiza-
tion. For example, Figure 3 showcases a substantial 
improvement in overall efficiency, with a total gain of 
0.36 in the combined indicator integrating both cost 
and makespan efficiency. The reduction in makes -
pan efficiency by 45% indicates a significant enhance-
ment in scheduling effectiveness, while the marginal 
reduction in costs reflects the minimal variability in 
prices among suppliers. Importantly, all scenarios 
exhibit execution times below 10 seconds, indicating 
the practical viability of the proposed approach for 
making technical decisions in real-world settings.
The alignment between the experimentation's 
findings and the broader trends and challenges out-
lined in the review of SCM dynamics is striking. The 
review underscores the increasing complexity of sup-
ply chains driven by globalization, outsourcing prac -
tices, and the emergence of Green Supply Chain 
Management (GSCM). This complexity underscores 
the need for dynamic and flexible approaches to sup-
plier selection and order allocation, precisely what the 
proposed optimization framework aims to achieve.

Regarding the contribution of the research from a 
methodological point-of-view. the algorithm was de-
signed for real-time optimization. Whenever a new 
order arrives, the GA is iteratively to update the sup-

tion, the GA produces a ranked set of near-optimal 
schedules, allowing the decision makers to switch 
between supply alternatives in case of disruptions, 
greatly enhancing the adaptability of the proposed 
method when compared to one-step SC scheduling 
approaches. It should be acknowledged that the pre-
sented experimental results reflect a static scenario of 
119 orders and 21 suppliers, as available at the time 
of evaluation. Disruption scenarios – such as supplier 
capacity loss or late delivery cascades – are supported 
by the algorithmic design (re-triggering the GA upon 
any state change) but were not formally simulated in 
the current experiments due to the industrial part-
ner's data constraints. This represents a limitation 
of the present validation, and structured disruption 
simulation is identified as a priority for future work, 
including controlled capacity-reduction and delivery-
delay injection experiments.
Another important aspect of the presented study 
is the fact that the framework was fully implemented 
in Julia, allowing to integrate a detailed production-
process simulator that replicates real industrial dy-
namics. Thus, since it is not a commercial tool, the 
code can handle the dynamic constraints of the tex-
tile case with flexibility within reduced computational 
time.
Overall, unlike MILP-based models from litera-
ture, this approach continuously adapts to new data 
and information, allowing to integrate green sup -
plier selection with optimization scheduling, whereas 
many other studies treat these aspects separately [20], 
[75]. The use of multi-objective GA with rich prob -
lem encoding represents a clear advance in computa-
tional realism and flexibility compared to traditional 
supply-chain optimization models.
5.2 Framework contributions for decision 
makers in industry
The model was tailored to a real textile manu-
facturer, considering multiple production capaci-
ties, several suppliers handling small-batch orders 
and close-fitting delivery deadlines. By modelling 
constraints like supplier availability, the framework 
allows to coordinate several concurrent outsourcing 
relationships. This delivers more flexibility to sched-
uling than conventional methods [68], [84]. The 
case study explicitly applies to green supplier selec-
tion and provides a structured basis for modelling 
institutional policy levers – such as green procure-
ment thresholds and non-compliance penalties – as 
parameters that shift stable allocation patterns under 
disruption [85]. This is a pathway consistent with re-
cent work demonstrating how behavioural heteroge-
neity, audit probabilities, and incentive-compatible 
contracts can be incorporated into adaptive supplier 
selection models [86]. Each supplier is scored, and 
the GA can prioritize greener options. By optimiz-
ing resource use (shortening makespan and reducing 
delays), the framework promotes waste reduction 
and more sustainable operations. This aligns with in-
dustry trends toward greener, circular supply chains 
and provides a practical tool for implementing such 
policies [78].
From a wider perspective, the framework proved 
capable of handling dynamic order inflows and multi-
objective decisions within low run-times. Its built-in 
order-update nature suggest that the framework 
could be integrated into different production plan -
ning systems, offering a scalable solution that makes 
SC scheduling more efficient and competitive while 
supporting greener production strategies.

6. Conclusions
In conclusion, this research paper introduces a 
framework model tailored for green supplier selec -
tion and order allocation within the dynamic textile 
market. The framework adeptly incorporates various 
constraints related to supplier selection, emphasizing 
sustainability criteria. By leveraging the flexibility and 
performance advantages of GA, the approach offers 
diverse action scenarios to optimize scheduling for 
order allocation and supplier selection while adher -
ing to contractor deadlines. The multi-objective na -
ture of the framework allows for the fine-tuning of 
parametric settings to prioritize either higher rev -
enues or shorter makespan.
Testing the framework with real data from a 
prominent textile company validates its efficiency 
and capability to manage continuous order updates 
and rescheduling tasks, generating various optimi-
zation scenarios based on multi-objective settings. 
Notably, time emerges as the variable with the most 
significant impact on optimization, likely influenced 
by contractor availability, while cost variations among 
contractors remain minimal. The key factors contrib-
uting to enhanced performance of the genetic algo -
rithm are population size and mutation probability, 
with a mutation probability exceeding 25% yielding 
superior results. Moreover, increasing the population 
size from 60-80 individuals enhances performance, 
with mutation probability exerting a more substantial 
influence.
However, the study acknowledges several limita-
tions stemming from the use of real data, necessitat -
ing further research endeavours. Additionally, the 
recommended GA parameters – mutation probabil-
ity of approximately 30% and population size of 180 
or more – were identified through sensitivity analysis 
on the same industrial dataset used for evaluation. 
This introduces a risk of instance-specific overfit -
ting, and cross-instance or out-of-sample validation 
on synthetic instances of comparable scale is recom -
mended to confirm the generalizability of these pa-
rameter recommendations. The constrained number 
of orders, suppliers, and selection criteria within the 
existing dataset may not fully challenge the robust -
ness of the framework under more demanding cir-
cumstances. Additionally, conducting sensitivity anal-
yses of the multi-objective optimization function in 
an automated environment, employing weighted cri-
teria, can elucidate the impact of competing interests. 
Furthermore, exploring different dimensions or gene 
stacks within the framework across various industry 
sectors can provide insights into their suitability, per-
formance, and the benefits they introduce. Further -
more, the sensitivity analysis focused on algorithmic 
parameters (mutation probability and population 
size) without exploring the space of objective weights 
w₁ and w₂ governing the cost–makespan trade-off. 
Systematic weight-space exploration – for instance, 
through a grid search over the simplex w₁ + w₂ = 1 
– would enable decision-makers to explicitly navigate 
the efficiency frontier and understand the conditions 
under which cost minimization should be prioritized 
over makespan reduction, or vice versa. This is iden-
tified as a near-term extension of the present work.
In summary, while this study demonstrates prom-
ising results and insights into green supplier selection 
and order allocation in the textile industry, further 
research is warranted to address the identified limita-
tions and expand the applicability and robustness of 
the framework across diverse operational contexts. 
In conclusion, the study's results provide practical 
insights that complement the theoretical discussions 
presented in the review. By addressing key challeng -
es and leveraging advanced optimization techniques, 
the proposed approach offers a promising solution 
to enhance order allocation and scheduling efficien -
cy within the textile industry, aligning with broader 
trends and challenges in SCM.
