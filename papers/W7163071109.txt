Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to automate components of product carbon footprinting (PCF). How-
ever, the carbon footprinting literature lacks criteria for evaluating the quality, documentation, uncertainty indication, and
reviewability of AI-assisted systems for speciﬁc PCF use cases. We developed a set of evaluation criteria for AI-assisted
carbon footprinting (AI-CF) through standards review, structured stakeholder elicitation with corporate practitioners and
veriﬁcation specialists, and iterative pilot testing. The resulting criteria are organized around transparent logic, benchmarking
against expert judgment, and uncertainty characterization. The criteria were then applied to two use cases: automated life-
cycle-inventory proxy mapping and cradle-to-gate PCF generation. Applying the criteria to two deployed proprietary AI-CF
systems demonstrates that the framework is operational and can identify where human review or better data are most needed.
For automated mapping, well-speciﬁed (“non-vague”) inputs achieved a 91.2% expert-accepted mapping rate, whereas inputs
with ambiguous or incomplete descriptions (“vague”; e.g., internal product codes or generic descriptors) achieved 59.7%;
repeat-run entropy increased with input ambiguity. For cradle-to-gate modeling, end-to-end benchmarking against 269 Envi-
ronmental Product Declarations yielded a 33% median relative error, while component-level benchmark checks, though
unvalidated, showed relatively strong pass rates for proxy mappings, hotspots, and direct emissions and weaker performance
for key input rates. The framework provides a practical reference for AI-CF developers, users, and reviewers to assess AI-CF
system and output quality for these use cases.

Previous studies, however, do not specify what combina-
tion of checks, documentation, uncertainty indicators, and
human review is needed to determine whether an AI-CF sys-
tem and its outputs satisfy stated requirements for an intended
use. Existing PCF and greenhouse gas (GHG) accounting
standards deﬁne requirements for PCF outputs, but the liter-
ature does not yet provide AI-speciﬁc evaluation criteria for
non-deterministic systems that may generate different out-
puts for the same input.
This paper addresses that gap by developing evalua-
tion criteria for two AI-CF use cases: automated mapping
of procurement data to proxy LCI datasets and automated
cradle-to-gate PCF generation through supply-chain model-
ing. We use the term “quality” in the ISO 14040 sense of
ﬁtness for use (International Organization for Standardiza-
tion, 2026a, 2006b). In this paper, data quality refers to the
extent to which input data, proxy datasets, modeled param-
eters, and PCF outputs satisfy stated requirements for the
intended use. Output-level reviewability refers to the avail-
ability of sufﬁcient documentation, sources, assumptions,
quality indicators, and uncertainty indicators for a qualiﬁed
reviewer to inspect and challenge a speciﬁc AI-generated
mapping, parameter, or PCF result. Expert-accepted per-
formance refers to the fraction of outputs that match at
least one option judged acceptable by qualiﬁed reviewers
under the study evaluation protocol. In this framing, we treat
repeated-run variability as a diagnostic uncertainty indicator
of input ambiguity or weak consensus in evidence, along-
side benchmarking, source documentation, and output-level
quality indicators.
The criteria were developed through standards review,
structured stakeholder elicitation with corporate practition-
ers and veriﬁcation specialists, and iterative pilot testing. We
then applied the criteria to two deployed proprietary AI-CF
systems: an auto-mapper for proxy selection and an advanced
modeling system for cradle-to-gate PCF generation. The
case study is not intended as evidence that the AI-CF sys-
tems tested meet the proposed evaluation criteria; rather, it
tests whether the proposed criteria can be operationalized
and whether they reveal actionable insights for improve-
ment. In the mapping case, performance declines sharply for
ambiguous inputs, and repeat-run variability helps identify
descriptions that require better data or human review. In the
modeling case, component-level checks identify key input
rates as weaker than proxy mappings, hotspot identiﬁcation,
and direct emissions checks, though these component-level
checks themselves still need calibration. Section 2 describes
the criteria development methodology. Section3 presents the
resulting criteria. Section 4 applies the criteria to the two
deployed systems. Section 5 discusses implications, limita-
tions, and future work for validation practice, and Sect. 6
concludes.
2 Methods: criteria development
2.1 Criteria development
The criteria were developed in three phases, described in
full in Supplementary Information SI3. Phase 1 combined
a review of ISO 14040/44 (International Organization for
Standardization, 2006b), the GHG Protocol Product Stan-
dard (2011), and the WBCSD PACT framework (2024) with
a structured practitioner elicitation organized as a multi-
phase Product Advisory Group. The group’s ﬁrst cohort
comprised eight enterprises across seven sectors and more
than sixty participants in sustainability, procurement, and
LCA roles. Engagement followed a standardized protocol
of multi-session workshops and recorded working sessions
in which participants applied AI-CF systems to their own
procurement data and incumbent LCA studies. Two pro-
fessional veriﬁcation ﬁrms, in Europe and North America,
were consulted separately. Not every requirement raised
was adopted; exact run-to-run reproducibility, conﬁdence
intervals on every value, agreement with an incumbent con-
sultant’s results, and third-party assurance as a precondition
were considered and set aside for the reasons given in Sup-
plementary Information SI3.
Following ISO 14040, we treat data quality as ﬁtness
for use and operationalize it for AI-CF systems through
use-case-speciﬁc criteria for output quality, methodologi-
cal conformance, uncertainty indication, and output-level
reviewability. An AI-CF output need not be perfect, a stan-
dard human experts do not meet either, but it should be
reviewable and should satisfy predeﬁned quality criteria for
its intended use. The criteria are organized around three oper-
ational requirements:
 Transparent logic: the reasoning behind a mapping or
modeling decision should be accessible and should fol-
low accepted carbon accounting principles.
 Benchmarking against expert judgment or documented
reference values: outputs should match expert-labeled
acceptable mappings, reviewer-accepted modeling
choices, or predeﬁned benchmark tolerances.
 Uncertainty characterization and reviewability: the sys-
tem should not present guesses as facts; it should provide
validated uncertainty indicators and documentation that
let users or reviewers intervene on material or high-risk
items.

These requirements answer calls in the emerging literature
for shared norms, quantitative relevance metrics, benchmark
datasets, and expert feedback in automated LCA (Preuss &

Phase 2 segmented the problem into three use cases,
because a single set of criteria would be too stringent
for simple applications and too lenient for complex ones.
Case 1, automated mapping, serves companies moving from
spend-based Scope 3 category 1 estimates to material-level
granularity, for example distinguishing grades of steel or
types of plastic across thousands of procurement lines. It
links unstructured text (“304 SS sheet”) to structured LCI
entries (“steel production, chromium steel 18/8, hot rolled”),
and its challenges are semantic ambiguity, taxonomy mis-
alignment, and granularity. Case 2, automated modeling,
serves companies seeking supply-chain hotspots and reduc-
tion scenarios for purchased materials, whether across a
portfolio for decarbonization planning or for a few materials
in supplier engagement. It generates synthetic bills of mate-
rials and process ﬂows for products with no direct database
match, such as a speciﬁc model of television, and its chal-
lenges are parameter estimation and structural logic. Case 3,
standards-conforming modeling under speciﬁc Product Cat-
egory Rules or reporting frameworks, is out of scope here
because each standard imposes its own requirements (Sup-
plementary Information SI3 discusses its relation to Case 2).
Phase 3 reﬁned the draft criteria through iterative pilot
testing against real procurement data and early versions of
Case 1 and Case 2 systems; the approaches rejected in that
process, and why, are tabulated in Supplementary Informa-
tion SI3.

3 Results: evaluation criteria for AI-CF
systems
The iterative research process described in Sect. 2 produced
a framework that operationalizes evaluation through system-
level validation performance, output quality, output-level
reviewability, and uncertainty characterization. System-level
evaluations assess the AI-CF system’s general capabilities
and scalability, while output-level indicators support doc-
umentation, user agency, and targeted human review for
individual outputs. Each criterion traces back to the three
operational requirements identiﬁed in Phase 1. Table 1 maps
each requirement to its criteria, the evaluation question it
addresses for a user or reviewer, and the metric families
appropriate to it.
3.1 Case 1 criteria
For systems automating the selection of proxy LCI datasets,
evaluation should assess whether the system can reproduce
expert-accepted mapping choices, provide transparent doc-
umentation, and indicate where human input is needed.
Effective AI-assisted mapping systems should demonstrate
the criteria in Tables 2 and 3. Additional details on criteria
are presented in the text.
Criteria Details:
[1.1] Benchmark performance: The precise benchmark
result will depend on the complexity of the products and
available datasets to map to, so we do not propose a spe-
ciﬁc target for this metric here. If a labeled dataset were to
be made available such as (Balaji et al., 2025; Dumit et al.,
2024), it could be used to track progress and enable compar-
isons against a common target. For example, on the subset
of inputs that exactly match a database entry, benchmark
performance should approach 100%, because the choice is
unambiguous. Below that ceiling, achievable performance
depends on input speciﬁcity and database coverage, not
only on expert agreement. Even where experts agree on a
single acceptable mapping, real-world procurement descrip-
tions carry ambiguity that lowers achievable performance. If
experts systematically map incorrectly, this benchmark may
also be less than 100%. Clearly represented plastic mate-
rials or chemicals mapped to a data source like ecoinvent
should also achieve a high benchmark performance, but a
result below 100% may be acceptable where multiple expert-
accepted proxy mappings exist.
Candidate metrics should reﬂect that mapping is a selec-
tion task in which multiple answers may be acceptable; a
match-any metric credits any defensible option but depends
on how completely the accepted sets are enumerated and is an
upper bound relative to single-best-answer scoring. Example
metric families are listed in Table 1, and the metrics used in
the case study are discussed in Supplementary Information
SI1. Because a benchmark inherits the noise of its labels,
benchmark evidence should also disclose how the reference
labels were produced: who annotated them, how many inde-
pendent annotators reviewed each item, how disagreements
were reconciled or accommodated (for example, by accept-
ing more than one defensible mapping), and inter-annotator
agreement where it was measured.
Whether a given level of performance (e.g., 60% versus
95%) is adequate depends on how the mappings will be used:
a system with lower performance may be acceptable only if
automated mappings are used for items that are not material

[1.5] Match quality indication: Many kinds of uncertainty
affect GHG analyses, and LCA offers many ways to cal-
culate and display them, from pedigree-matrix data quality
indicators to Monte Carlo propagation and global sensitivity
analysis (Mendoza Beltran et al., 2018; Qiao et al., 2025;
Cullen et al., 2024b; Adams & Allacker, 2025); the GHG
Protocol Technical Working Groups expect uncertainty and
data quality to gain weight in the coming standards update
(GHG Protocol Technical Working Group, 2025). For proxy
mapping, an additional uncertainty arises from the match
between the input material description and the selected proxy
dataset. Match quality refers here to the degree of correspon-
dence between the input description and the selected proxy
LCI dataset, considering material properties, form, process-
ing state, speciﬁcity, and expected emissions proﬁle. For
proxy mapping to a single dataset, a qualitative uncertainty
or data-quality framework may sufﬁce to inform the user of
the relative quality of each mapping. We found that match
quality is often overlooked in LCA but can have a strong
impact on emissions representation (Dumit et al., 2026).
[1.5.1] Indicator validation: If a match-quality indicator is
used to decide which mappings a person reviews and which
are accepted without review, it acts as an error detector,
and how far it can be trusted becomes an empirical ques-
tion. Whether the indicator is produced by a second model,
a heuristic, or a statistical procedure, the provider should
benchmark it against mappings whose correctness was estab-
lished independently of the system and disclose the results.
These metrics may include the share of erroneous mappings
it marks acceptable, the error rate among accepted mappings,
the share of errors caught at the intended review budget, how
the validation set relates to production inputs, and when it
is revalidated. There is no universal set of metrics ﬁt for all
uses. Instead, we suggest users and builders of such systems
use these metrics to set their own risk budgets accordingly

3.2 Case 2 criteria
All of the Case 1 criteria for proxy mappings are required
as prerequisites to the end-stage mappings in Case 2 models.
In addition to those criteria, automated modeling systems
should demonstrate the following criteria (Tables 4, 5).
Criteria Details:
[2.1] Reasonable precision and benchmarking: Carbon
footprints and the underlying activity estimates and energy
and processing components can be benchmarked against
datasets. Full carbon footprints can be compared to results
from published EPDs (EPD International, 2024; Smart EPD,
2026), licensed data such as ecoinvent (Ecoinvent Asso-
ciation, 2024; Wernet et al., 2016), and literature values
such as The Carbon Catalogue (Meinrenken et al., 2022).
Within a modeled product or material, sub-processes and
components can be benchmarked against literature values
or calculated values. For example, theoretical and practical
energy minimums for industrial processes can be quantiﬁed
using methodology proposed in Bolson et al. (2025). A poten-
tial target for full product footprints is median error and P90
error below speciﬁed thresholds when benchmarked against
veriﬁed PCFs (where available), plus clear assessments of
subcomponents. This is an area where publicly available
datasets and leaderboards or competitions might be useful
for assessing and calibrating different AI-CF systems. Care
would be needed to ensure there is no cross-contamination
of training data and evaluation data.
[2.2] Display of uncertainty: Modeled materials and prod-
ucts should make clear to the user which assumptions or
nodes have the biggest uncertainties driving the overall uncer-
tainty and sensitivity. This could be a simple indicator, or
it could be numeric. PCFs contain a plethora of sources of
uncertainty. A goal is to reduce these with a focus on where
data can enable directionally correct comparative decisions.
Lacking clear ground-truth data or sufﬁcient sample sizes, it
may be infeasible to achieve true statistically supported con-
clusions, which Henriksson et al. (2015) suggests is required

Case 2 validation requirements:
V alidation should include all of the same components as
for Case 1, plus additional validation against the criteria
described above for Case 2.
System-level review is an important aspect of scalable AI-
CFs. The WBCSD PACT framework (2024) already records
which steps and processes were reviewed for PACT align-
ment during PCF validation, which points to scalable review
concepts such as system-level assurance, boundary checks,
and data input checks. Performed once on the PCF generation
system using the artifacts described above, such checks may
reduce repetitive review while preserving risk-based review
of material outputs and assumptions.
By reviewing a sample of outputs systematically and
conﬁrming that the system provides consistent traceability,
benchmarking, and data quality indicators for individual out-
puts, reviewers and auditors may be able to generalize some
system-level checks to repeated outputs from the same sys-
tem. This should not be interpreted as eliminating PCF-level
review. Rather, it can allow review effort to focus on material
items, low-quality matches, high-uncertainty components,
and outputs ﬂagged by benchmark checks.
Non-exhaustive examples where system-level demonstra-
tions of consistent application for example cases may be
reviewed at the system level rather than repeated for every
PCF-level assumption are as follows:
 If the system follows boundaries provided as input
methodology, then a validation for an AI-CF from that
system does not need to separately conﬁrm that the
boundary for a particular methodology is followed for
each PCF.
 If the system incorporates reference data or lookup tables
provided as inputs, then a validation for an AI-CF from
that system does not need to separately conﬁrm that the
speciﬁc reference data is used for each PCF.
 If the system is able to provide justiﬁcation for key
assumptions, not every key assumption needs to be
reviewed.
 If the system uses reasonable formulas and assumptions
or veriﬁed software for unit conversions, not every unit
conversion in an AI-CF needs to be reviewed.
These examples are relevant to AI evaluation precisely
because deployed AI-CF systems are hybrid architectures
rather than bare language models: they embed LLM compo-
nents within deterministic software such as ﬁxed boundary
logic, controlled reference-data lookups, and algorithmic unit
conversion and mass-balance checks. An evaluation frame-
work must therefore distinguish guarantees that derive from
deterministic software, which can be validated once at the
system level as in the examples above, from behaviors that are
AI-generated and require per-output veriﬁcation through the
ﬂagging, benchmarking, and documentation criteria. Con-
ﬂating the two either wastes review effort re-checking deter-
ministic behavior or, worse, extends software-style one-time
validation to stochastic outputs that need ongoing checks.
System-level reviews are common for deterministic sustain-
ability software and spreadsheet tools; their application to
AI-assisted systems is newer, with precedents in software
(Chen et al., 2013; Hand & Khan, 2020) and medicine (All-
gaier & Pryss, 2024; Baxter & Jannin, 2023; Straub, 2021)
and generative-AI-speciﬁc approaches surveyed by Y u et al.
(2025). Once a system is reviewed, material outputs and
outputs with data quality concerns are still reviewed; low-
materiality and repeatedly checked elements may receive
more limited review, as in risk-based GHG audits.
4 Case study: applying the framework to a
deployed system
We applied the criteria to two proprietary AI systems
deployed at Watershed Technology Inc.: an auto-mapper for
proxy selection (Case 1) and an advanced modeling sys-
tem for cradle-to-gate PCF modeling (Case 2). This section
reports the quantitative benchmarking criteria (1.1, 1.2, 1.4,

4.1 Systems
The Auto-Mapper (Fig. 1) maps unstructured procurement
text to proxy LCI datasets in ecoinvent v3.11 through a dual-
agent workﬂow: a mapping agent that augments the input
with domain context and queries a vector-indexed material

library, and an independent Quality Reviewer that scores
each mapping on a two-dimensional rubric (material match
and emissions match) without seeing the mapper’s reasoning
(Dumit et al., 2026). The system always produces a map-
ping; poor matches are ﬂagged by a low score for human
review or escalation to Case 2. The Advanced Modeling Sys-
tem generates a cradle-to-gate PCF as a production graph,
decomposing products into sub-components and raw mate-

composition, and processing inputs, deterministic validation
checks, and an independent Graph Reviewer that benchmarks
components against external reference values; leaf nodes
are mapped to ecoinvent activities with the same mapping
approach as the Auto-Mapper, so the Case 1 mapping evi-

mapping agent uses Gemini 2.5 Flash, the Quality Reviewer
Claude Haiku 4.5, and the modeling system Claude Opus
4.5; both systems are model-ﬂexible and select the lowest-
cost model that meets the benchmarks in Sect. 3 (Online

4.2 Case 1: automated mapping performance
Benchmark Performance (Criterion 1.1): The Auto-
Mapper was evaluated on N = 1198 test cases spanning
an external benchmark and production data using expert-
accepted accuracy: the fraction of cases where the selected
mapping matches any expert-annotated acceptable option
(Dumit et al., 2026). The labels were curated by a pool of
six in-house sustainability scientists, three holding doctorates
in LCA or adjacent ﬁelds, the same pool that annotated the
public PCFBench benchmark (Rao et al., 2026). Each item
was reviewed by at least two and up to six annotators, who
recorded every mapping they judged defensible and settled
disputed labels by consensus; labels for the public Para-
keet subset were re-reviewed with each change documented
(Dumit et al., 2026). No formal inter-annotator agreement
statistic was computed (Sect.5.6); the dataset captures expert
disagreement implicitly, with 24.8% of items carrying two
accepted mappings, 7.3% three, and 3.1% four or more,
consistent with the practitioner variability documented by
Courtat et al. ( 2025) and Kuczenski et al. ( 2021). Test cases
were tagged vague when the description lacked the speci-
ﬁcity for unambiguous identiﬁcation (internal codes, unclear
abbreviations, generic descriptors such as “plastic part”).
For non-vague inputs the system achieved 91.2% expert-
accepted accuracy in the January 2026 evaluation (98.9%
on the Parakeet benchmark, 85.9% on production data); for
vague inputs, 59.7% (Table 6). Input quality is therefore
the primary driver of accuracy; items with several accepted
options were not easier, since accuracy declined modestly as
the number of accepted options rose (Supplementary Infor-
mation SI1). The Quality Reviewer ﬂags nearly all mappings
outside the accepted set as low quality: at the HIGH tier,
0.3% of mappings are erroneous, and 1 of the 97 errors in the
evaluation set lands there (Supplementary Information SI1,
Criterion 1.5.1).
Consistency (Criterion 1.2): Performance varied with input
quality more than with material type: 100% on catalysts,
95.6% on packaging, and 85.9% on production data for non-
vague inputs, with vague inputs degraded in every category
(production data 55.4%).
Repeatability and Entropy (Criterion 1.4): Run ten times,
the system maps exact database names identically (normal-
ized entropy 0.00) and varies on vague inputs (0.57 for
“plastic part”, between polyethylene and polypropylene), so
entropy tracks input ambiguity (Supplementary Information
SI1, Criterion 1.4). Whether it also marks errors was tested
on a labeled evaluation of 1193 items run ten times each:
Flagging any run-to-run disagreement sends 39% of items
to review, catches 79% of incorrect mappings and 91% of
items with a severe emissions deviation, and leaves a 5.7%
incorrect-mapping rate among unﬂagged items. Flagging
only items whose emission factors range by more than a fac-
tor of two across runs sends 22% to review and still catches
88% of the severe deviations (Supplementary Information
SI1, Criterion 1.4).
4.3 Case 2: automated modeling performance
Precision and Benchmarking (Criterion 2.1): Against 269
EPDs pre-ﬁltered to cradle-to-gate (A1–A3) emissions on the
same declared unit (sources and product categories in Supple-
mentary Information SI2), the Advanced Modeling System
achieved a median relative error of 33%, an 80% interval
of [6%, 98%], and 86% of cases within 80% of the bench-
mark; the median signed error is −2% with symmetric over-
and under-estimation (Fig. 2). The largest over-predictions
arise from complex allocation (e.g., cashmere), sparse public
data (specialty chemicals), and a system limitation on recy-
cled content for certain alloys. Residual differences from any
single EPD are expected, since EPDs for identical products

(Gelowitz & McArthur, 2017; Konradsen et al., 2024) and
six expert teams modeling the same cotton data diverged by
44% (Textile Exchange, 2026); the 33% median error sits
within that range of practitioner disagreement.

components fell within bounds by check (Supplementary

5 Discussion
These ﬁndings provide a framework for evaluating AI-
assisted product carbon footprinting systems and their out-
puts for speciﬁed use cases.
5.1 System-level validation performance and
targeted output review
With corporations reporting on tens of thousands of SKUs,
manual veriﬁcation of random samples of all aspects of indi-
vidual PCFs is practically infeasible at the scale required. The
proposed framework supports system-level validation per-
formance checks while preserving targeted output review.
By assessing whether the AI-CF system meets predeﬁned
benchmark, documentation, uncertainty, and reviewability
criteria across representative test cases, assurance can focus
on material outputs, ﬂagged cases, and speciﬁed components
with data quality concerns that need further review. This mir-
rors risk-based review approaches in other domains, such as
ﬁnancial algorithms, where control environments are tested
rather than every transaction. The benchmarking criteria (1.1,
1.2, 2.1) supply the evidence for such system-level validation,
while the ﬂagging criteria (1.4, 1.5, 2.2) direct the residual
review effort.
5.2 Output-level documentation and reviewability
System-level benchmarking establishes aggregate perfor-
mance but does not by itself make a speciﬁc output
reviewable. That gap is closed at the output level by documen-
tation, quality indicators, citations, recorded assumptions,
and uncertainty ﬂags (Criteria 1.6–1.10 and 2.3–2.8), which
give users the information to accept, override, or investigate
individual materials or model components. Data quality indi-
cators, benchmarking research, and component-level checks
are valuable because they do not depend on a perfect-match
ground truth and so generalize across product categories; they

5.3 Output variability as a diagnostic indicator
A recurring critique of generative AI in science is its non-
determinism. We argue that, treated methodically, run-to-run
variability is not merely a defect to be engineered away but
a measurable diagnostic that any AI-CF system can report,
regardless of architecture; it is the basis of the repeatability
and uncertainty-indication criteria (1.4 and 2.2) rather than a
feature of any particular implementation. In contexts where
input data are ambiguous, repeat-run variability may be infor-
mative for review because it can reveal that the input does not
support a single well-speciﬁed mapping or parameter choice.
A deterministic system that always maps “plastic wrapper” to
“LDPE ﬁlm” may imply certainty that is not warranted, while
a system that varies between LDPE, LLDPE, and PP signals
that the input lacks speciﬁcity for a conﬁdent determination.
Likewise, modeled parameters may rely on different relevant
citations across repeated runs when the available evidence
base is broad or weakly constrained. In this light, expert-
accepted accuracy measures whether a system’s committed
single output is defensible, while the breadth of acceptable
options a system surfaces across repeated runs is a comple-
mentary signal that supports review and uncertainty display,
not a substitute for single-output accuracy. The correlation
between high output entropy and input ambiguity suggests
that output variability can be used as one diagnostic indicator
of input ambiguity, consistent with recent ﬁndings that LLM
output entropy can be decomposed into input-driven ambi-
guity and model-internal uncertainty (Farquhar et al., 2024;
Taparia et al., 2026).

Entropy diagnoses ambiguity; it is not by itself a validated
error detector, because an ambiguous input may have several
defensible mappings among which the system varies with-
out being wrong. Where entropy is used to route items for
review, it falls under Criterion 1.5.1. The stochastic nature of
LLMs can thus be used to support review without replacing
deterministic outputs for end users (see Sect. 4). In principle,
entropy analysis could also serve as a runtime feature: results
could be presented as a range of plausible or expert-accepted
candidate outputs rather than a single selection, giving users
visibility into output sensitivity to ambiguous inputs.
5.4 Human review and risk-based oversight
Empirical evidence shows that while AI excels at reasoning,
it requires expert-deﬁned guardrails for speciﬁc constraints
(e.g., appropriate proxy selection). The graph reviewer agent
in the case study, an AI that critiques other AI outputs through
web search and benchmarking, was developed by codify-
ing the intuition of senior carbon accounting experts into
prompts. Ablation studies in Dumit et al. ( 2026) demon-
strate that curated domain expertise is essential: removing
expert-encoded proxy selection guidance reduced mapping
accuracy by 9 percentage points even when the full iterative
architecture was retained. Y et ongoing human oversight of
AI-generated PCFs remains critical. The framework recom-
mends that reviewers always examine the highest-materiality
outputs and all material items ﬂagged by a validated quality
indicator, consistent with risk-based audit practices. An indi-
cator may be used to reduce or waive review only where its
error-detection performance has been established (Criteria
1.5.1 and 2.2.1); until then, review effort should be allocated
by materiality and random sampling regardless of what the
indicator reports. In the case study, the match-quality ﬂag
meets this bar and the Case 2 component checks do not yet
(Supplementary Information SI1). The AI system augments
but does not replace human judgment.
Potential risks of AI-CF systems must also be acknowl-
edged: companies could cherry-pick favorable AI out-
puts, use screening-level results for regulatory compliance
beyond their intended scope, or suppress low-conﬁdence
ﬂags. The framework mitigates these risks through rec-
ommended design patterns such as data quality indicators,
continuous benchmarking against external datasets, and
transparency and traceability requirements. A risk-based
sampling approach that examines the most material inputs
alongside a random sample of remaining items can fur-
ther mitigate abuse potential, ensuring that cherry-picking
is detectable. Acceptable performance thresholds and mate-
riality are context-dependent and should be deﬁned by the
reporting framework in use. A related concern, whether a
stochastic system could be rerun until it yields a lower value,
is addressed by the same criteria: an undocumented rerun-
and-select rule fails Criterion 1.9, the review trail of Criterion
1.10 would expose it, and assurance practice polices consis-
tency across reporting periods (Supplementary Information
SI1, Sect. 5).
5.5 Distinguishing adequate from inadequate
systems
The case study describes systems that meet most of the
criteria, so it is worth stating how the criteria expose fail-
ure; Supplementary Information SI1 (Sect. 5) works through
examples at the output, system, and indicator levels, draw-
ing on the public PCFBench evaluation of frontier language
models (Rao et al., 2026) and on the case-study ﬂag vari-
ants (Dumit et al., 2026), together with the red-ﬂag patterns
that appear across Criteria 1.1, 1.4, and 1.5 in combination.
Whether such a pattern makes a system unﬁt is a ﬁtness-
for-purpose judgment. The criteria supply the evidence for
it: the residual error rate among outputs accepted without
review (at its upper conﬁdence bound) compared with the
materiality tolerance of the intended use, performance on
the production-like segment of inputs, and whether the indi-
cators relied on have been validated at all. A screening-level
footprint of thousands of low-materiality items can tolerate
a residual error rate that a reported ﬁgure under a 5% mate-
riality threshold cannot, and the same system may be ﬁt for
the ﬁrst use and unﬁt for the second.
5.6 Limitations and future research
Several limitations bound the criteria and the process that pro-
duced them. First, the criteria were developed for two of the
three identiﬁed use cases; standards-conforming modeling
(Case 3) remains open because each standard imposes its own
requirements. Second, benchmarking against expert judg-
ment presupposes expert-labeled datasets, and the datasets
available today (including our own) are produced by small
pools of annotators through iterative curation, without for-
mal inter-annotator agreement measurement; the expert-
accepted ceiling itself therefore carries unquantiﬁed uncer-
tainty, which is why Criterion 1.1 asks providers to disclose
label provenance instead of presuming consensus. Third, the
reference data needed for the indicator validation of Criteria
1.5.1 and 2.2.1 exist unevenly: expert-labeled mapping sets
allowed the case-study ﬂag to be validated, but for whole-
product modeling only end-to-end reference values exist, so
component-level checks can be tested against product totals
but not against the component values they claim to assess.
Finally, the case study shows that the criteria can be opera-
tionalized on one provider’s deployed systems; independent
applications to other AI-CF systems would further test the
framework’s generality.

6 Conclusion
AI-assisted carbon footprinting is likely to become increas-
ingly important as companies seek to assess large numbers
of products, materials, and suppliers at a level of detail
that is difﬁcult to achieve through manual modeling alone.
However, in order for AI-CF systems to support meaningful
carbon accounting and decision-making, their outputs must
be reviewable, traceable, and appropriate for their intended
use. This requires evaluation practices that go beyond aggre-
gate performance metrics or isolated examples of apparently
successful outputs.
This paper proposes a framework for evaluating AI-CF
systems through a combination of system-level assessment
and material-level transparency. The system-level criteria
examine whether an AI-CF workﬂow applies appropriate
methods consistently across many cases, while material-level
criteria ensure that individual outputs expose the reason-
ing, data sources, assumptions, uncertainty indicators, and
quality signals needed for expert review. Together, these
two layers support a risk-based validation approach: review-
ers can examine the overall behavior of the system while
focusing detailed human attention on high-materiality items,
low-conﬁdence outputs, and components ﬂagged as uncer-
tain or weakly supported.
The case study illustrates how the framework can be oper-
ationalized in practice and it demonstrates how component-
level evaluation can reveal where an AI-CF workﬂow is
relatively stable and where additional data, methodological
guardrails, or human review are needed. In this sense, the
framework is diagnostic as much as evaluative: it helps iden-
tify speciﬁc weaknesses in AI-assisted carbon footprinting
workﬂows and provides a basis for iterative improvement.
The broader contribution of this work is to shift the
discussion from the binary endorsement or rejection of
AI-CF outputs toward a more nuanced yet practical ques-
tion: under what conditions, with what evidence, and for
what intended use can an AI-CF system be relied upon?
Answering that question requires transparent documentation,
benchmarked performance, uncertainty characterization, and
human oversight. As AI becomes more deeply embedded in
environmental assessment, such evaluation frameworks will
be necessary to ensure that automation improves the con-
sistency, scalability, and usefulness of carbon footprinting
without weakening the standards of scientiﬁc and profes-
sional review.
