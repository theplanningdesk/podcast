ABSTRACT
Motivated by paint oven on-off scheduling in an automotive plant, this study investigates the con-
trol of a single energy-intensive resource that can operate in on, off, warm-up, and multiple idle 
modes. The problem is to determine the energy mode of the resource over a finite planning horizon 
so as to minimise the average total cost of energy consumption and product waiting. We propose 
a data-driven on-off scheduling procedure that collects real-time upstream vehicle locations at the 
beginning of each shift, uses a digital twin to generate arrival scenarios conditional on these loca-
tions, and then determines the resource schedule. Two solution approaches are developed and used 
in parallel. The first is a scenario-based mathematical programming formulation strengthened by 
a scenario reduction technique based on clustering, and the second is a problem-specific genetic 
algorithm that encodes the resource schedule directly. Numerical experiments show that incorpo-
rating advance arrival information into multi-mode scheduling can reduce the normalised energy 
cost per vehicle by up to 41% relative to benchmark policies, with energy measured in equivalent 
on-mode operating minutes. The results highlight the value of digital-twin-based arrival information 
for energy-intensive manufacturing systems.

1. Introduction
In recent years, rising energy costs, stricter sustainability 
regulations, and growing environmental concerns have 
created a clear need to improve energy efficiency in 
the manufacturing industry, which is among the largest 
energy-consuming sectors worldwide (Duran, Ozturk, 
and O’Sullivan 2025). In response to this need, energy-
efficient production control policies have emerged as an 
important way to reduce energy use while preserving reli-
able and timely production. These policies decide when 
machines should operate, stay in a low-energy standby 
mode, or be switched off, so that energy is not wasted dur-
ing idle periods while production requirements are still 
met. Studies based on real industrial applications report 
that such control policies can reduce energy-related pro-
duction costs by about 20–30% (Gahm et al. 2016; Tan, 
Karabağ, and Khayyati 2023b), while also contributing 
to broader sustainability goals by reducing carbon diox-
ide emissions. In addition, advances in automation and 
information technologies, such as the Internet of Things

on this motivational context, our study aims to address 
two key research questions relevant for such manufac-
turers: (i) How can advance arrival information be lever-
aged to control an energy-intensive resource with multiple 
idle modes in a way that saves energy and meets produc-
tion requirements? (ii) T o what extent can energy savings 
be achieved by incorporating advance arrival information 
and multiple stand-by modes, compared with benchmark 
approaches such as threshold-based or always-on policies?
T o address the first research question, we propose a 
mathematical programming formulation that leverages 
estimated vehicle arrival times to determine the opti-
mal timing for activating the oven (setting it to opera-
tional temperature), selecting an intermediary tempera-
ture from a set of given options, or deactivating it entirely 
(cooling it to room temperature) in the planning hori-
zon. The expected arrival pattern at the oven changes as 
vehicles move through the upstream system, new vehicles 
enter, and random disruptions such as machine failures 
occur. At each predefined scheduling epoch, a digital 
twin of the production system is therefore initialised with 
the observed upstream system state, including the cur-
rent locations of vehicles. Stochastic replications of the 
digital twin then generate arrival-time scenarios condi-
tional on this state, capturing the uncertainty in sub-
sequent vehicle arrivals. The mathematical formulation 
uses these scenarios to determine a common oven sched-
ule, which remains fixed until the next scheduling epoch. 
The main contribution of this study lies in integrating 
state-dependent advance arrival information generated 
through a digital twin with multi-mode operational deci-
sions for the control of an energy-intensive resource. 
As the numbers of vehicles, scenarios, and operating 
modes increase, however, solving the formulation to opti-
mality may exceed the computation time available for 
operational decision-making. T o address this computa-
tional challenge, we develop a problem-specific genetic 
algorithm and a scenario-reduction approach based on 
clustering to obtain high-quality schedules within prac-
tical computation times.
Our mathematical formulation is capable of yielding 
results that are more precise and adaptive in terms of 
energy management, as it integrates predictive arrival 
information generated by a digital twin along with multi-
mode flexibility. In contrast, benchmark approaches such 
as threshold-based and always-on policies rely solely on 
the aggregate number of vehicles in the system, which 
limits their responsiveness to real-time variations. Corre-
spondingly, the comparison between the results of these 
benchmark strategies and those obtained from our math-
ematical model reveals how and under what conditions 
the incorporation of advance arrival information and 
multiple intermediate modes leads to notable energy sav-
ings and improved operational responsiveness, thereby 
allowing us to address the second research question. Our 
numerical analyses further show that the benefits of using 
advance arrival information are particularly pronounced 
when warm-up periods are long and costly and sys-
tem utilisation is high, with reductions of up to 41% in 
normalised energy cost per vehicle, measured in units 
equivalent to one minute of oven operation in the on 
mode, relative to the benchmark policies considered. T o 
the best of our knowledge, no prior study has incorpo-
rated this question into a formal modelling framework, 
which further reinforces the contribution of this study to 
the literature on energy-efficient production control.

2. Literature review
In the literature review, we focus on studies that exam-
ine on and off type energy control policies in production 
systems and assess their performance through analyti-
cal modelling approaches. More specifically, we organ-
ise the reviewed studies into two groups: those that

investigate such policies through Markovian Decision 
Process (MDP) and learning based methods, and those 
that examine them through simulation and digital twin 
implementations.
2.1. MDP and learning based studies on on and off 
type energy control policies
Several review studies discuss the state of the art and the 
evolution of research on energy aware production con-
trol, including Gahm et al. (2016), Römer (2021) and 
Bänsch et al. (2021). Within this broader literature, how-
ever, we focus specifically on studies that examine on and 
off type energy control policies in production systems 
and assess their performance through stochastic mod-
elling approaches, particularly Markovian models and 
learning based methods. In this stream, the literature can 
be broadly divided into two groups. The first group con-
siders single machine settings and develops structural 
insights for relatively compact systems. The second group 
examines multi machine or more complex production 
settings, where the main emphasis shifts toward per-
formance evaluation and control in higher dimensional 
environments.
A first group of studies examines single-machine 
production systems and primarily focuses on infinite-
horizon performance analysis rather than short-term, 
data-driven operational control. Within this group, Tan, 
Karabağ, and Khayyati (2023a) analyze the joint produc-
tion and energy mode control problem in a make-to-
stock system with multiple operational modes and expo-
nentially distributed inter-event times, and show through 
extensive numerical experiments that the optimal pol-
icy has an inventory-position-based threshold structure. 
Building on this result, Özkan and Tan (2025) provide 
an analytical proof of the optimality of this policy under 
heavy-traffic conditions. Extending this framework fur-
ther, Tan, Karabağ, and Khayyati (2023b) incorporate 
phase-type inter-event times and show that, unlike in 
the exponential case, the optimal policy follows a thresh-
old structure depending not only on the inventory posi-
tion but also on the phases of the inter-arrival process. 
Tan and Karabağ (2024) also study the deterministic 
counterparts of the stochastic models proposed in Tan, 
Karabağ, and Khayyati (2023a, 2023b) using a fluid mod-
elling framework. By deriving closed-form results from 
the deterministic setting, they develop an approximate 
policy that is shown to be effective for systems in which 
the production, warm-up, and demand processes have 
exponential or phase-type distributions.
A second group of studies addresses production set-
tings with multiple machines or richer system interac-
tions. Compared with the single-machine literature, this 
stream of research remains relatively limited and places 
greater emphasis on system-level performance evalua-
tion and control in larger state spaces. In the corre-
sponding group, Chen et al. (2012) provide one of the 
earliest applications of a Markovian approach to energy-
efficient production control by analyzing Bernoulli serial 
lines under machine start-ups and shut-downs, with 
a specific focus on automotive paint shop operations. 
W ang et al. (2017) incorporate real-time machine and 
buffer states into a Markovian framework to evaluate 
on/off policies, offering a rigorous basis for performance 
comparison. A few recent studies have also introduced 
learning-based methods to address high-dimensional or 
dynamically evolving production settings. For instance, 
Li et al. (2021) adopt a reinforcement learning approach 
for serial production systems and develop system-level 
energy control policies. Their analysis, yet, focuses on 
transient behaviour and does not address steady-state 
performance. Heydar, Mardaneh, and Loxton (2022) 
also develop an approximate dynamic programming 
approach for an energy-aware unrelated parallel machine 
scheduling problem with stochastic job arrivals, aiming 
to minimise a weighted combination of makespan and 
energy cost.
2.2. Simulation and digital twin studies on on and 
off type energy control policies
Complementing these analytical and learning-based 
studies, a few works rely on simulation-based approaches 
to investigate energy control policies in different produc-
tion settings. Such approaches become particularly use-
ful when the production environment involves complex 
interactions, operational constraints, or system dynamics 
that are difficult to capture in a tractable analytical model. 
As one of the earlier studies in this stream, W ang, Li, and 
Liu (2013) attempt to identify energy-saving windows 
for idle machines in serial production lines and demon-
strate that using these windows can substantially reduce 
energy consumption without compromising throughput. 
Su, Frigerio, and Matta (2016) examine a comparable 
production setting to that of W ang, Li, and Liu (2013), 
but focus on the performance of buffer-based on/off 
strategies under varying levels of information availabil-
ity. Renna (2018) studies pull-controlled production lines 
and develops a dynamic on/off policy that incorporates 
downstream buffer and demand variability information, 
showing that energy savings can be achieved without 
harming service performance. Recently, Frigerio and 
Matta (2019) and Frigerio, Tan, and Matta (2024) study 
a serial system with finite buffers and examine machine-
level and system-level energy control decisions, show-
ing how the simultaneous control of multiple machines

influences performance and providing exact benchmarks 
for evaluating alternative policies. A comparable prob-
lem setting in a parallel machine environment is also 
examined by Loffredo et al. (2024).
Beyond these simulation based studies, we also con-
sider digital twin applications in energy aware pro-
duction control. Although their use in this specific 
area is relatively recent, digital twins have been dis-
cussed and applied for a longer period in fields 
such as healthcare (Katsoulakis et al. 2024; Zheng 
et al. 2025), manufacturing (Kritzinger et al. 2018; Shao 
and Helu 2020), and maintenance (V an Dinter, T ekiner-
dogan, and Catal 2023). In manufacturing and energy 
applications, the concept has gradually evolved from a 
digital representation used mainly for monitoring and 
visualisation into a broader decision-support framework 
that integrates real-time data, predictive models, optimi-
sation, and, more recently, AI-driven adaptation. General 
discussions of this development and the conceptual foun-
dations of digital twins can be found in Yildiz, Møller, 
and Bilberg (2022) and Ivanov (2024, 2026). In the spe-
cific context of energy aware production control, studies 
that explicitly adopt a digital twin perspective are still 
relatively limited, although this stream has been grow-
ing steadily in recent years. An early contribution in a 
related direction is Zou et al. (2017), which develop a 
data driven modelling and real time distributed control 
framework for energy efficient manufacturing systems. 
Even though the study is not formulated explicitly as a 
digital twin application, it shows how real time system 
information can be integrated with control decisions to 
improve energy performance metrics. A more explicit 
digital twin application in this research stream is pre-
sented by Karanjkar et al. (2018), which study energy 
optimisation in a printed circuit board assembly line 
based on surface mount technology and uses a digi-
tal twin representation to evaluate alternative operating 
decisions. W ang, Huang, and Tang (2021) consider real 
time energy saving control in a serial production system 
and develop a digital twin simulator in which real time 
production data are transmitted to the digital model, can-
didate energy saving strategies are evaluated in the virtual 
environment, and the resulting control decisions are fed 
back to the physical system. Xia et al. (2023) integrate 
learning and real-time adaptation by developing a digital 
twin-based optimisation model that explicitly accounts 
for machine faults when making energy-related control 
decisions. Recently, Billey and Wuest (2024) and Kasper 
et al. (2024) examine energy digital twins in smart man-
ufacturing through a case study and highlight their role 
in monitoring, analysis, and decision support.
2.3. Contribution to the literature
Overall, this study differs from the ones reviewed above 
in several important respects. First, we develop a novel 
scenario-based mathematical programming model that 
explicitly integrates advance arrival information gener-
ated by a digital twin with multi-mode operational deci-
sions for the control of an energy-intensive resource. 
Second, unlike most studies that adopt infinite-horizon 
optimisation frameworks based on simulation or Markov 
decision process approaches, our formulation focuses on 
short-term (finite-horizon) operational control, which 
better aligns with real-time decision-making require-
ments in energy-intensive production environments. 
This integration and modelling perspective enable more 
adaptive and energy-efficient operational decisions com-
pared with traditional threshold-based or always-on con-
trol policies, thereby representing a major methodolog-
ical contribution of this study. Third, recognising the 
computational complexity of the proposed model aris-
ing from an increasing number of arrivals, scenarios, and 
operating modes, we develop heuristic approaches based 
on genetic and clustering algorithms that produce high-
quality solutions within practical computation times. T o 
the best of our knowledge, no prior study has attempted 
to develop such an approach in a comparable problem 
setting. Lastly, as far as we concerned, this is the first study 
to examine how the incorporation of multiple interme-
diate modes and advance arrival information leads to 
notable energy savings and improved operational respon-
siveness through a digital twin. This constitutes a practi-
cal contribution, offering guidance to practitioners and 
academics seeking to enhance the energy efficiency of 
real-world production systems, and by bridging theoreti-
cal modelling with implementable decision-support tools 
for energy-intensive operations.
3. Problem definition
In this section, we first describe the problem setting, 
including the dynamics and operational modes of the 
resource and the associated energy and waiting cost 
structure. W e then introduce the scenario-based rep-
resentation used to capture uncertainty in upstream 
arrivals.
3.1. Resource dynamics and operating modes
W e consider a dynamic control problem for implement-
ing energy-efficient production strategies for a resource 
that receives jobs from an upstream serial production 
system and is capable of operating in three core oper-
ational modes: the on mode, the warm-up mode, and 
INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF PRODUCTION RESEARCH 
 5
multiple standby modes. Specifically, the resource’ s oper-
ational mode is represented by m where m ∈ M =
{1, 2 . . ., M, O, W}. Each operational mode entails a dis-
tinct energy cost rate, reflecting the specific energy con-
sumption characteristics of that mode.
In the set of operational modes, m = O denotes the 
mode in which the resource is at its operational temper-
ature and able to actively process jobs, whereas m = 1 
denotes the mode in which the resource is cooled to 
ambient temperature and is completely shut down. That 
is, m = 1 corresponds to the resource’ s off mode. When 
the resource’ s mode is m = 1, that is, when it is in the 
off mode, a warm-up delay is required to reach the 
operational temperature upon a production decision. 
When m ∈ {2, 3, . . ., M}, it represents a standby mode in 
which the resource sustains the mth predefined interme-
diate temperature level. Similar to m = 1, these standby 
modes also require a warm-up delay before produc-
tion starts. For all standby and off modes, i.e. m ∈ ˇM =
{1, 2, 3,. . ., M}, the lower the index, the lower the tem-
perature level maintained by the resource, and conse-
quently, the longer the warm-up time needed for it to 
resume operation.
Of these operational modes, m = W denotes the 
warm-up mode, which is activated when the resource 
transitions either from m = 1, namely the off mode, to 
m = O, or from an intermediate standby mode m ∈ ˇM \
{1} to m = O. As the warm-up processes of industrial 
resources are typically governed by standardised heat-
ing protocols and inherent physical characteristics, their 
durations remain highly consistent; thus, the variability 
in heating time to reach the operational mode is generally 
negligible. Accordingly, in our model, the warm-up delay 
required when transitioning from any m where m ∈ ˇM to 
m = O is assumed to be deterministic and strictly greater 
than zero (for a similar assumption, see, e.g. Mashaei and 
Lennartson 2012; Tan and Karabağ 2024). This delay is 
represented as tm > 0, denoting the required warm-up 
time for the corresponding operational mode m where 
m ∈ ˇM. For some processes, particularly those involv-
ing heating, as in our case, the cost rate of being in the 
warm-up mode may even exceed that of the on mode, 
since raising the resource to its operational temperature 
requires a temporary surge of energy that can surpass 
the steady-state consumption during regular production 
operations.
With respect to the transition structure, transitions are 
allowed from m = O to any mode m ∈ ˇM and from any 
mode m ∈ ˇM to m = O. Y et, transitions among differ-
ent standby modes are not allowed, because the energy 
required to raise the resource from one standby temper-
ature level to a higher one would in any case be expended 
when bringing it fully back to the operational state, i.e. 
the m = O mode. Interrupting this process at an inter-
mediate temperature level and then reheating it to the 
operational temperature would therefore be suboptimal. 
This is analytically proven under mild conditions, and 
the corresponding results are presented the following 
sections.
3.2. Cost structure
The energy cost rates associated with the on (m = O) and 
warm-up (m = W) modes are denoted by eO and eW, 
respectively, whereas the energy cost rate for a standby 
mode m is denoted by em, where m ∈ ˇM. As full opera-
tion engages all energy-demanding subsystems and most 
subsystems remain deactivated in standby modes, the 
energy consumption in the on mode is typically higher 
than in any mode m ∈ ˇM. Accordingly, it is assumed that 
eO > em for all m ∈ ˇM. When the resource is in the on 
mode, it can transition either to a standby mode or be 
completely shut down, with a cooling delay that depends 
on the target mode. During this cooling process, the man-
ufacturer incurs no additional cost, as no active energy 
input is required. Hence, the cooling process is not repre-
sented as a separate mode in the model, since its inclusion 
would merely enlarge the state space without providing 
any additional explanatory power. When the resource 
is completely shut down, namely m = 1, its energy con-
sumption is limited to a few essential functions, such as 
maintaining network connectivity, supporting minimal 
monitoring, and safety functions. Therefore, the energy 
cost rate associated with the off mode, e1, is assumed to be 
lower than those of all other modes, that is, eO, eW, em >
e1 where m ∈ ˇM \ {1}. Furthermore, no specific relation-
ship between eO and eW is imposed in our model, so we 
allow both eO > eW and eO ≤ eW.
The costs associated with warm-up, operation, and 
standby are assumed to be linear in the time spent in the 
corresponding modes over the planning horizon under 
consideration. Accordingly, the energy-related cost of 
each mode is defined as the product of its mode-specific 
cost rate and the corresponding time spent in that mode, 
while the total energy cost is obtained by summing these 
mode-specific costs. This representation is consistent 
with the physical setting, since the oven consumes energy 
continuously while operating in each mode. It is also 
supported by empirical evidence on time-based energy 
consumption in manufacturing systems (e.g. Frigerio and 
Matta 2014; W einert, Chiotellis, and Seliger 2011) and 
aligns with related analytical studies that model energy 
use through mode-dependent cost rates (e.g. Frigerio, 
Tan, and Matta 2024; Karabağ 2026; Loffredo et al. 2024).
In addition to energy costs, we associate a penalty with 
the time each job spends waiting for processing in order

to discourage excessive waiting and promote a smoother 
flow through the system. This penalty, denoted by e1, 
represents the per-unit-time cost of keeping a job in the 
system before it is processed. In other words, e1 serves 
as a policy parameter that reflects the relative impor-
tance assigned to waiting versus energy savings. A higher 
value of e1 places greater emphasis on completing the 
required production within a shorter time span, whereas 
a lower value of e1 allows the same production target 
to be completed over a longer period and thereby cre-
ates more room for energy saving opportunities. Thus, 
e1 governs the trade off between production respon-
siveness and energy efficiency. Incorporating this penalty 
into the model allows us to account explicitly for the eco-
nomic effect of waiting while aligning production control 
decisions with both energy efficiency and operational 
performance objectives. Section 8.1 presents numerical 
experiments showing that e1 can be used effectively to 
achieve significant reductions in energy consumption 
while generating only limited operational inefficiencies.
3.3. Upstream arrival process and scenarios
It is assumed that the jobs to be processed by the resource 
arrive from an upstream serial production system and 
therefore reach the resource only after completing a 
sequence of upstream operations. As a result, their arrival 
process reflects the stochastic evolution of the upstream 
system. V ariability in processing times, machine failures, 
blocking, congestion, and other operational disturbances 
in upstream stages may all affect job completion times 
and hence the arrival epochs at the resource. Therefore, 
the exact arrival times of jobs at the resource cannot be 
known with certainty in advance. This arrival uncertainty 
is central to the control problem, because the effective-
ness of a given operating schedule depends on how well 
the selected operating modes are aligned with the realised 
arrival pattern at the resource. If arrivals occur later than 
expected, keeping the resource in a high energy mode 
may lead to unnecessary energy consumption. By con-
trast, if arrivals occur earlier than expected or if multiple 
jobs arrive within a short time interval, jobs may expe-
rience additional waiting before being processed. The 
model must therefore account explicitly for uncertainty 
in upstream driven arrivals.
T o achieve this, we adopt a scenario-based representa-
tion in which the uncertainty in job arrivals is captured 
through a set of alternative arrival scenarios generated by 
the digital twin developed in this study. Each scenario 
represents one possible realisation of job arrivals to the 
resource in a system consisting of an upstream serial pro-
duction line followed by the resource at its downstream 
end. Formally, each scenario s ∈ S = {1, 2, . . ., S} speci-
fies the arrival time of job j ∈ J = {1, 2, . . ., J}, denoted 
by aj,s ≥ 0, together with its corresponding processing 
time at the resource, denoted by dj,s > 0. This formu-
lation allows us to evaluate alternative schedules under 
different realisations of the arrival process and thereby 
incorporate upstream uncertainty directly into the math-
ematical model.
4. Mathematical model and solution approach 
overview
In this section, we present the mathematical model and 
the proposed solution approach. W e first formulate the 
finite horizon control problem under scenario based 
upstream arrival uncertainty. W e then outline the solu-
tion approach used to solve the resulting scheduling 
problem.
4.1. Mathematical model
W e study the problem of controlling an energy-intensive 
resource by using information from upstream processes 
to reduce both energy consumption and the waiting time 
of parts in the system. Under a first-come-first-served 
processing rule, a production control schedule is rep-
resented by ψ = {(Mc, Uc, Wc) : c ∈ C = {1, 2, . . ., C}}, 
where Mc denotes the energy mode selected in cycle c, 
Uc denotes the start time of the warm-up process, and Wc
denotes the time at which the resource stops in that cycle, 
as illustrated in Figure 1. That is, the schedule deter-
mines only the timing of the resource modes and does not 
alter the first-come-first-served processing sequence or 
the assignment of individual jobs. Consequently, changes 
in the resource-mode schedule across successive schedul-
ing epochs do not require the operational rearrangements 
typically associated with revisions to job-level sched-
ules and therefore do not give rise to the corresponding 
schedule-adjustment costs. So, the objective is to deter-
mine the optimal schedule ψ that minimises the average 
total cost TC(ψ), comprising the average energy and

4.2. Solution approach overview
Having introduced the scenario-based representation of 
upstream arrival uncertainty, we now explain how these 
scenarios are generated and used in the solution proce-
dure. The digital twin plays a central role at this stage by 
transforming real time information from the upstream

system into scenario inputs for the scheduling problem. 
Specifically, it uses the current locations of jobs in the 
upstream system to generate alternative arrival scenar-
ios, denoted by aj,s for j ∈ J = {1, 2, . . ., J} and s ∈ S =
{1, 2, . . ., S}. Jobs located farther from the oven must 
pass through more upstream stages and are therefore 
exposed to more potential disruptions before reaching 
the resource. Their arrival times consequently tend to 
exhibit greater variability, as illustrated in Figure 2. The 
digital twin captures this relationship by using real-time 
job locations to generate arrival-time scenarios condi-
tional on the observed upstream system state.
Using these scenario inputs, we construct the resource 
schedule at the beginning of each shift. In particular, 
we first access the real time locations of upstream jobs, 
then construct arrival scenarios from this information, 
and finally determine the schedule that minimises the 
average total cost over the planning horizon. In this 
way, the digital twin provides the information layer of 
the approach, enabling the scheduling model to account 
for location-dependent arrival uncertainty when deter-
mining the oven schedule over the planning horizon. 
In practice, however, the digital twin may not cover the 
entire upstream system. As a result, low-variability arrival 
estimates may be available only for a subset of the jobs 
in J, while the remaining jobs may have arrival esti-
mates based only on the time at which they entered the 
plant and therefore exhibit higher variability. T o study 
the implications of this limited visibility, we assume 
that reliable arrival time estimates are available for jobs 
{1, 2, . . ., K}, and we refer to K ≤ J as the visibility win-
dow. As K increases, more real time information becomes 
available to the scheduling model. The effect of increasing 
the visibility window is also discussed in Section 8.2.
Once the scenario set has been constructed, we apply 
two solution approaches to the resulting scheduling prob-
lem: solving the MILP formulation with a commercial 
solver and using a problem-specific genetic algorithm. In 
practice, the time available to generate the schedule may 
be limited. For example, the motivating application may 
allow approximately 10 minutes to compute a schedule 
before the start of a shift using the latest available sys-
tem information. Both solution methods are therefore 
run in parallel under the same time limit, and the feasi-
ble schedule with the lowest average total cost is selected 
for implementation. The selected schedule remains fixed 
throughout the shift and is not revised in response to 
operational data updates during that period. At the next 
predefined scheduling epoch, updated information on 
upstream job locations is used to generate a new sce-
nario set, and the same solution procedure is repeated to 
determine the schedule for the subsequent shift. Figure 3 
depicts this process. Note that for the MILP approach, we 
use the full scenario set when it can be handled within the 
available computation time. If the number of scenarios 
makes the full formulation too computationally demand-
ing, we apply clustering to obtain a smaller set of repre-
sentative scenarios and solve the corresponding reduced 
formulation. T o ensure a consistent comparison between 
the two solution approaches, schedules obtained using 
the reduced set are evaluated over the original scenario 
set. The details of the digital twin, the mathematical pro-
gramming formulation, the genetic algorithm, and the 
clustering approach are given in the following sections.
5. Digital twin
This section introduces the digital twin framework used 
to support scheduling decisions in a stochastic pro-
duction environment. W e begin by presenting the sys-
tem model and operational baselines, then describe the 
design of the digital twin and the generation of state-
dependent arrival scenarios. The section concludes with 
the scenario reduction procedure, namely the clustering 
approach, employed to ensure computational tractability.
5.1. System model and operational baselines
In this section, we describe the digital twin developed in 
this study, including the underlying system representa-
tion and the main elements of its implementation. W e 
model the upstream of the ovens as a serial production 
line in which each station is represented as a delay block 
with finite capacity and an infinite number of servers, i.e. 
the number of servers at each station equals its maximum

capacity. Processing times at each station are determinis-
tic. However, machine breakdowns introduce stochastic-
ity through random up and down times. The serial line is 
subject to blocking after completion of service, and delays 
across stations may range from a few minutes to up to two 
hours because of machine breakdowns, blocking, conges-
tion, and other operational disruptions in the upstream 
system. In addition, vehicle colour, classified as either 
special or regular and known upon entry into the system, 
is also random. On average, one quarter of the vehi-
cles require processing in the oven for special colours. 
For the scheduling problem considered here, however, 
the relevant uncertainty does not lie in the final colour 
assignment itself, but in the realised composition, tim-
ing, and sequence of the vehicles reaching the specialised 
oven. In other words, while vehicle colour determines 
whether a vehicle requires the specialised oven, the main 
source of uncertainty for the scheduling problem is when 
such vehicles arrive and in what order they arrive from 
the upstream system.
W e consider two types of threshold-based con-
trol policies as the benchmark for this system. These 
threshold-based policies are implemented in the digital 
twin so that system performance under the benchmark 
policies can be evaluated and compared with the per-
formance obtained using the schedules generated by the 
proposed solution approach. The first is a buffer-based 
threshold policy, under which the oven is turned on when 
the number of vehicles in the system exceeds a speci-
fied upper threshold. Once activated, the oven continues 
processing vehicles until the system inventory falls below 
a second (lower) threshold, at which point the oven is 
turned off. The second policy is a time-based thresh-
old policy. Under this approach, the oven is turned on 
for a fixed duration (the first threshold), then turned 
off and kept idle for another fixed duration (the second 
threshold).
5.2. Digital twin design
T o generate stochastic arrival scenarios without using 
current system information, a traditional offline simula-
tion can be constructed by fitting the job arrival process 
to historical data and simulating S realisations of the fit-
ted process to obtain scenario-specific arrival times aj,s. 
These scenarios can then be supplied to the scheduling 
model to determine the oven schedule {(Mc, Uc, Wc) :
c ∈ 1, 2, . . ., C} over a finite planning horizon. How-
ever, reusing the fitted process across successive planning 
intervals without conditioning on the current production 
state means that the generated scenarios do not explicitly 
account for changes in the upstream system. For exam-
ple, the same fitted arrival-time distribution may be used 
regardless of current vehicle locations or operating con-
ditions. Such an offline approach therefore cannot exploit 
current system information to represent the arrival pat-
tern expected during a particular planning interval. This 
limitation motivates generating arrival scenarios condi-
tional on the current production state.
The key distinction between the offline approach 
described above and the digital twin used in this study 
lies not in the use of simulation itself, but in how the 
simulation is initialised and used to support scheduling 
decisions. The digital twin is initialised with the most 
recent information on vehicle locations, resource states, 
and buffer contents. As illustrated in Figure 2, vehicle 
location is particularly informative because it determines 
how much of the upstream production line a vehicle must 
still traverse before reaching the oven. V ehicles closer to

the oven are therefore exposed to fewer potential disrup-
tions and generally have lower arrival-time uncertainty, 
whereas vehicles farther upstream remain exposed to 
more processing stages, machine failures, blocking, and 
congestion. Using the observed system state as its initial 
condition, the digital twin generates arrival-time scenar-
ios that capture these differences in uncertainty. These 
scenarios are then supplied to the scheduling model, 
allowing the oven schedule to reflect the current produc-
tion configuration.
T o implement this state-dependent scenario-
generation approach, we develop a digital twin of the 
upstream production system, represented as a serial line 
with finite buffer capacities and unreliable stations. Each 
vehicle entering the first upstream station is either a 
special- or a regular-colour vehicle according to known 
probabilities. The subsequent evolution of the upstream 
system is therefore affected by both the stochastic com-
position of arriving vehicles and operational disruptions 
such as station breakdowns, blocking, and congestion. 
Because the arrival scenarios must reflect the current pro-
duction state, the digital twin is synchronised with the 
physical system at predefined synchronisation epochs. 
At each synchronisation epoch t, the current configu-
ration of the upstream system is observed and used to 
initialise the digital twin. This observed state includes 
the positions of jobs along the line feeding the oven, the 
operational status of the stations, the buffer contents, and 
other factors that may affect future arrival times. Condi-
tional on this observed state, let Aj denote the random 
time from t until job j reaches the oven.
Using the observed system state at epoch t as the 
common initial condition, the digital twin generates S
stochastic realisations of the subsequent evolution of the 
upstream system. Each realisation s yields an arrival time 
aj,s for each job j, corresponding to one realisation of Aj. 
These scenario-specific arrival times are then supplied to 
the scheduling model, which determines the oven sched-
ule {(Mc, Uc, Wc) : c ∈ {1, 2, . . ., C}} that minimises the 
expected total cost TC over the planning interval from 
t until the next synchronisation epoch. The resulting 
schedule remains fixed throughout this interval. At the 
next synchronisation epoch, the newly observed sys-
tem state is used to reinitialise the digital twin, generate 
a new set of arrival-time scenarios conditional on the 
updated system configuration, and determine a new oven 
schedule by solving the same expected-cost minimisa-
tion problem for the subsequent planning interval. In this 
way, successive schedules reflect changes in the upstream 
production state while no rescheduling is performed 
between synchronisation epochs.
5.3. Scenario reduction using clustering
Accounting for stochasticity in complex optimisation 
models often requires considering many scenarios, each 
corresponding to a set of realisations of the random vari-
ables. Since the system dynamics largely depend on these 
realisations, incorporating scenarios generally requires 
replicating a subset of the binary variables for each sce-
nario, which can make the resulting formulation difficult 
to solve with commercial solvers. In digital-twin-based 
scheduling settings, this issue becomes particularly rele-
vant when the digital twin generates a large number of 
state dependent scenarios at each decision epoch, while 
the optimisation problem must still be solved within 
short computational times. If only a small number of sce-
narios is sufficient to represent the relevant uncertainty, 
the benefit of scenario reduction is naturally limited. 
However, when a richer scenario set is needed to cap-
ture system variability with adequate fidelity, the mathe-
matical formulation can quickly become computationally 
demanding. In such cases, clustering can provide a prac-
tical way to preserve the main structure of uncertainty 
while reducing the computational burden. Many scenario 
reduction methods rely on clustering to group scenarios 
so that those within the same group have a similar impact 
on the problem (Grass et al. 2023). In this work, we treat 
arrival times as features for each scenario. This is mainly 
due to the fact that the processing times in the oven have 
very low stochasticity, and the main effect of the stochas-
ticity in the system is due to the arrival times. Hence, the 
distance between for any given two scenarios s1 and s2 is 
calculated as the euclidean distance ∑︁J j=1(as1,j − as2,j)2. 
The scenarios are then clustered using this distance. W e 
adopt the standard K-means algorithm because of its 
computational efficiency. The K-means algorithm itera-
tively updates the centres of the clusters and the assign-
ments of the scenarios to the clustering. For a detailed 
exposition of the algorithm, we refer the reader to Reddy 
and Aggarwal (2018).

Equation (2c) imposes a positivity constraint on the 
warm-up times, ensuring that each transition from a 
standby mode to the on mode requires a strictly positive 
duration. Equation (2d) specifies the warm-up time of 
each cycle according to its corresponding standby mode. 
Since the system starts in the off mode, Equation (2e) 
enforces that the warm-up time of the first cycle cor-
responds to that of standby mode m= 1. Equation (2f) 
specifies that if a cycle is not used, the standby mode is 
m = 1 for the next cycle. Equation (2g) enforces the logi-
cal sequencing of operations by ensuring that the oven is 
switched to the off mode in a cycle only after it has first 
been switched to the on mode. Equation (2h) ensures that 
if, in scenario s, vehicle j is assigned to cycle c, its pro-
cessing begins only after the oven start time of that cycle. 
Equation (2i) implies operational coherence by requiring 
that vehicle processing may begin only after the corre-
sponding vehicle has arrived. Equation (2j) specifies that, 
for each vehicle j in scenario s, the completion time is 
equal to the sum of its start time and processing time.
Equation (2k) imposes a first-come-first-served pol-
icy, ensuring that vehicles are processed sequentially one 
at a time in their order of arrival. Note that the proposed 
formulation does not optimise the processing sequence 
of vehicles. Instead, vehicles are indexed according to 
their realised arrival order in each scenario, and Con-
straint (2k) enforces this order. The scheduling decisions 
concern the timing of the oven’ s operating modes and 
cycles, through variables such as Uc, Vc, Wc, Yc,m, and Zc, 
together with the assignment of vehicles to these cycles

In what follows, we outline our solution approach 
to the proposed scenario-based mathematical program-
ming model, including relevant details. In particular, we 
first examine the feasibility and computational limits of 
solving the model directly with commercial solvers. W e 
then establish structural properties of the optimal solu-
tion and, based on these insights, propose several valid 
inequalities that can strengthen the formulation.
6.1. Computational limits of the mathematical 
model
The proposed mathematical model can be solved directly 
with commercial solvers for small-scale instances, typi-
cally in under a minute, consisting of up to 60 jobs, 5 sce-
narios, and 3 operating modes. However, as the problem 
scales, it quickly becomes computationally intractable 
due to its mixed-integer structure. Specifically, the for-
mulation consists of (C × J × S) + (C × M) + (2 × J ×
S) + (7 × C) + 1 variables in total, of which (C × J ×
S) + (C × M) + C are binary and (2 × J × S) + (6 ×
C) + 1 are continuous, along with (2 × C × J × S) +
(C × M) + (4 × J × S) + (11 × C) − S − 1 constraints.
This rapid combinatorial growth in both decision vari-
ables and constraints poses significant challenges for 
exact optimisation, despite recent advances in state-of-
the-art commercial solvers. In our case study, approxi-
mately 100 vehicles are processed daily, which implies 
that the solution approach must efficiently handle prob-
lem instances of this scale while accounting for a large 
number of scenarios to adequately represent system ran-
domness. For instance, a problem with 100 vehicles, 5 
cycles, and 100 scenarios results in nearly 500,000 binary 
decision variables. On a machine equipped with an AMD 
Ryzen 9 PRO CPU 4.00 GHz and 32 GB of RAM, using 
CPLEX Optimiser (version 22.1.2), such an instance can 
not be solved within one hour. This demonstrates that 
solving the model at this scale with commercial solvers 
is not feasible within practical time limits, as the solution 
space expands exponentially and overwhelms available 
computational resources.
Overall, all these observations further underscore the 
importance of leveraging problem structure, incorporat-
ing valid inequalities, and employing heuristic methods 
to obtain high-quality solutions for large-scale problem 
instances encountered in practice.

7. Approximate solution using a heuristic 
algorithm
T o utilise advance arrival information, the digital twin 
generates arrival-time scenarios based on the system state 
observed at a designated scheduling epoch. These scenar-
ios are then used to determine the oven schedule for the 
subsequent planning interval, and the resulting schedule 
remains fixed until the next scheduling epoch. There-
fore, the scenario set must capture the uncertainty in 
how the upstream system may evolve over this interval. 
In the motivating paint-shop setting, vehicle positions 
and production conditions may change over relatively 
short periods, and operational data are updated approx-
imately every ten minutes. These data updates do not 
trigger rescheduling. The oven schedule is recomputed 
only at predefined decision epochs, such as the begin-
ning of each shift. Accordingly, the scenario set gener-
ated at each decision epoch must represent the range of 
arrival patterns that may arise before the next schedule 
is determined. Representing this uncertainty adequately 
may require a large number of scenarios. As the num-
ber of scenarios increases, the size and computational 
burden of the scenario-based mathematical program also 
increase, which may prevent exact optimisation from 
generating a schedule within the available computation 
time. This computational limitation motivates the devel-
opment of the problem-specific genetic algorithm, which 
provides high-quality schedules for larger scenario sets 
within practical time limits.
Before describing the problem-specific implementa-
tion, we briefly outline the general logic of the genetic 
algorithm used in this study. The genetic algorithm main-
tains a population of candidate solutions, each associated 
INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF PRODUCTION RESEARCH 
 15
with a fitness value, and improves this population itera-
tively (Mitchell 1998). The population at each iteration is 
referred to as a generation. At each iteration, candidate 
solutions are evaluated based on their objective func-
tion values. A stochastic selection mechanism is then 
used to remove a subset of the population, with lower-
quality solutions having a higher probability of removal. 
The surviving solutions are modified through mutation 
and used to generate new candidate solutions that replace 
the discarded ones. This process continues until a stop-
ping criterion is met, which is typically defined as either 
a computational time limit or a maximum number of 
iterations.
Although this general structure provides the basis of 
the solution approach, it cannot be applied directly to 
the full mathematical formulation without further adap-
tation. More specifically, in the present problem, directly 
applying a generic genetic algorithm to the full math-
ematical formulation would be inefficient. The formu-
lation contains a large number of highly interdepen-
dent variables, including cycle-level decisions, vehicle-
to-cycle assignments, and scenario-dependent start and 
finish times. If all these variables were encoded directly in 
a chromosome, most randomly generated chromosomes 
would be infeasible, and even small mutations would 
frequently violate the temporal sequencing and assign-
ment constraints of the model. Therefore, the proposed 
algorithm exploits the structure of the problem by encod-
ing only the cycle-level scheduling decisions that define 
the oven schedule. More specifically, each chromosome 
represents a candidate schedule through the standby 
mode, warm-up start time, and on-mode finishing time 
of each cycle. V ehicle-level decisions are not encoded 
explicitly. Instead, once a candidate schedule is specified, 
a dedicated evaluation procedure reconstructs the cor-
responding processing start and finish times under each 
scenario and computes the resulting objective value. This 
design substantially reduces the dimension of the search 
space while preserving the operational logic of the orig-
inal mathematical model. For example, in an instance 
with 100 vehicles, 100 scenarios, and 5 cycles, a direct 
encoding of the full formulation would require more 
than 50,000 decision components, whereas the proposed 
encoding requires only 15 components. The algorithm is 
also executed for a fixed number of cycles. This choice 
is motivated by preliminary experiments showing that 
treating the number of cycles as a mutable component 
of the chromosome leads to weak search performance. 
Therefore, instead of encoding the number of cycles as 
part of the chromosome, we determine it through an 
exhaustive search. The initialisation procedure also uses 
structural information from the problem, particularly the 
requirement that the total on-mode time must be at least 
as large as the maximum total processing requirement 
across scenarios. Overall, the proposed algorithm is not 
a direct application of a generic genetic algorithm to the 
full variable space; rather, it is a problem-specific heuris-
tic that combines compact cycle-level encoding with a 
fast schedule evaluation procedure.
In particular, given a specified number of cycles, a new 
chromosome is generated by first assigning a random 
standby mode to each cycle. Subsequently, the warm-up 
start times Uc are drawn randomly in increasing order, 
ensuring Uc < Uc+1. Next, the total processing require-
ment is randomly allocated across the cycles. For each 
cycle, the completion time Wc is then computed as the 
corresponding warm-up start time Uc plus the mode-
dependent warm-up duration and the allocated process-
ing time. On the other hand, in each iteration, chromo-
somes can undergo mutation. T o perform a mutation, 
one cycle is first selected at random. Then, the type of 
mutation is determined randomly. Mutations may affect 
Mc, Uc, or Wc. If the mutation affects Mc, the standby 
mode of the selected cycle is replaced with a randomly 
selected standby mode. If it affects either Uc or Wc, the 
corresponding value is adjusted upward or downward 
by a random amount drawn from a continuous uni-
form distribution. After any modification is applied to a 
chromosome, it must be verified that the resulting chro-
mosome remains feasible. This is done by performing 
a sequence of feasibility checks and applying corrective 
adjustments when necessary. Whenever a correction is 
made, all checks are repeated to confirm that no new vio-
lations have been introduced. The checks include: (i) ver-
ifying that Uc and Wc are positive and strictly increasing 
in c, which keeps all warm-up start times and cycle com-
pletion times nonnegative and properly ordered, and (ii) 
verifying that Uc+1 ≥ Wc for all c, so that the warm-up 
of a new cycle never begins before the previous cycle has 
finished, thereby preventing infeasible overlaps between 
consecutive cycles.
The genetic algorithm also requires a measure, namely 
the fitness value or objective function value, to evaluate 
candidate chromosomes and eliminate those that per-
form poorly, allowing computational effort to be focussed 
on more promising solutions. T o compute the objec-
tive function value for a chromosome {(Mc, Uc, Wc) : c ∈
C}, all remaining problem variables must first be deter-
mined. Specifically, the variables that are independent of 
the scenario can be calculated as follows. The end of the 
warm-up period in each cycle, Vc, is obtained by adding 
the mode-dependent warm-up duration corresponding 
to the cycle’ s standby mode, Mc, to its warm-up start 
time, Uc. The start time of the standby mode in each 
cycle, Ic, is determined by adding the cool-down time 
required for the corresponding standby mode, σMc, to

8. Numerical experiments
In this section, we examine the value of using the digital 
twin by comparing the proposed method with two widely 
used benchmark policies: the always-on policy and the 
threshold policy. Although these benchmarks are simple 
to implement, they do not use advance information on 
future arrivals. By contrast, the proposed method uses 
arrival information generated from real-time upstream 
vehicle locations, enabling oven schedules to anticipate 
scenario-dependent arrivals. This comparison allows us 
to quantify the operational value of this information in 
terms of energy savings and the associated waiting-time 
trade-off. W e then extend the analysis to consider the role 
of predictive accuracy by considering a visibility window 
K, within which arrival times are known precisely using 
the live information from the digital twin, while those 
beyond are estimated only using the time the vehicle has 
entered the shop. This setting enables us to examine how 
varying degrees of information availability impact oper-
ational efficiency and to determine the conditions under 
which increased visibility yields substantial benefits. Fur-
thermore, to address the computational challenges of 
solving the exact model for large-scale instances involv-
ing many vehicles and scenarios, we develop heuristic 
approaches based on genetic algorithms and clustering 
techniques. Their performance is compared against the 
MILP results to evaluate both efficiency and solution 
quality. Finally, we analyze how key system parameters, 
such as the number of vehicles, scenarios, and opera-
tional modes, etc., affect the computational performance 
of the MILP model.
8.1. Impact of using advance arrival information 
from the digital twin on energy consumption and 
system performance
T o examine the value of advance arrival information for 
energy performance, we compare the proposed MILP-
based solution with two widely used benchmark strate-
gies: the always-on policy and the threshold-based pol-
icy. These benchmarks are commonly used in indus-
try and the academic literature because of their ease 
of implementation and intuitive appeal (see, e.g. Frige-
rio, Tan, and Matta 2024; Karabağ 2026; Khayyati and 
Tan 2020; Loffredo et al. 2024). Under the always-on
policy, the oven is switched on at the beginning of the 
planning horizon, typically at the start of a shift or day, 
and remains in the operational mode until all jobs are 
processed. Under the threshold-based policy, the oven 
changes mode according to predefined thresholds based 
either on the number of vehicles in the system or on 
the elapsed time since the last mode change. Accord-
ingly, we consider two variants of the threshold-based 
policy, namely buffer-based and time-based policies. In 
the buffer-based variant, the oven is switched on when 
the number of vehicles in the system exceeds an upper 
threshold and is switched off when it falls below a lower 
threshold. In the time-based variant, the oven alternates 
between on and off periods according to two time thresh-
olds. In both variants, the threshold values are obtained 
by searching over the corresponding two-dimensional 
threshold space. Since the buffer-based policy outper-
forms the time-based policy in all problem instances 
considered, we use it as the threshold-based benchmark 
in the comparative analyses.
This comparison is designed to separate the value of 
advance arrival information from the value of simple 
reactive control rules. The benchmark policies repre-
sent simple decision rules that operate without access 
to advance information on future arrivals, whereas the 
MILP model captures an idealised setting in which

9. Conclusions
Motivated by a real case study from a large automo-
tive manufacturer, this study investigates the problem 
of controlling energy-intensive operations in manufac-
turing environments where part arrival times are par-
tially predictable via a digital twin and multiple standby 
modes with different warm-up characteristics are avail-
able. W e propose a data-driven solution framework that 
leverages real-time upstream information and predictive 
arrival scenarios generated by a digital twin within a 
finite-horizon optimisation setting to construct energy-
efficient resource schedules at the beginning of each 
shift and minimise the total average cost over the plan-
ning horizon. The proposed framework operates by col-
lecting real-time system information, generating arrival 
scenarios through the digital twin, and determining an 
energy-aware schedule that remains fixed until the next 
predefined scheduling epoch. Two solution approaches 
are employed in parallel: a scenario-based mathematical 
programming formulation strengthened with a scenario-
reduction technique based on the proposed clustering 
method, and a tailored genetic algorithm that directly 
encodes the system schedule. The schedule to be imple-
mented is then selected by comparing the results of the 
two approaches within a given time limit based on their 
corresponding objective values, thereby balancing solu-
tion quality and computational tractability. This process, 
which consists of collecting the current locations of the 
vehicles from the shop floor, using the digital twin with 
the updated locations to generate arrival scenarios, deter-
mining the oven schedule, and implementing the sched-
ule, is repeated at each predefined scheduling epoch.
Extensive numerical experiments based on real indus-
trial parameters demonstrate the effectiveness of the pro-
posed framework and provide several insights for both 
practitioners and academicians. The results show that 
exploiting predictive information generated by the digital 
twin can lead to substantially lower energy consump-
tion than both always-on and threshold-based bench-
mark policies. While in infinite-horizon settings with 
highly variable arrival time patterns, simple threshold-
based policies are often near-optimal, the results indicate 
that in finite-horizon environments with low to moderate 
arrival variability, such policies can be significantly out-
performed by predictive scheduling strategies. In partic-
ular, when reliable arrival time predictions are available, 
the production schedule can exploit low-traffic periods 
by switching the resource to low-energy modes, thereby 
achieving substantial energy savings. This highlights the 
value of anticipative, data-driven control policies enabled 
by digital twins in contrast to purely reactive state-based 
rules.
The numerical analysis further shows that the addi-
tional waiting required to achieve non-negligible energy 
savings remains limited and does not cause significant 
disruptions to downstream operations. In particular, tol-
erating an approximately 35% increase in average wait-
ing time per vehicle can yield up to a 41% reduc-
tion in normalised energy cost per vehicle, measured 
in units equivalent to one minute of oven operation in 
the on mode. This is critical from an operational per-
spective, as it indicates that meaningful reductions in 
energy use can be achieved without compromising ser-
vice levels, throughput, or production stability. More-
over, recognising that full digital twin coverage of the 
entire production system may not always be feasible 
in practice, we demonstrate that even partial upstream 
visibility already provides highly valuable information. 
Limited visibility windows are sufficient to support effec-
tive energy-aware scheduling decisions, thereby lower-
ing technological and organisational barriers to adoption 
and enabling incremental implementation strategies in
practice.
From a computational and methodological perspec-
tive, the study shows that high-quality schedules can be 
generated within practical time limits, even for large-
scale, realistic problem instances. The scenario-based 
formulation provides a flexible modelling framework 
for capturing stochastic arrivals, multi-mode operational 
decisions, and finite-horizon control requirements. The 
proposed structural properties and valid inequalities 
yield insights into the nature of optimal schedules and 
significantly strengthen the mathematical formulation. 
The numerical results further show that the tailored 
genetic algorithm is capable of identifying near-optimal 
solutions in instances where optimal solutions can be 
computed and that the combined use of valid inequalities 
and scenario reduction significantly outperforms direct 
solution of the original formulation, both in terms of 
computation time and scalability, while preserving high 
solution quality. These findings indicate that advanced 
optimisation techniques can be effectively embedded in 
real-time or near-real-time industrial decision-support 
systems provided that problem structure is carefully 
exploited and appropriate approximation strategies are 
employed.
Overall, the results highlight several important impli-
cations for both practitioners and academicians. Predic-
tive information generated by digital twins can be directly 
translated into implementable control policies that 
substantially reduce energy consumption while main-
taining reliable production performance. The explicit

quantification of the trade-off between waiting time and 
energy usage offers a tool for calibrating operational ser-
vice levels in line with sustainability targets and cost 
objectives. By combining real-time data collection, pre-
dictive modelling, and optimisation within a unified 
framework, the proposed approach provides a systematic 
decision-support methodology that can be embedded 
into existing manufacturing execution systems to reduce 
energy costs, improve sustainability performance, and 
enhance operational flexibility.
Several extensions of the present work merit further 
investigation. Many production systems operate multi-
ple energy-intensive machines in parallel, where coordi-
nated workload allocation and energy-mode scheduling 
may reduce warm-up cycles and improve energy effi-
ciency. Appendix D outlines how the formulation can be 
extended to this setting, providing a starting point for 
investigating these potential benefits. Similarly, although 
the waiting-cost component penalises delayed process-
ing, it does not explicitly control the probability of com-
pleting specific batches by their due dates, which may be 
required in settings with firm delivery commitments. The 
chance-constrained extension examined in Appendix E 
provides an initial basis for addressing this requirement, 
and further research could build on it to analyze in 
more detail the trade-off between timely batch comple-
tion and the flexibility to exploit energy-saving operating 
modes. Beyond these extensions, the interaction between 
downstream energy control and upstream production 
dynamics, including potential blocking effects and bulk-
processing opportunities, deserves further investigation. 
When downstream operating decisions affect upstream 
material flow, the arrival process itself may depend on the 
selected resource schedule. Accounting for this depen-
dence would require jointly representing resource sched-
ules, buffer dynamics, and upstream production, poten-
tially increasing the computational effort required. Study-
ing these interactions could broaden the applicability of 
the framework to production systems with stronger inter-
dependencies between resources. Finally, incorporating 
time-varying electricity prices would allow the model to 
evaluate opportunities to shift operating periods toward 
lower-cost intervals while accounting for the resulting 
effects on vehicle waiting times.
In summary, this study proposes a comprehen-
sive data-driven framework that integrates real-time 
data acquisition, digital twin technology, mathemati-
cal programming, and heuristic optimisation to gener-
ate energy-efficient production schedules in industrial 
settings. By bridging predictive modelling with imple-
mentable scheduling policies, the proposed approach 
provides both a practical roadmap for industrial adop-
tion and a flexible methodological foundation for future 
research on energy-aware production control in modern 
manufacturing environments.
