Abstract
In Make-to-Order (MTO) production, products and production are non-standard and incoming orders arrive variably, with
varying work content and due dates. Aggregate Production Planning (APP) takes place in the medium run, and the
purpose of it is to match resource capacity with demand. In APP, orders are roughly scheduled for processing so that the
resource capacity constraints and deadlines can be met. The static resource constrained project scheduling problem is
the conventional research approach, but real-world production planning requires dynamic scheduling to integrate new
orders over time. The dynamic model in this study employs periodic scheduling, treating it as sequential static mixed
integer linear optimization problems, focusing on minimizing tardiness and rescheduling disruptions. The model is
applied in experiments concerning a Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS) -centered MTO production system. The
experimentation focuses on the effects of replanning frequency, order arrival rate, delivery date slack, forward planning
vs. backward planning, restrictions in order processing activity timing and activity workload allocation method on the
planning results. Based on the results, conclusions and guidance applicable to practical production planning are
provided.
1 Introduction
Production planning is vital for maintaining a competitive edge, ensuring the availability of essential resources such as
raw materials, workforce, and machinery to produce goods according to schedule. In Make-to-Order (MTO) production,
Aggregate Production Planning (APP) is often handled as a project scheduling challenge, using models like the
Resource-Constrained Project Scheduling Problem (RCPSP) or its multi-project variant, the Resource-Constrained Multi-
Project Scheduling Problem (RCMPSP). However, static RCPSP models fall short in real-world settings where new orders
arrive continuously and need integration into existing plans. This dynamic aspect calls for a dynamic aggregate
production planning approach, as unpredictable arrival times and diverse order content affect system eﬃciency directly.
Eﬃcient scheduling of new orders is critical, but optimal timing − whether at regular intervals or triggered by a speciﬁc
arrival threshold − remains challenging. Furthermore, while rescheduling enhances resource utilization, it can disrupt
production stability. Typically, orders can only be delayed because advancing them often requires also advancing
customer order engineering or raw material procurement, which are usually not feasible.
The focus in this study is primarily on minimizing order tardiness and rescheduling, i.e., optimizing system’s operational
eﬃciency rather than ﬁnancial costs or Work-in-Progress (WIP). To improve dynamic production planning, this study
seeks to answer several research questions:
What is the optimal frequency for scheduling incoming orders − and rescheduling existing orders?
How do average order arrival rates impact tardiness and the need for rescheduling?
How does delivery date slack inﬂuence key performance metrics?
What are the effects of forward and backward planning methods on system performance?
How do constraints on order processing activity timing and activity workload allocation method affect planning
outcomes?
To address the research questions, we apply a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) model to conduct factorial
experiments across various parameter sets. The model incorporates a rolling horizon planning approach, periodically
updating the production schedule at predetermined intervals to integrate new orders. Consequently, the framework
processes new order scheduling and potential revisions to existing orders as a sequential series of static planning
problems.
1.1 Literature Review
Page 2/17
Aggregate production planning is often treated as a project scheduling problem, formulated as a Resource-Constrained
Project Scheduling Problem (RCPSP). The RCPSP is widely applied in contexts like construction and MTO manufacturing
systems, where the goal is to optimize project duration amidst constraints like resource availability [1, 2]. The version of
the problem in which multiple projects are scheduled simultaneously and compete for the same limited resources,
consist of various activities, and may have different start times and due dates, is referred to as the Resource-Constrained
Multi-Project Scheduling Problem (RCMPSP) [3]. Traditional practical planning systems, such as Manufacturing Resource
Planning (MRP), create aggregate plans with weekly or monthly resolutions but rely on standardized schedules. Project
scheduling-based approaches can offer ﬂexibility by scheduling activities individually per order to achieve shorter lead
times with a focus on achieving leveled resource proﬁles through workload allocation [4]. The intensity of activities can
either be ﬁxed or variable across time periods. Fixed intensity means that the activity’s workload is evenly distributed
over its planned duration. Variable intensity, on the other hand, allows different amounts of workload to be allocated to
the activity across different execution periods. Variable intensity enables a more balanced resource proﬁle, i.e. higher
utilization of capacity. In order for variable intensity to guarantee feasibility, the resource types used are assumed to be
continuously divisible − an assumption that typically holds for a group of workers but may not always apply to a single
machine or a machine group [5].
In production environments, dynamic scheduling often occurs due to unpredictable events [6, 7, 8]. A rolling horizon
approach iteratively solves decision-making problems by updating plans as new data becomes available, allowing
adjustments to real-time events [9]. Fang and Xi [10] applied this to job-shop scheduling problems. Dynamic environment
referred to a situation where new jobs arrived randomly over time, machines could break down, and job due dates could
change during processing. To address this, the authors proposed both a periodic and an event-driven rolling horizon
scheduling method. The job scheduling was performed using a hybrid algorithm combining a genetic algorithm with a
dispatch rule. The objective was to minimize the makespan as well as job tardiness. The rolling horizon approach
enables the solution of real-time scheduling problems while also reducing the computational complexity, as only a limited
subset of the overall problem is optimized at a time. The rolling horizon scheduling strategy implemented in this study is
based on the approach presented by Fang and Xi.
Several studies have examined dynamic RCMPSP scheduling approaches based on heuristic rules using simulation, e.g.
[11] and [12]. Later on, optimization studies have appeared: [13], [14], [15], [16] and [17]. However, their focuses have been
on developing optimization algorithms for speciﬁc problems, and the testing has been limited to selected case problems.
The papers do not offer much guidance to industrial production managers in the way aggregate planning in MTO
environments should be executed. This paper aims to address this gap by testing the effects of decision parameters that
the managers can inﬂuence under the circumstances assumed in this study. The ultimate goal is to better understand
how various replanning and rescheduling strategies in dynamic MTO environments behave and provide practical
guidelines for industrial decision makers. To our knowledge, the important and practical research problems presented
above have not been studied before in a systematic manner like we do.
2 Model
The model in this study is based on the resource-constrained multi-project scheduling problem, aiming to eﬃciently
allocate multiple orders to resources with ﬁxed daily capacities. Orders are highly variable, and each order comprises
sequential activities, which must be completed according to precedence constraints. Workload, measured in hours,
dictates the activity duration, which includes additional slack beyond the workload-derived time. The dynamic nature of
the problem stems from the continuous but ﬂuctuating arrival of new orders, necessitating regular plan updates every
day or every few days. The planning starts with an empty factory, continuing until the end of the planning horizon when all
orders are processed, featuring initial and ﬁnal phases where capacity is actually not a constraint. Each planning iteration
creates constraints for the next schedule. The rescheduling approach combines reactive scheduling with robust features
Page 3/17
to optimize production eﬃciency (minimizing tardiness) while reducing deviations from the previous plan. Due dates
depend on activity durations and slack coeﬃcients. Future orders are unpredictable, preventing pre-scheduling.
The mathematical formulation of our APP optimization problem is similar to e.g. the static model of Morton and Pentico
[18]. There are some differences due to the way the rolling horizon method is implemented in this model, which affects
certain constraints. Table 1 lists the symbols used in our mathematical model. Among these ci,t, the activity completion
times, are the decision variables that govern the solution. In this context, an “activity” refers to a single activity within an
order. The set I thus includes all individual activities in all orders, and each activity is logically linked to its corresponding
order through precedence constraints.

3 Experiments
To answer the research questions, we use our model to test the effects of the test parameter values on a hypothetical
manufacturing system with pre-generated random order data. The data is incrementally “revealed” to the model over time
mimicking the ﬂow of incoming orders to a real manufacturing system. The dynamic planning problem is thus solved as
a series of static problems. Every time a scheduling occurs, a new model instance is created and solved. The schedule of
each iteration (planned start times of the activities) is saved and compared in the next replanning iteration. Each model
considers only variables that belong to the activities that have arrived but have not yet started. This implies that

3.1 Manufacturing System
The schematic of the manufacturing system is shown in Fig. 1. The system consists of one Preprocessing center (Pre),
one Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS), two Postprocessing centers (Post1 and Post2), one Quality Control center
(QC), and ﬁnally a Dispatch center (Dispatch). This type of FMS-centered manufacturing system is common in parts
manufacturing in the mechanical engineering industry.
The process path of an order (i.e., a product to be manufactured) follows one of twelve alternative paths. The probability
of a speciﬁc path is calculated based on the proportions shown in Fig. 1. It is worth noting that only the FMS and the
dispatch center are included in the process path of every single order.
3.2 Data Generation and Design of Experiments
Although the model itself is deterministic, the generated data sets incorporate stochastic elements. This randomness is
intended to reﬂect the variability of a real-world production environment, where both the structure of incoming orders and
the duration of individual processing activities can vary.
The interarrival time of the orders is exponentially distributed. The resource that an activity loads is determined by the
order path. The work content hours of the activities are modeled as realizations from a truncated gamma distribution.
The distribution parameters depend on the speciﬁc resource that performs the activity and are presented in Table 2. The
rate and scale parameters of the gamma distribution can be derived from the mean and the Coeﬃcient of Variation (CV).
The allowed duration of the time within which the activity must be executed depends on the duration coeﬃcient and the
shift arrangement of the resource according to Eq. (20), except resource Dispatch, where the duration is always 2 days.

The time horizon of the model, i.e. planning horizon, is 100 days (and some cooling period after that), and thus the
number of orders is determined by the arrival rate of orders. The arrival rate, one of the model parameters, here means
the average number of orders arriving in one day.
Capacity is set on the basis of the average order arrival rate, the average workload of activity on certain resource, and the
desired utilization rate:
capacity k = ⌈Order Arrival Rate ∗ AvgWCk / Utilization⌉ (22)
The utilization rate of 0.8 is used. This value is determined experimentally; it ensures that the problem is not trivial (i.e.,
no tardiness at all), while also keeping the computational complexity at a manageable level.
We implement a factorial experiment with the factors and their levels as follows:
Planning frequency: 1 day, 2 days, 3 days
Average order arrival rate: 3 orders/day, 5 orders/day
Due date slack coeﬃcient: 0.5, 0.75, 1.00
Planning method: Forward, Backward
Timing template ﬁxed and free
Free workload allocation within activity’s duration with the free timing template
Therefore, this is a 3 x 2 x 3 x 2 x 3 experiment with a total of 108 combinations. A single trial consists of ten repetitions
for each combination of parameter values and a single planning horizon. Thus, the total number of experiments is 1080
and the total number of orders in each trial is 300 or 500, depending on the average order arrival rate parameter. The
planning takes place on average every other day, and therefore the optimization problem is solved approximately: 50 x
1080 = 54 000 times. The solver tolerance is left at 20%.
The linear scalarization method is used in the multi-criterion optimization problem. The objective function weights used
are 𝑤1 = 10 (maximum tardiness), 𝑤2 = 1 (total tardiness), 𝑤3 = 1 (number of reschedulings), and 𝑤4 = 0.01 (forward
planning) or 𝑤4 = 0.035 (backward planning). The weights were determined empirically, with the aim of ﬁnding pareto
eﬃcient values without disproportionate emphasis on any individual objective.
4 Results and Discussion
The combinations of parameter values for planning frequency, order arrival rate, due date slack coeﬃcient and planning
method used in the experiments are presented in Appendix 1. These combinations are tested with ﬁxed and free timing
templates and with variable workload distribution together with the free timing template. The analyzed and recorded
dependent values are the maximum tardiness, total tardiness and number of reschedulings that are minimized in the
objective function of the planning problem. In addition total rescheduling length of all activities is reported and the
individual activities rescheduling length analysed. Average totals of these dependent values from all orders in each
repetition of each parameter value combination and their standard deviations (of the 10 experiment repetitions) are
presented in Appendices 2–5. The main results are presented in Sections 4.1–4.3. Further analysis is provided in Section
4.4.

4.2 Total tardiness
For total tardiness analysis, as well as for number of reschedulings and total rescheduling length, the results are
normalized by dividing them by the particular number of orders (300 or 500) to make them comparable. Figure 3 shows
the total tardiness in average days per order as a boxplot. From the plots can be concluded that the variation in the
values is large and the distributions are more skewed, i.e. the datapoints are concentrated closer to zero than for
maximum tardiness. The regression R2 (Table 3) value is low, and the frequency of replanning and free workload
distribution are no longer statistically quite as signiﬁcant as they were in the case of maximum tardiness. Generally, the
regression main effects are in line with those to maximum tardiness and the observations above concerning the
maximum tardiness boxplot are also similar.
According to 2-way ANOVA, signiﬁcant interactions again exist between timing and other factors so that with free timing
(and variable workload) the tardiness increasing effect of lower values for low order arrival rates, backward planning, low
due date slack and infrequent planning tend to be largely absorbed. The same effect exists between forward planning
and low order arrival rates. Also, the effect of low order arrival rate on total tardiness is eﬃciently eased by higher due
date slack.
4.3 Reschedulings
Figure 4 shows the activity reschedulings per order. Contrary to the effect on tardiness, less frequent replanning seems
to reduce the number of reschedulings, while the other effects have the same direction as for tardinesses. This is
conﬁrmed by the regression analysis. In Table 3, the coeﬃcient sign for planning frequency is negative. Again, all
independent variables are observed to be statistically signiﬁcant. Higher average order arrival rate per day and free timing
reduce the number of reschedulings mostly. Based on 2-way ANOVA results, signiﬁcant interactions exist between timing
and order arrival rate, planning frequency and planning method and between planning method and order arrival rate.
Again, free timing has an enhanced effect on reducing reschedulings when order arrival rate is low or backward planning
is used and in this case with high planning frequency. Also forward planning has a signiﬁcant improvement effect on low
order arrival rates.
Total rescheduling length has a high correlation with the number of reschedulings. The average value of individual
activity move is 1.12 days with a standard deviation of 0.9 days. From regression analysis ﬁgures in Table 3 can be seen
that planning frequency does not have signiﬁcant effect on the length of the reschedulings. Free workload distribution
together with free timing correlates with short moves. On the other hand, free timing alone produces longer moves. High
order arrival rate signiﬁcantly reduces length of reschedulings whereas forward planning and high due date slack have
only a minor reducing effect.
4.4 Further Discussion
Reliability
Page 10/17
The results presented reveal clear relationships between almost all independent variables and the various dependent
variables. This is supported both by the visualizations derived from the data and by the statistical signiﬁcance of the
regression models. However, there is considerable variation in the results, as seen in the box plot ﬁgures and in the
unexplained variance of the regression models (see the R2 values in the regression model summary in Table 3).
Some of this variation can be easily attributed to the randomness inherent in the datasets, which could be better
controlled by using a within-subjects experimental design instead of a between-subjects design. In addition, the allowed
solver tolerance was quite high, which undoubtedly affected the precision of the results.
The large variance and presence of outliers, especially in total tardiness and the number of reschedulings, may also be
due to the nature of these dependent variables: both are cumulative results of multiple planning iterations, and thus any
variation introduced by randomness in individual iterations compounds in the ﬁnal outcome. This issue is less prominent
with maximum tardiness, as can be seen both visually (Fig. 2) and from the standard deviation statistics (Appendices 2–
5). Total tardiness is recorded as sum of tardinesses of all activities, not the sum of tardiness of all orders. Depending on
the order path and tardiness of the order, this activity-based total tardiness overemphasizes the tardiness of some
orders.
The credibility of the results is also inﬂuenced by the use of the linear scalarization method in the multi-objective
optimization model. Although this approach is relatively straightforward to implement, identifying suitable and Pareto
eﬃcient weights for objectives of different magnitudes is not a trivial task. Although some analysis was conducted with
different weight combinations, the analysis was not exhaustive. An alternative multi-objective optimization method might
have yielded more reliable results.
Results
Planning method. Based on the results, forward planning is the better option to minimize tardiness. The likely reason for
this is the combination of the implementation of backward planning and the forward scheduling strategy. When orders
are scheduled to ﬁnish exactly or close to their due dates, and they can only be rescheduled by moving them forward, this
inevitably leads to tardiness when new urgent orders arrive at unfavorable times, with resources being overloaded. In
practice, however, advancing orders could sometimes be allowed, if the orders are initially scheduled far in the future. In
addition, scheduling orders to ﬁnish just on time might not be the way in which the planning is implemented in practice. It
must be underlined that in this model, the focus was on minimizing tardiness while aiming for a stable schedule.
Therefore, the model does not take into account the other beneﬁts and drawbacks of different planning methods.
With forward planning, rescheduling is avoided much more often. Unlike forward planning, with backward planning,
orders are scheduled for a longer period of time, making it theoretically possible to reschedule them at all. It is also
observed that when rescheduling is necessary, the shifts are on average not very long and that the shifts are generally
about the same length for both planning methods. This is somewhat in conﬂict with how one might expect the
phenomenon to behave: with backward planning, even a short shift can easily result in tardiness, but with forward
planning, this is not necessarily the case. This might lead one to assume that the shifts with backward planning would be
shorter than with forward planning. However, this seeming contradiction can be explained by the fact that with forward
planning, the need for rescheduling simply does not arise as often, which is why their rescheduling shifts also remain
relatively short.
Timing template and workload distribution. Use of timing method that allows free timing of activities while respecting
precedence constraints reduces tardiness and also number of reschedulings eﬃciently. This type of increased ﬂexibility
makes resource utilization much easier in the highly variable circumstances typical to MTO production. Use of this
ﬂexibility results in increased rescheduling lengths, as indicated by the positive coeﬃcient in Table 3. The possibility to
Page 11/17
allocate work content freely over the allowed activity duration further improves resource utilization, even so that both the
number and length of reschedulings are reduced; again, see regression coeﬃcients in Table 3.
Planning frequency. The tardiness is observed to increase when replanning is performed less frequently. This is an
obvious ﬁnding, as in a dynamic production environment, infrequent replanning may not respond effectively to new
orders, potentially resulting in uneven resource utilization. In particular, infrequent replanning can be disadvantageous for
urgent orders.
However, less frequent replanning reduces the number of reschedulings. This is a rather expected result, since in less
frequent planning, orders remain ﬁxed for a longer period of time, which means that their start times cannot be adjusted
as often. The quality of planning may also improve due to the larger number of orders being considered simultaneously.
With daily replanning, jobs are not really ﬁxed at all before they begin. Especially if orders are scheduled close to their
arrival time (i.e., under forward planning), they may not be rescheduled at all. With ﬁxed timing template rescheduling of
later activities after the ﬁrst activity has been started does not take place. Since minimizing tardiness and the number of
reschedulings are conﬂicting objectives when the replanning frequency is a parameter, the optimal replanning frequency
depends on the decision-maker’s preferences.
Order arrival rate. With a higher average daily order arrival rate, both tardiness and the number of reschedulings are lower.
The reason for this is likely the more eﬃcient use of resources, made possible by handling more orders simultaneously.
As a result, the effect of randomness of the workload decreases, making it easier to respect the capacity constraints.
The effect of randomness is also observed in the distributions of the dependent variables for different order arrival rates.
In Figs. 2 − 4, a much larger dispersion can be seen in the results when the arrival rate is three orders per day. This
suggests that randomness in the workload has a signiﬁcant effect on the planning outcome.
Due date slack coeﬃcient. A higher slack coeﬃcient reduces tardiness. This is an obvious result: with a large slack
coeﬃcient, the due date of orders is farther away, making scheduling within capacity constraints easier. However,
increasing the slack coeﬃcient has diminishing returns. This is because, after a suﬃciently large slack coeﬃcient value,
many orders can be scheduled with almost no tardiness, so the beneﬁts cannot grow further. However, tardiness does
not go to zero due to urgent orders or due to factors such as an exceptionally large number of orders arriving in a short
period of time, or exceptionally large workloads of activities.
4.5 Practical considerations
Some comments on the results obtained and analysis above considering common industrial practice are provided.
The advantages of free activity timing and workload allocation on activity durations are obvious. The reason for
comparing these to the use of ﬁxed timing templates is that according to the authors’ experience and knowledge, the
latter are indeed commonly used in the industry. This practice is probably partly due to traditions and legacy software
(MRP/ERP - Manufacturing/Materials/Enterprise Resource Planning, MES - Manufacturing Execution System, etc.), but
also because managing often chaotic MTO manufacturing systems asks for order and administrative eﬃciency brought
in by product families and related standard manufacturing recipes including standard timing templates. On the other
hand, modern APS (Advanced Production Planning System) software offer tools for implementing the kind of practices
this study suggests. Controlling the potentially increasing complexity sets additional challenges for production
management, but as shown, the rewards are signiﬁcant.
Another generally beneﬁcial and controllable factor in our analysis is the forward planning method. However,
minimization of WIP and the related tied capital favors timing the manufacturing operations as late as possible, i.e.
backward planning. Another practical reason for favoring backward planning is that it provides more room for changes in
Page 12/17
customer orders. Make-to-order production typically takes place in production of non-standard products, where customer
needs tend to change even after the products are ordered. Being able to react to such requests creates a lot of goodwill
at the customer, to say the least.
An illustrative detail concerning limiting excessive WIP and ﬂuctuation in process ﬂow is setting limits on the time gaps
between activities or total ﬂow time of an order. These sorts of constraints are very easy to implement in the kind of
optimization models we are suggesting.
Our model is deterministic, i.e. no disruptions due to machine failures, worker absenteeism, material shortages, quality
problems, etc. take place and work contents are exactly known at the time of planning. Robustness against uncertainty
related to such incidents is partly reﬂected in the activity duration to work content ratio – Duration coeﬃcient – of 3 for 1-
shift work and 5 for 3-shift work. Shorter-leadtime aggregate planning results could be achieved with lower ratios and the
resulting disaggregated schedules would still be theoretically mostly feasible [5]. In practice the appropriate tightness of
the schedules is largely dependent on the reliability of the process, and the values of the duration coeﬃcients must be
determined locally by the production management.
The due dates of the orders were generated mechanistically based on the estimated (deterministic) manufacturing lead
time. In reality, setting the delivery time of the orders is a result of a negotiation process between the customer and the
producer. However, from the results of the current study one can conclude that the conclusions should be generally valid
for any due dates that are not shorter than those set in the experiments.
5 Conclusions and further study
Under the given experimentation environment and the related assumptions, based on the results and their analysis, the
main conclusions are summarized as follows:
Relaxing the ﬁxed activity timing reduces tardiness and need for rescheduling
Allowing variable workload distribution within activity duration further increases these positive effects
Forward planning generally results in lower tardiness values and less rescheduling than backward planning
Tardiness rises with less frequent replanning, while rescheduling is reduced
Higher order arrival rate, with capacity maintained equally, reduces tardiness and the need for rescheduling.
Higher due date slack reduces tardiness, but at a decreasing rate
Generally, the positive effects of free activity timing and forward planning are so dominant, that the negative effects
of infrequent replanning, low order arrival rate and small due date slack are relatively reduced compared to what they
are with ﬁxed activity timing templates and backward planning.
These ideas could and should be noted by practitioners and tested in real life. Due to the nature of the problem, the
probability and consequences of any risks in such testing should be limited. A further study direction could be to
experiment with different types of production. E.g., study of larger and more complicated production systems might
reveal interesting new insights. This direction might require using eﬃcient heuristic solving algorithms, which should not
be a major problem.
Another interesting minor issue is the forward/backward planning, which could be ﬁnetuned to cover planning of known
but not released orders and using safety margins in backward planning.
