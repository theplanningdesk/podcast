ABSTRACT
The complexity and risk uncertainty stemming from supply disruptions underscore the need for 
enhanced transparency in supply chain management. Although blockchain technologies offer 
promising solutions through secure, immutable ledgers, adoption rates remain low, and extant 
research focuses primarily on theoretical frameworks lacking empirical substantiation. Addressing 
this gap, the present study reconceptualises transparency barriers as capability frictions, system-
atic impediments that prevent firms from effectively deploying sensing, seizing, and transformation 
capabilities. Through an in-depth case study of a Swedish OEM and its suppliers, four transparency 
barriers are identified: information inaccuracy, risks of information sharing, lack of communica-
tion protocols, and future communications compatibility. Analysis demonstrates how these barriers 
create capability frictions by weakening sensing through information asymmetry, constraining seiz-
ing through limited collaboration, and restricting transformation through weak data governance. 
The findings reveal how blockchain’s distinctive properties, immutability, transparency, and decen-
tralised consensus, systematically reduce these frictions, enabling firms to transition from weakened 
to enhanced dynamic capabilities. Five propositions are developed theorising the mechanisms 
through which blockchain serves as a digital enabler for capability transformation, contributing to 
dynamic capabilities theory and supply chain management practice .

Introduction
Research interest in supply chain transparency, infor-
mation sharing, tracking and tracing has increased in 
recent years due to the marked changes in global sup-
ply networks triggered by events such as the worldwide 
pandemic and the expansion of e-commerce (Budler, 
Quiroga, and Trkman 2024; Pournader et al. 2020). 
Ongoing challenges including material shortages, pro-
duction interruptions, and increased costs in both sourc-
ing and logistics have led to research about the advan-
tages, disadvantages and barriers to supply chain trans-
parency for firms (Guan et al. 2020; Mol 2015; W ognum 
et al. 2011; Y ang, Fu, and Zhang 2021). These concerns 
are reflected in recent findings showing that while 77% of 
procurement experts consider supply chain transparency 
to be crucial, only 31% are satisfied with their organisa-
tion’ s current transparency efforts (Procurement Leaders 
2022).
Despite its critical importance, increasing trans-
parency in supply chains faces persistent challenges such 
as the lack of available resources and accurate informa-
tion, and the need to create an integrated and common

(Addo-T enkorang and Helo 2016; Choi and Siqin 2022

of blockchain technologies is hindered by barriers such 
as insufficient IT infrastructure, lack of implementation 
knowledge, misunderstanding of the technology, and 
financial constraints (Rad et al. 2022; Raj et al. 2024; Tran-
Dang et al. 2022). Moreover, empirical evidence shows 
that successful blockchain implementation requires spe-
cific organisational capabilities. Recent research on elec-
tronics companies in Bangladesh confirms that three key 
capabilities are particularly critical: innovation capability, 
information-sharing capability, and technological capa-
bility (Polas et al. 2025).
Despite some recent case studies on technology accep-
tance and implementation (Balzarova, Dyer, and Falta 
2022; Centobelli et al. 2022), research on transparency 
facilitated by blockchain use remains largely theoretical, 
with limited empirical evidence (Chou et al. 2023; Guo, 
Sun, and Lam 2023; Iranmanesh et al. 2023). Addressing 
this gap, the present study examines the barriers to trans-
parency and information sharing within the supply chain 
of a Swedish Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) 
and its first-tier suppliers. Through qualitative case study 
analysis involving interviews and document analysis, 
four key barriers were identified: information inaccu-
racy, risk of information sharing, lack of communication 
standards, and the need for future communications com-
patibility. These transparency barriers are interpreted as 
capability deficits that restrict firms’ ability to sense, seize, 
and transform information flows effectively. Blockchain 
adoption is examined as a digital enabler that diminish 
these deficits and thus creates dynamic capabilities for 
adaptability and transparency.
This study advances both theoretical and practi-
cal understanding in several important ways. Theoreti-
cally, it provides much-needed empirical evidence in a 
field dominated by conceptual frameworks (e.g. Chou 
et al. 2023; Guo, Sun, and Lam 2023). Practically, it 
moves beyond generic blockchain discussions to examine 
specific transparency barriers within an actual OEM-
supplier network, offering actionable insights for imple-
mentation. The dynamic capabilities framework helps 
explain how the four identified barriers, information 
inaccuracy, sharing risks, communication standards, and 
future compatibility, represent capability deficits that 
blockchain technology can help organisations systemat-
ically address and transform. Moreover, recent research 
has shown that practitioners can benefit from strong the-
oretical foundations, enhancing strategy implementation 
and more effectively identifying key success factors and 
potential barriers (Goertler, Papert, and Fischer 2024).
This study employs dynamic capabilities theory as 
its theoretical lens because transparency challenges in 
supply chains fundamentally affect a firm’ s abilities to 
sense, seize, and transform its information-sharing and 
collaboration capabilities in response to environmen-
tal changes. This framework is particularly suited for 
analysing blockchain’ s impact as it outlines the activ-
ities required to digitalise processes of transparency 
and information sharing (Hallikas, Immonen, and Brax 
2021), building on the dynamic capabilities frame-
work that identifies sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring 
as essential capacity drivers enabling corporations to 
enhance their resilience in environments of rapid change
(T eece 2007).
This study addresses the lack of empirical evidence 
on transparency barriers in supply chains and proposes 
actionable blockchain strategies for their mitigation. W e 
argue that transparency barriers manifest as capabil-
ity frictions, systematic impediments to sensing, seiz-
ing, and transformation, and that blockchain adoption 
serves as a digital enabler that reduces these frictions, 
thereby enabling enhanced dynamic capabilities and sup-
ply chain resilience. Through an in-depth case study of a 
Swedish OEM and its suppliers, we identify four trans-
parency barriers, demonstrate how they create capability 
frictions, and develop five propositions articulating how 
blockchain systematically transforms these frictions into 
enhanced dynamic capabilities, contributing to both the-
oretical understanding and practical supply chain man-
agement knowledge.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. 
First, a literature review is conducted exploring trans-
parency and blockchain in supply chains, focusing on 
both barriers and benefits to its adoption, and con-
cluding with a discussion on dynamic capabilities the-
ory in supply chains. Second, the focal firm and the 
blockchain project of focus in this case study are intro-
duced, followed by the data collection and analysis meth-
ods used to interpret the empirical data. Third, a presen-
tation of the study’ s empirical evidence supporting the 
four key barriers discovered, followed by five proposi-
tions relating to the utilisation of blockchain technolo-
gies to mitigate these barriers is presented. The paper 
concludes with a summary of its theoretical and prac-
tical contributions and a discussion of future research
opportunities.

Review of literature
Transparency in supply chains
Supply chain transparency can encompass reporting and 
communicating information to key stakeholders, trac-
ing products along the supply chain, or making current 
activities more visible to different stakeholders in the net-
work (Morgan, Richey, and Ellinger 2018). As a part of 
this process, management, regulatory, consumer, and/or 
public information is shared with different upstream and 
downstream supply chain members (Mol 2015). The level 
of transparency applied to communication with differ-
ent supply chain actors, both internally and externally, 
is often context-driven, with a particular purpose, for 
example sustainable supply chain information (Schafer 
2023). According to Bai and Sarkis (2020), transparency 
practices are mainly applied to product tracking and trac-
ing, as well as to participant activities (i.e. sustainability 
initiatives) in supply chains. However, the inconsistent 
utilisation of transparency practices across different tiers 
of suppliers presents a key challenge, particularly in fos-
tering trust and collaboration between firms and their 
supply chain partners. It is thus important to under-
stand how firms can manage technical and organisational 
barriers to implementing transparency initiatives.
Product tracing, from source to customer delivery, 
forms a complete product provenance record. This can 
demonstrate to the consumer, for example, that prod-
ucts are authentic and/or that ethical procedures were 
being followed during manufacturing, increasing the 
likelihood of repurchasing (Bai and Sarkis 2020; Kshetri 
2017). Supply chain members can also benefit from prod-
uct tracking, as this facilitates the analysis of product 
movements and delivery paths. Using this information, 
it is possible to determine whether a particular prod-
uct was incorrectly processed, placed in the wrong loca-
tion, or failed to meet the delivery deadline (Koetsier 
2017). Thus, increased visibility of upstream and down-
stream supply chain processes enhances the governance 
and coordination of supply chain operations (Lumineau, 
W ang, and Schilke 2021; Tran-Dang et al. 2022).
Despite these benefits, for supply chain transparency 
to be effective, all firms and partner agents in the supply 
network must be involved. As most businesses have little 
to no information about their own second- and third-tier 
suppliers, transparency is a significant business challenge 
(Bai and Sarkis 2020). Thus, firms must foster coordi-
nation with global supply chain stakeholders to deepen 
their transparency in relation to these suppliers (Rad 
et al. 2022). This needs to include information sharing on 
upper-tier supply chain disruptions, including internal 
shortages, recalls, and bankruptcies, as well as external 
issues due to natural disasters and pandemics (Sodhi 
and Tang 2019). Enhanced supply chain transparency 
will function as a governance system that lowers opera-
tional risks for different stakeholders within the network 
(Mol 2015). Effective transparency initiatives may also 
lessen supply chain partners’ uncertainties and support 
a collaborative information sharing culture, leading to 
more in-depth product tracking and tracing (Montecchi, 
Plangger, and W est 2021).
T oday’ s supply networks are composed of thousands 
of suppliers spread all over the world, making it diffi-
cult for firms to gather accurate, timely, and trustworthy 
information. This fragmentation is caused by focal firms 
looking for ways to lower their costs, putting pressure on 
suppliers to outsource their production to less developed 
countries, leading to little or no transparency and to neg-
ative externalities in supply chains (Kamble et al. 2019; 
Sodhi and Tang 2019). These drawbacks can be over-
come by increasing the transparency of supply partner 
operations, and through timely sharing of information 
about current supply states and sustainability conditions 
(Baah et al. 2022; Benstead et al. 2024). Greater trans-
parency improves trust among the various stakeholders, 
which is conducive to better collaboration and decision-
making, thereby increasing supply chain performance 
and resilience (Barratt and Oke 2007). Collaboration also 
creates a competitive advantage through cost reductions, 
improved problem-solving capabilities, quicker adjust-
ments to market fluctuations, risk sharing, and faster 
knowledge acquisition. These benefits have been shown 
to mitigate supply chain risks stemming from market 
volatility, essentially creating more agile supply chains 
(Baah et al. 2022; Barratt and Oke 2007).
While it is indisputable that increasing transparency in 
supply chains is beneficial to all stakeholders, achieving 
this aim can be difficult due to the growing complexity of 
supply chains, the technical deficiencies of stakeholders, 
and multiple standards of communication (V enkatesh 
et al. 2020). These challenges can be mitigated using 
digital technologies that facilitate the timely availabil-
ity of information to all stakeholders, the rapid tracing 
of product history, secure information transmission, and 
the automation of manual processes across the entire net-
work (Li, Lee, and Gharehgozli 2023). Blockchain is one 
such solution, known for its ability to improve trans-
parency and trust in supply chain networks through 
trusted and immutable ledgers that can be visible to 
everyone (Addo-T enkorang and Helo 2016; Boone, Skip-
per, and Hazen 2017; Y ang, Fu, and Zhang 2021).
Blockchain in supply chains
Blockchain is defined as ‘a digital, decentralized and dis-
tributed ledger in which transactions are logged and

added in chronological order with the goal of creating 
permanent and tamperproof records’ (Treiblmaier 2018, 
547). Its main benefits stem from the implicit consen-
sus achieved through the verification of transactions with 
other blockchain copies in the ledger and through digital 
smart contracts for data sharing and transaction gate-
keeping (Agrawal et al. 2023). Since its development, 
blockchain technologies have been extensively used in 
cryptocurrency transactions because of the security it can 
provide through data encryption and immutability (Y aga 
et al. 2019). Currently available blockchain networks are 
either permissionless (meaning that everybody can join) 
or permissioned (users need to be authorised before 
joining), with the latter being more common in private 
blockchain solutions used in supply chains (Agrawal et al. 
2021; Y aga et al. 2019). Blockchain’ s benefits in supply 
chains include secure data sharing, permanent trans-
action recording, and automation with smart contracts 
(Rejeb, Keogh, and Treiblmaier 2019; Saberi et al. 2019). 
As shown by Agrawal et al. (2023), smart contracts are 
particularly useful in this context, as they allow focal 
firms to impose tailored rules to encourage cooperation 
among certain actors in the supply network and facilitate 
the sharing of resources among them.
According to Saberi et al. (2019), transparency created 
through blockchain reduces the opportunistic behaviour 
of supply chain partners and creates efficiencies by elimi-
nating the need for intermediaries. Smart contracts can 
create additional efficiencies through automation and 
the use of real-time information, while reducing paper-
work and mitigating supply chain disruptions such as 
the bullwhip effect (Bodkhe et al. 2020; Rejeb, Keogh, 
and Treiblmaier 2019). Additional benefits of blockchain 
adoption in supply chains are attained through greater 
transparency, traceability, and scalability, difficult to 
achieve via traditional centralised tracking and trac-
ing systems (Sunny, Undralla, and Madhusudanan Pil-
lai 2020). Lastly, tracking and tracing in combination 
with secured data flows through blockchain improve the 
authenticity of information around a product’ s prove-
nance (Bodkhe et al. 2020).
Data is an essential component of supply chains, and 
the seamless integration of information flows throughout 
the supply chain is vital for generating value. There-
fore, any technology that promotes a prompt and pre-
cise transmission of information is expected to have a 
substantial influence on the efficiency of supply chain 
operations beyond the capabilities of existing Enterprise 
Resource Planning (ERP) systems (Benstead et al. 2024 
). Blockchain fulfills the necessary criteria for facilitating 
the efficient integration of supply chains, while lower-
ing transaction costs by reducing regulatory bottlenecks 
and the potential for human error (Bai and Sarkis 2020; 
Benstead et al. 2024). Additional cost savings can be 
realised through efficiency gains in identifying and eval-
uating trading partners, as well as reducing the need 
for supplier governance and simplifying dispute resolu-
tions (Lumineau, W ang, and Schilke 2021). Indeed, as 
shown by Centobelli et al. (2022), supply chains that do 
not incorporate a blockchain system necessitate a sig-
nificant number of human inspections and transactions, 
requiring multiple intermediaries to build trust.
Blockchain allows for better connectivity with supply 
chain partners and their networks (Bai and Sarkis 2020), 
thus improving compliance with regulatory require-
ments, consumer and investor trust, and the external 
auditing process (Sodhi and Tang 2019). Additionally, 
databases utilising blockchain technology organise data 
in a more structured manner, thereby simplifying its use 
and analysis (Choi, Guo, and Luo 2020). Furthermore, 
the tracing and transparency provided by blockchain 
technology enables better prediction of variations in 
demand within the supply chain, making it possible to 
effectively manage costs and minimise supply swings 
(Centobelli et al. 2022). However, despite its benefits, 
blockchain adoption can have unintended consequences. 
For instance, the transparencies enabled by blockchain 
may reveal vulnerabilities such as unethical sourcing 
practices, labour exploitation, counterfeit goods, or inef-
ficiencies in supply chain operations that might damage a 
firm’ s reputation (Bodkhe et al. 2020; Montecchi, Plang-
ger, and W est 2021). The negative externalities from sup-
ply chain partners can result in production challenges, 
disruptions, unethical practices, environmental issues, 
and poor working conditions in factories, all of which 
must be managed effectively to prevent further issues. 
This highlights the importance of governance mecha-
nisms and collaboration among firms and supply chain 
partners to mitigate these risks (Montecchi, Plangger, and 
W est 2021).
Accordingly, when digital technologies such as block
chain are coupled with governance mechanisms, cap-
turing pertinent supply chain information promotes an 
environment of openness that is beneficial for all sup-
ply chain stakeholders (Lumineau, W ang, and Schilke 
2021; Montecchi, Plangger, and W est 2021). However, 
despite these advantages, many firms remain reluctant 
to increase information sharing with supply chain stake-
holders, leading existing research in this field to focus 
predominantly on the enablers and obstacles to broader 
blockchain adoption (Han and Fang 2024; Li, Lee, and 
Gharehgozli 2023). There are limited empirical stud-
ies exploring the barriers to increased transparency and 
information sharing in supply chains with blockchain, 
with a few exceptions (Dubey et al. 2020; Fosso W amba, 
Queiroz, and Trinchera 2020).

Transparency and blockchain barriers in supply 
chains
Barriers to transparency in supply chains can be both 
technical and social in nature. For many firms, the costs 
associated with the adoption of a new technology, its 
merging with legacy systems, and personnel training can 
be prohibitive (Rathee et al. 2021; Sharma et al. 2021). For 
others, the barriers stem from the lack of communication 
protocols and/or sharing standards, software compati-
bility issues, and poor internet connectivity. Even firms 
that proceed with blockchain adoption need to design 
governance mechanisms to manage the huge amounts 
of data collected through the system, which could hin-
der transparency efforts (Iddris 2018; Kamble et al. 2019; 
Lumineau, W ang, and Schilke 2021; Sharma et al. 2021). 
While these challenges are acknowledged by existing 
studies, there is little empirical research investigating 
how governance mechanisms can manage these issues, 
particularly in multi-tiered supply chains.
Some firms are also hesitant to reveal supplier infor-
mation for fear of losing their competitive advantage, 
while others indicate security concerns as a barrier to 
greater transparency (Sharma et al. 2021; Sodhi and Tang 
2019; Y adav et al. 2020). Reputational damage is also 
a cause for concern, as the discovery of issues such as 
poor work environments in the supply chains or prod-
uct provenance issues (e.g. conflict minerals) could create 
unnecessary bad press for the supply chain stakehold-
ers. Transparency can put firms in a position where they 
are either guilty by association due to upstream sup-
plier problems or guilty for not disclosing information 
about suppliers when issues arise; they are thus reluctant 
to be pushed into loss of deniability about their supply 
networks (Sodhi and Tang 2019).
From an inter-organisational perspective, critical fac-
tors in blockchain adoption include lack of common 
standards, lack of trust in the system, and lack of faithful 
information sharing (Choi and Siqin 2022). Standard-
isation protocols related to blockchain technology can 
encompass industry, communication, legal, and regula-
tory standards, many of which are closely tied to gov-
ernmental regulations on implementation and interoper-
ability risks (Keoh, Kumar, and T schofenig 2014; Kumar 
et al. 2022; Saberi et al. 2019). As was shown by Mis-
try et al. (2020), lack of communication and technical 
standardisation creates interoperability issues as it pre-
cludes direct data transfer across different ledgers. The 
absence of common communication standards among 
supply chain partners also limits blockchain informa-
tion sharing capability, resulting in knowledge gaps 
between stakeholders (Rejeb, Keogh, and Treiblmaier 
2019). Intra-organisational barriers such as lack of capi-
tal, technical knowledge, support and commitment from 
management, and adequate IT infrastructure are further 
issues that hinder blockchain adoption in supply chains 
(Choi and Siqin 2022; Saberi et al. 2019). For exam-
ple, private blockchains require skilled workers, robust 
IT infrastructure, capital, and commitment across all 
departments (Kumar et al. 2022). Depending on how 
a blockchain is set up, scalability mandated by increas-
ingly longer chains increases the storage space require-
ments, and additional nodes necessitate greater process-
ing resources which may increase synchronisation times, 
reducing overall system performance (Reyna et al. 2018).
Dynamic capabilities in supply chains
According to T eece (2007), ‘The enterprise will need 
sensing, seizing, and transformational/reconfiguring
capabilities to be simultaneously developed and applied 
for it to build and maintain competitive advantage’ 
(1341). In this context, the term ‘sensing’ refers to the 
process of capturing, sifting, and filtering data, uncov-
ering changes in markets, and creating new knowledge 
to respond to supply chain demand shifts (Hallikas, 
Immonen, and Brax 2021; T eece 2007). Through seiz-
ing, firms evaluate and reorganise resources to facilitate 
information-sharing and decision-making procedures, 
capitalising on opportunities, and creating additional 
value with current resources (Foerstl et al. 2021; Zhu, Bai, 
and Sarkis 2022). The ability of a firm to adjust, reorgan-
ise, and create new or replace old business models is thus 
foundational to its dynamic capabilities (Hallikas, Immo-
nen, and Brax 2021; T eece 2007). On the other hand, new 
competitive advantage can only be attained by transform-
ing business models through the continuous realignment 
and reassessment of assets, both tangible and intangible, 
managing knowledge, and creating governance mecha-
nisms to manage threats and respond to opportunities 
(T eece 2007). T o remain competitive in rapidly changing 
supply chain markets, firms need to quickly create new 
knowledge. However, uncertainty regarding the veracity 
of available information, market volatility, and security 
concerns cause managers to adjust slowly to these rapid 
changes (Eisenhardt and Martin 2000).
The concept of dynamic and volatile markets, as 
defined by Eisenhardt and Martin (2000), is characterised 
by unclear market boundaries, frequent, sporadic, and 
often unpredictable changes, and not always clearly dis-
cernible market players. This definition is particularly 
applicable to supply chains, which can be regarded as 
dynamic markets due to their inherent complexity, the 
lack of visibility of the entire supply network, and the 
prevailing disruptions they face, in which factors such

as pandemics can introduce additional layers of unpre-
dictability and disruption. Research has found that firms 
may create dynamic capabilities by collaborating with 
supply partners and reconfiguring their operational rou-
tines to increase efficiencies (Aslam et al. 2020; Hallikas, 
Immonen, and Brax 2021; Rajaguru and Matanda 2019). 
However, Schilke (2014), found that capabilities such as 
the management of alliances and new product develop-
ment are not necessarily linear when considering highly 
dynamic markets. He found that a U-shaped contingent 
relationship exists wherein the impact of dynamic capa-
bilities on competitive advantage is most pronounced in 
moderately dynamic situations, while it diminishes in 
both low and high levels of environmental dynamism 
(Schilke 2014). Firms will need to distinguish between 
what Fainshmidt et al. (2016) referred to as higher-order 
and lower-order capabilities. Higher-order capabilities 
allow firms to make transformational changes to organi-
sational performance by enhancing lower-order capabili-
ties. Firms that can distinguish between higher-order and 
lower-order capabilities will enhance problem-solving 
and increase the likelihood of driving organisational 
changes within their dynamic environments (Fainshmidt 
et al. 2016).
However, this process necessitates that all supply chain 
members develop the skills required to appropriately 
adapt to shifting market demands and environmental 
changes (Bojesson and Fundin 2021; Eslami et al. 2024). 
As the world becomes more interconnected, the ability 
to share timely and accurate information becomes crit-
ical for responsiveness in supply chains (Dubey et al. 
2020). Digital technologies such as blockchain offer sig-
nificant potential for enhancing transparency through 
the facilitation of secure, real-time information sharing 
with supply chain partners (Eslami et al. 2024). By cre-
ating more accurate, reliable, and trusted information, 
blockchain can support managers in making better deci-
sions, thereby strengthening the organisation’ s dynamic 
capabilities.
Transparency barriers as capability frictions
Building on the dynamic capability’ s framework dis-
cussed above, transparency barriers in the current study 
are conceptualised as capability frictions, systematic 
impediments that prevent firms from effectively deploy-
ing their sensing, seizing, and transformation capabilities 
in supply chain environments. While prior research has 
documented various barriers to transparency in supply 
chain management (Sharma et al. 2021; Sodhi and Tang 
2019), these barriers have largely been treated as opera-
tional or technological challenges rather than as funda-
mental constraints on the development and deployment 
of organisational capabilities. This represents a gap in 
the theoretical understanding of how transparency chal-
lenges impair dynamic capabilities and, consequently, 
how digital technologies such as blockchain can address 
these capability deficiencies.
The present study theorises that capability frictions 
emerge through three interconnected mechanisms that 
directly impair the dynamic capabilities essential for 
supply chain resilience. First, information asymmetry 
created by inaccurate data and incompatible commu-
nication protocols prevent reliable environmental scan-
ning, thereby weakening sensing capabilities (Hallikas, 
Immonen, and Brax 2021; T eece 2007). When firms can-
not trust the information received from supply chain 
partners or cannot efficiently gather intelligence due 
to system incompatibility, their ability to detect mar-
ket changes, identify emerging risks, and recognise new 
opportunities becomes fundamentally impaired. This 
diminished sensing capacity constrains the knowledge 
creation and environmental awareness necessary for 
responding to supply chain disruptions. Second, lim-
ited collaboration stemming from information-sharing 
risks constrains resource coordination and opportu-
nity exploitation, thereby weakening seizing capabilities 
(Foerstl et al. 2021; Zhu, Bai, and Sarkis 2022). When 
stakeholders withhold critical operational information 
due to competitive concerns or security fears regarding 
data breaches, firms cannot effectively realign resources 
or engage in collaborative decision-making processes in 
response to sensed environmental changes. This col-
laboration deficit prevents firms from capitalising on 
opportunities and responding swiftly to market dynam-
ics. Third, weak data governance structures and con-
cerns about future compatibility restrict organisational 
adaptability and process reconfiguration, thereby weak-
ening transformation capabilities (T eece 2007). With-
out robust, future-proof information infrastructure capa-
ble of accommodating evolving regulatory requirements 
and technological advancements, firms cannot continu-
ously realign assets or develop the governance mecha-
nisms necessary for sustained competitive advantage in 
dynamic supply chain environments.
Blockchain technology, through its distinctive prop-
erties of immutability, transparency, and decentralised

Theoretical framework
Figure 1 presents the theoretical framework guiding the 
present investigation, integrating transparency barriers, 
capability frictions, and blockchain-enabled capability 
enhancement within supply chain contexts. The frame-
work builds on dynamic capabilities theory (T eece 2007; 
T eece, Pisano, and Shuen 1997) to illustrate how trans-
parency barriers generate capability frictions through 
three distinct mechanisms, how these frictions mani-
fest as weakened dynamic capabilities across the dimen-
sions of sensing, seizing, and transformation, and how 
blockchain’ s distinctive technological properties system-
atically address these weaknesses to enable enhanced 
capabilities necessary for supply chain resilience.
As illustrated in the framework, transparency barri-
ers generate capability frictions through distinct mecha-
nisms that directly impair the dynamic capabilities essen-
tial for supply chain management. Information inaccu-
racy and the absence of standardised communication 
protocols create information asymmetry, thereby pre-
venting firms from accurately sensing their environment 
(Hallikas, Immonen, and Brax 2021; T eece 2007). When 
data is incomplete, delayed, or incompatible across sys-
tems, firms cannot reliably monitor supplier operations, 
identify critical dependencies across multiple tiers, or 
detect emerging disruptions in their supply networks. 
The risks associated with information sharing limit stake-
holder collaboration, thereby constraining the seizing of 
opportunities through coordinated resource alignment 
(Foerstl et al. 2021; Sharma et al. 2021; Sodhi and Tang 
2019). When supply chain partners withhold operational 
intelligence due to competitive concerns or security vul-
nerabilities, firms cannot effectively realign resources 
or engage in collaborative decision-making processes in 
response to sensed market changes. W eak data gover-
nance structures and concerns about future compatibility 
restrict organisational transformation by limiting adapt-
ability across the supply chain (T eece 2007). Without 
robust, future-proof information infrastructure capable 
of accommodating evolving regulatory and technological 
requirements, firms face repeated disruptive system over-
hauls rather than the continuous capability development 
necessary for sustained competitive advantage.
Blockchain technology systematically addresses these 
capability frictions through its distinctive technological 
properties (Treiblmaier 2018; Y aga et al. 2019), as shown 
in the framework. The immutability and transparency

characteristics of blockchain reduce information asym-
metry by ensuring data accuracy and enabling real-time 
visibility across supply chain tiers, thereby enhancing 
sensing capabilities (Bai and Sarkis 2020; Pattanayak, 
Arputham, et al. 2024). Smart contracts and permis-
sioned access controls enable secure collaboration by 
protecting proprietary information while simultaneously 
facilitating the information exchange necessary for coor-
dination (Rejeb, Keogh, and Treiblmaier 2019; Saberi 
et al. 2019), thereby strengthening seizing capabilities. 
Standardised communication protocols and extensible 
architectural design support both governance and adapt-
ability by providing a future-proof platform that accom-
modates evolving regulatory requirements and techno-
logical advancements (Benstead et al. 2024; Centobelli 
et al. 2022), thereby facilitating transformation capabil-
ities.
This framework positions blockchain adoption not 
merely as a technological implementation, but rather 
as a catalyst for fundamental capability transforma-
tion in supply chain management. By systematically 
reducing frictions across all three capability dimen-
sions, blockchain enables firms to transition from weak-
ened capabilities, characterised by delayed sensing, frag-
mented seizing, and constrained transformation, to 
enhanced capabilities that support supply chain resilience 
in dynamic and uncertain environments (Fainshmidt 
et al. 2016; Zhu, Bai, and Sarkis 2022). Importantly, these 
capability enhancements are interconnected and mutu-
ally reinforcing improved sensing capabilities provide the 
reliable information foundation necessary for effective 
seizing, which in turn enables the organisational trans-
formation required for sustained competitive advantage 
(T eece 2007). The empirical investigation presented in 
this study examines this framework through an in-depth 
case study analysis of blockchain implementation in an 
OEM supply chain context.
Methodology
Case study design and context
T o explore and understand the concept of transparency 
and how blockchain can be used to increase transparency 
in an OEM supply chain, a single in-depth case study 
technique was adopted in this research. Case study meth-
ods can be used when the objective is to understand 
more clearly a phenomenon of interest in a complex real-
life context where boundaries between the context and 
the phenomenon might not be clear (Yin 2009). This 
strategy was suitable for addressing the research ques-
tions guiding this investigation since it makes it possible 
to have a thorough grasp of how supply chain partners 
define transparency and see its strategic advantage, and 
to identify the technologies used to increase information 
sharing. Additionally, according to Ketokivi and Choi 
(2014), the case study approach should be utilised when 
the empirical data is analysed not just to confirm a theory 
but also to elaborate on its postulates, based on the empir-
ical findings. In this research, concentrating on a specific 
case also provided the opportunity to understand trans-
parency in supply chains and the perspective of the focal 
firm on the adoption of blockchain as well as the perspec-
tives of its key suppliers (Eslami, Lakemond, and Brusoni 
2018).
The focal firm is an international corporation with 
over 15,000 employees that is headquartered in Swe-
den. It is a leading manufacturer of construction equip-
ment used in rock quarries and in infrastructure projects 
such as road construction. At the time of the study, like 
many other industrial manufacturing firms, the focal 
firm had experienced supply chain shortages and disrup-
tions, prompting the adoption of digital technologies to 
improve collaboration with suppliers, as well as to sense 
disruptions in their supply lines early for quicker deci-
sion making. Many supply chain members had developed 
deep long-term relationships with the focal firm.
The focal firm’ s supply chain contains approximately 
4,000 Tier 1 suppliers from various parts of the world and 
an unknown number of Tier N suppliers. These suppli-
ers are categorised in groups such as business partners, 
supply partners, suppliers, and backup suppliers, depend-
ing on their internal scores and relationship strengths. 
Blockchain technology is being utilised in a project called 
Material Data System (MDS) Sponsoring and involves all 
Tier 1 suppliers. A prioritised rollout of the blockchain 
project to suppliers started with those in the ‘business 
partners’ category, since the focal firm has the strongest 
relationship with those suppliers. MDS is a universal 
information-sharing platform designed for the manufac-
turing industry, allowing users to report on vehicle con-
tents as required by governmental regulations and to help 
with afterlife recycling and reuse programmes (Euro-
pean Automobile Manufacturers’ Association 2018). The 
focal firm has implemented a compliance data exchange 
(CDX) system developed with input from other manu-
facturers and provided by DXC T echnologies. DXC T ech-
nologies assists multinational corporations in managing 
their essential systems and operations, as well as updat-
ing IT infrastructure, optimising data frameworks, and 
assuring security and scalability in public, private, and 
hybrid cloud environments (CDX 2023).
By October 2023, approximately 2,000 of these Tier 
1 suppliers had signed on to the service, with the focal 
firm covering all associated fees, allowing the suppliers 
to implement the system with their own Tier 1 supply 
networks. The ultimate aim of this initiative was to make

Data collection
Data collection was conducted through a semi-structu
red interview process. Interviews allow researchers to 
investigate a topic through discussion with participants 
about their experiences, viewpoints, and understandings 
of the subject matter. This process allows the researchers 
to acquire extensive, intricate, and nuanced data that 
might not be obtainable using alternative techniques. T o 
obtain a wide range of viewpoints on the subject, the 
researchers conducted interviews with management per-
sonnel from both the focal firm and its Tier 1 suppliers. 
Professionals with expertise in procurement, logistics, 
IT , and supply chain management contributed valuable 
perspectives and experiences that enhance the quali-
tative data. Interviewees were first chosen by conve-
nience to the researchers, with additional interviewees 
discovered through a snowball technique. As intervie-
wees were located in various parts of the world, inter-
views were conducted over the internet and were digitally 
recorded. During their individual interviews, all infor-
mants were presented with the same open-ended ques-
tions, with slight question variations to focus on their 
business expertise. The interviews lasted 27−60 min, and 
commenced with questions related to transparency, fol-
lowed by questions focusing on their business exper-
tise and finishing with technology-related questions. Ini-
tially, 17 interviews were conducted between March 2023 
and mid-May 2023, and this empirical data was sup-
plemented by two follow-up interviews conducted in 
October 2023, as shown in Table 1.
After data collection, representatives from the focal 
firm received a summary of research results so that they 
might assess and leave comments and feedback. Upon 
receipt of this feedback, the report was refined through 
iteration and then sent back to the focal firm for valida-
tion. As this was a long-term project for the focal firm, 
two key representatives in the overall project’ s timeline 
from focal firm were interviewed a second time to follow 
up on how the blockchain implementation project was 
proceeding, the follow-up interviews served to capture 
the evolving nature of the blockchain implementation 
and were analysed as supplementary data rather than 
additional primary interviews. This ensured that the per-
spectives of Procurement B and Engineering A were not 
overweighted in the final analysis established from the 
initial 17 interviews, as follow-up insights used to track 
project developments and confirm our earlier findings 
only.
Data analysis
Data analysis followed the Gioia method of data analy-
sis (Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton 2013), where first order 
analysis took the form of exact quotes pulled from the 
transcripts. From here the quotes were distilled into cat-
egories and subcategories that pertained to the different 
transparency barriers discussed in the literature. Themes 
were then abstracted from these categories to encompass 
an overall description of the transparency barriers uncov-
ered during the interview process. Lastly, an aggregation 
of these themes was created to align the findings with the 
dynamic capabilities of sensing, seizing, and transforma-
tion (T eece 2007). The Gioia analysis method is a process 
of taking data terminology, themes, and dimensions and 
creating a structure through which to present the data 
analysis process of the researchers in a more rigorous 
manner (Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton 2013).
All interviews first transcribed manually by the 
research group and later re-coded in Nvivo© software. 
Coding commenced with the use of broad keywords 
found in the literature on transparency and blockchain 
use in supply chain management (Montecchi, Plangger, 
and W est 2021; Rejeb, Keogh, and Treiblmaier 2019; 
Schafer 2023; Sodhi and Tang 2019). Initially, the aim

Findings
Transparency barriers and blockchain solutions
Drawing on the empirical findings and applying the the-
oretical framework presented in Figure 1, this section 
explores how transparency barriers function as capa-
bility frictions that systematically weaken the dynamic 
capabilities of the focal firm and its supply chain part-
ners. The analysis identified four primary transparency 
barriers, information inaccuracy, risks of information 
sharing, lack of communication protocols, and the need 
for future communications compatibility, each of which 
creates specific friction patterns that impede the deploy-
ment of sensing, seizing, or transformation capabilities 
within supply chain environments. As illustrated in the 
theoretical framework, information inaccuracy creates 
sensing friction through information asymmetry, thereby 
preventing accurate environmental scanning; risks asso-
ciated with information sharing create seizing friction 
by limiting stakeholder collaboration and constraining 
resource coordination; the absence of standardised com-
munication protocols creates friction across both sensing 
and seizing capabilities through system incompatibility; 
and concerns regarding future communications com-
patibility create transformation friction by constraining 
organisational adaptability and the capacity for continu-
ous reconfiguration.
The presentation of findings in this section is struc-
tured to systematically demonstrate the friction-to-
capability transformation mechanism central to the theo-
retical framework. For each transparency barrier, empiri-
cal evidence is first presented to illustrate how the barrier 
manifests within the focal firm’ s supply chain operations 
and its relationships with first-tier suppliers. This is fol-
lowed by an analysis of the specific capability friction 
generated by the barrier, explicating how it systemati-
cally impairs the firm’ s ability to sense environmental

changes, seize market opportunities, or transform organ-
isational processes. Subsequently, the discussion demon-
strates how blockchain adoption, through its distinctive 
technological properties of immutability, transparency, 
decentralised consensus, and smart contract functional-
ity, addresses these capability frictions and enables the 
transformation from weakened to enhanced dynamic 
capabilities. This analytical structure facilitates the devel-
opment of five propositions that theorise the mechanisms 
through which blockchain implementation systemati-

seizing, and transformation dimensions, thereby con-
tributing to supply chain resilience in dynamic and 
uncertain environments.
Information inaccuracy
During their individual interviews, upstream supplier 
activities, plans, and risks were frequently discussed by 
the focal firm representatives, who reported that these 
factors became increasingly more important during the 
pandemic. They further pointed out that most of their 
Tier 1 suppliers did not have complete information about 
their own upstream supplier’ s production challenges. 
Many Tier 1 suppliers also lacked the resources to accu-
rately map their supply chains, requiring the focal firm to 
utilise its own resources for this process. The focal firm 
relied on its suppliers to understand their long-term fore-
casts, which often fluctuated with market demand. The 
focal firm also relied on suppliers to provide accurate 
delivery information to manage their production process 
more efficiently. In this context, Senior Buyer−Sourcing 
Global Commodities at the firm (labelled as Procurement 
A, see Table 1) noted, ‘ . . . the quality of information that 
we received from our suppliers wasn’t perfect and there 
were a lot of gaps . . . . ’
Additionally, the focal firm was worried that they did 
not know where the critical suppliers were positioned 
along the chain, as was the case with they mapped one of 
their electronic supply lines back to Tier 7 to determine 
critical weak points in the chain. This brought to light the 
lack of knowledge of where the critical points in their sup-
ply line were, as was discussed by Procurement B, Global 
Purchasing Development Director: ‘ . . . for us the most 
important thing is when you get all this information, to 
understand who [is] in your supplier chain and maybe 
what we know today about the supply chain is totally 
wrong. ’ This person continued, ‘So that’ s what we count 
on, this full transparency, to understand exactly who our 
suppliers are, the most critical ones, the ones that maybe 
we never heard about. ’ This lack of knowledge of critical 
suppliers or incorrect understanding of critical suppli-
ers added to the inaccuracy of procurement information. 
Through dissemination of blockchain throughout their 
supply chain, the focal firm was counting on finding those 
critical suppliers that might not be known today but that 
might have a large impact on business in the future.
Trusting the accuracy of information was another 
source of concern for the focal firm. Their forecasts were 
developed through production meetings with down-
stream customers, but often did not include upstream 
supply chain partners. Forecast information was shared 
via spreadsheets that were emailed to suppliers as updates 
were made, causing delays in information sharing and 
requiring suppliers to read complex datasheets to deter-
mine changes to forecasts. One of the focal firm repre-
sentatives expressed their concerns as follows: ‘ . . . first 
problem that you see when lack of transparency or even 
having the transparency, the accuracy of the information 
you provide to others’ (Logistics B, Supply Chain Spe-
cialist, Focal Firm). These concerns were echoed by one 
supplier, who noted: ‘Either you rely on the information 
provided by your supplier, your transport supplier, but 
the reliability of that information is not at the level we 
want everywhere in the world’ (Supplier A, VP of Sales).
The focal firm utilised blockchain to handle its infor-
mation sharing with upper-tier suppliers with whom the 
focal firm does not have a direct relationship. Accord-
ingly, one of the goals of the MDS Sponsoring project 
was to bring to light those unknown suppliers and use 
the trust in blockchain to provide correct information. A 
representative from the focal firm elaborated on this issue 
as follows:
‘Due to some reasonable risks, we decide to trust the 
blockchain technology to do this as we don’t really need 
to spend so much time on double checking, triple check-
ing what the supplier is telling us because we know that 
this is from a trustworthy source and we know any-
way that if something does not go okay, there are plenty 
of possibilities for them to be replaced’ (referring to 
non-key suppliers). (Procurement B, Global Purchasing 
Development Director, Focal Firm)
Additionally, the focal firm utilised blockchain to cre-
ate trust with Tier 1 suppliers, ensuring that their infor-
mation is safe and will be accessed only by those who 
need to see it. This blockchain functionality, based on 
smart contracts and permissions, was the driving force 
for improving information accuracy and sharing, rather 
than monitoring suppliers: ‘ . . . we are using this kind of 
technology, this is protected all the way, no one can access 
it except the person who really needs it, and this is the 
way we are somehow paving the road to build this kind of 
trust’ (Procurement B, Global Purchasing Development 
Director, Focal Firm).

Risks of information sharing
The reticence of some suppliers to share adequate infor-
mation was another transparency barrier uncovered dur-
ing the interviews. From the perspective of the focal firm, 
protectionism is widespread in this supply chain, as most 
of its suppliers are located in China and Russia, so trade 
tensions can compromise supply efficiency. The focal 
firm and its suppliers were also concerned with sharing 
proprietary information with other actors in the supply 
chain. Many of the suppliers manufactured components 
designed by the focal firm, and their apprehension about 
these designs getting into the hands of their competitors 
or other unauthorised entities was a real concern. This 
was highlighted by the focal firm representative when he 
remarked, ‘ . . . not as a disruption but as a big barrier, 
are the increasing protectionism of different commercial 
blocks and the kind of cold commercial war being held 
right now between China and the US’ (Procurement B, 
Global Purchasing Development Director, Focal Firm). 
These concerns were also mentioned by one of their sup-
pliers, who stated: ‘How much are we willing to share? 
Because some of it is proprietary, some of it is considered 
internal. So, one barrier is the will to share, the ability to 
share based on guidelines’ (Supplier B, Head of Supply 
Chain Europe).
As discussed by the IT specialist from the focal firm, 
one of the key reasons for protectionism is the risk of 
cyber-attacks, as well as misinformation about shipping 
dates coming downstream and/or misinformation about 
production needs going upstream:
. . . suppliers might get the wrong idea about future fore-
casts or what we actually need and when we need it, 
which may disrupt our operations in our factory, or it 
may create conflicts that are not supposed to be there if 
we get a middleman manipulating the data. (Engineering 
A, Chief Enterprise Architect, Focal Firm)
As a part of the MDS Sponsoring project initiated by 
the focal firm, blockchain was utilised to address these 
barriers. Blockchain was seen as the most suitable tech-
nology for streamlining information flows in the supply 
chain and overcoming obstacles such as cost and data 
misuse concerns, particularly from Tier 1 supply chain 
partners. The Global Purchasing Development Director 
of the focal firm (denoted as Procurement B in Table 1) 
explained their rationale:
. . . we will have to go to the supplier and ask them to 
disclose information of every possible nature you can 
imagine. So, if we do not have a solid ecosystem sup-
ported by a good technology, it would be very difficult 
to convince or to get the supplier buy-in to start this 
disclosing for the same reasons we have already talked 
about.
The focal firm also acknowledges that blockchain 
technology can facilitate early recognition of changes in 
supply and demand within their supply chain, as dis-
cussed by Procurement B, Global Purchasing Develop-
ment Director:
The arguments that we are using in order to convince 
our supplier base to follow us in this particular initia-
tive (implementing blockchain) is that the more trans-
parency you get, the more early warnings you get for your 
business. [. . . ]
By having early access to both supply and demand 
changes, firms can better coordinate resources and han-
dle disruptions. The focal firm discussed how shipping 
companies scrapped container ships because they could 
not see the larger picture that demand for containers 
would be increasing in a few months. With all firms 
on the same system, here the blockchain, the focal firm 
was confident that the early detection of changes in the 
market would be beneficial to managing resources more 
effectively for all stakeholders in the supply chain, not just 
the OEM. This future state of increased transparency was 
considered crucial to combating future disruptions such 
as those seen during the pandemic.
Concern over risk is always present when implement-
ing new technology, especially when confidential infor-
mation is shared across the entire supply chain. The 
focal firm acknowledges these risks but believes that 
blockchain provides a sufficiently secure platform for 
information sharing. One of its representatives empha-
sised the importance of demonstrating this security to 
their suppliers to encourage wider adoption:
W e needed to really take a reasonable risk. So, if we all 
agree that this technology (Blockchain) is safe to a cer-
tain extent, there is nothing 100% safe, we all need to 
accept that. But if we know that the risk is reasonable, 
I don’t think there should be any kind of concerns, and 
it is important that someone starts doing that. (Procure-
ment B, Global Purchasing Development Director, Focal 
Firm)
This statement reflects the focal firm’ s approach to 
managing risk and fostering trust in new technologies

During the interview, the complexity of information

Lack of communication protocols
The absence of a common communication protocol was 
identified by several interviewees as an additional trans-
parency barrier. The focal firm, being a part of the indus-
try known for creating standards like the Electronic Data 
Interchange (EDI) system, views the establishment of 
standard rules for information sharing as a critical issue 
to be addressed. Reflecting on this, the focal firm’ s Logis-
tics Manager (Logistics C) stated, ‘If we talk about trans-
parency, we would like to have one common standard 
such as an API interface and interaction, defining exactly 
the technical specification and what kind of informa-
tion should be exchanged and how . . . . ’ This observation 
highlights their desire for a streamlined, standardised 
approach to data exchange and transparency. This sen-
timent was shared by one of their suppliers, who noted: 
‘Europe is working differently from North America with 
different information systems and practices and then 
between products also you may have differences because 
of industry’ (Supplier A, VP of Sales).
Furthermore, while the lack of infrastructure to sup-
port this transparency was acknowledged as a barrier by 
both the focal firm and the suppliers, it was seen as a 
challenge that needed addressing rather than as a major 
obstacle. This viewpoint is reinforced by the Head of 
Supply Chain Europe at Supplier B, who noted, ‘ Another 
barrier obviously are the systems, the infrastructure, the 
technology. ’ This comment underscores the technologi-
cal aspects of the transparency barrier, indicating that, 
while the infrastructure is a concern, it is considered 
manageable within the broader context of improving the 
communication and transparency standards in the indus-
try.
T o contribute to this industry-wide endeavour, the 
focal firm collaborates closely with other OEMs in the 
manufacturing industry to standardise communication 
processes and considers blockchain technology as benefi-
cial for this process. Renowned for originally implement-
ing the EDI system now widely used in manufacturing 
industry supply chains, the focal firm is taking a simi-
lar pioneering role with blockchain. They are using this 
technology to track material data sheets in the industry, 
a task undertaken by DXC, a firm with significant pene-
tration in the automotive industry and with partnerships 
with all major OEMs. Reflecting on the transition to this 
new technology, one of the focal firm’ s representatives 
stated, ‘So this is what we expect blockchain to become, 
this kind of protocol where you say, come on, you cannot 
challenge this. Are you trying to tell me that they’re going 
to do business and not use blockchain?’ (Procurement B, 
Global Purchasing Development Director):
. . . now everything is kind of more user-friendly for 
everyone to use. So, we are removing a lot of barriers 
that were there preventing us from getting the flow of 
information. (Procurement B, Global Purchasing Devel-
opment Director)
The overarching objective of this strategy is to have 
all major OEMs using this unified system, which will 
subsequently be extended to upper-tier suppliers. Given

Discussion
Our research provides interesting findings around firms 
lacking trust in sharing information among different sup-
ply partners, thereby reducing collaboration and long-
term commitment beyond first tier suppliers, as high-
lighted in Choi and Siqin (2022), Dubey et al. (2020), 
Kumar et al. (2022), and Qian and Papadonikolaki 
(2021). Our study reveals that trust is still needed to 
manage the business relationships, but blockchain, with 
its smart contracts, can be used to mitigate the lack of 
trust with upper tier suppliers, a point discussed by Bai 
and Sarkis (2020), Sodhi and Tang (2019), and Mon-
tecchi, Plangger, and W est (2021). This in turn creates 
a communication platform and protocol for increas-
ing capabilities of information symmetry, information 
sharing, collaborative decision making, and standardised 
communications. Through increased information shar-
ing, firms can adjust their resources and mitigate many 
supply chain disruption risks, while enhancing their col-
laboration with supply chain partners. These capabilities, 
created with blockchain, will contribute to increased trust 
in the technology and the platform, and more widespread 
adoption in the supply chain. How blockchain can create 
sensing, seizing, and transformational capabilities within 
a firm’ s supply chain will now be discussed along with a 
presentation of the research propositions.
Sensing capability with blockchain
Firms that can capture and identify opportunities in their 
outside environment (i.e. supply chains) through the util-
isation of blockchain technology and enhance their capa-
bility to create value from business opportunities (Zhu, 
Bai, and Sarkis 2022). T o attain these benefits, firms must 
continuously capture and filter information from their 
professional contacts (i.e. supply chain partners), while 
sensing changes in customer needs and market condi-
tions, and making business decisions that respond to 
this new operating environment (Hallikas, Immonen, 
and Brax 2021; T eece 2007). While the focal firm exam-
ined in this study was aware of these requirements, the 
quality of the information provided by its suppliers was 
inadequate for performing the aforementioned tasks. The 
information received from the supply chain partners was 
often incomplete or in diverse formats, as Procurement 
A, Senior Buyer−Sourcing Global Commodities noted, 
‘the quality of information that we received from our sup-
pliers wasn’t perfect and there were a lot of gaps, ’ resulting 
in disruptions and inefficiencies, while posing a risk of 
breaching regulatory standards. Additionally, the infor-
mation provided by the focal firm was often too complex 
for suppliers to interpret, leading to issues when forecast 
changes and increased purchase orders for components

were not accurately identified or adequately planned 
for by suppliers. Thus, inadequate information, whether 
in terms of accuracy, completeness, or uniformity, was 
recognised as a barrier and created capability frictions 
with asymmetric information. T o overcome these chal-
lenges, firms can adopt blockchain technology to create 
a trustworthy platform for information and knowledge 
sharing. Pattanayak, Ramkumar, et al. (2024) came to 
similar conclusions in their research, where they found 
that suppliers want accurate, timely, and relevant infor-
mation and are more willing to share information when 
these criteria are met. By integrating blockchain into their 
systems, firms can enhance their sensing capabilities, as 
trust in data is implicit, and such reliable information 
can be used for rapid identification of and response to 
supply chain disruptions. Here the blockchain technol-
ogy as a digital enabler can enhance weakened capa-
bilities of sensing, helping firms sense disruptions early 
through better visibility of accurate and timely informa-
tion (Pattanayak, Arputham, et al. 2024). For the focal 
firm’ s forecast, smart contracts with blockchain can be 
used to reduce the complexity of Excel sheets, only exe-
cuting instructions when certain conditions at the focal 
firm were met, for example releasing POs to suppliers as 
per actual customer demand, thereby reducing the need 
for large, complex Excel sheets. Smart contracts them-
selves do not directly highlight information, but they 
can be designed to manage and process data in a way 
that makes important information more accessible and 
actionable (Samanta, Sarkar, and Chaki 2021), decreasing 
the issue of missing important forecasting information 
between suppliers.
Through blockchain implementation, the focal firm 
transforms from information uncertainty to informa-
tion confidence, a fundamental enhancement in sens-
ing capability. This enhanced sensing capability mani-
fests in several ways: First, the focal firm gains real-time 
visibility into supplier operations across multiple tiers, 
enabling early identification of critical dependencies. As 
Procurement B, Global Purchasing Development Direc-
tor explained, the goal is ‘to understand exactly who 
our suppliers are, the most critical ones, the ones that 
maybe we never heard about. ’ Second, smart contracts 
transform complex forecast communication into auto-
mated, actionable information, reducing supplier inter-
pretation errors and enabling proactive production plan-
ning. Third, the trusted platform enables continuous 
environmental scanning rather than periodic, manually 
verified reporting (i.e. excel spreadsheets). The focal firm 
no longer needs to ‘spend so much time on double check-
ing, triple checking what the supplier is telling us’ (Pro-
curement B, Global Purchasing Development Director), 
freeing resources for strategic analysis rather than data 
verification. This transformation represents a shift from 
reactive gap-filling to proactive, comprehensive supply 
chain intelligence, the essence of enhanced sensing capa-
bility. Therefore, the first proposition arising from the 
current study findings is:
Proposition 1: Blockchain implementation in supply 
chains enhances supply chain partners’ sensing capabilities 
by providing real-time access to accurate and immutable 
transaction data across the entire supply chain.
Empirical evidence further indicates that the risk of 
share information, often due to fears of losing compet-
itive advantage or data breaches, intensifies information 
sharing challenges, limiting the flow of crucial data across 
the supply chain (Sharma et al. 2021; Sodhi and Tang 
2019; Y adav et al. 2020). Protectionism by supply part-
ners, the risk of proprietary information leakage, and 
information security concerns also create frictions with 
the focal firms sensing capabilities by limiting timely 
access to the information needed to make better deci-

its encryption and smart contracts capabilities ensure 
that information is only accessible to authorised sup-

This secure environment fosters a culture of trust among 
the supply chain partners, thus facilitating the sharing of 
timely and accurate information, reducing supply chain 
stakeholders’ uncertainties, and promoting closer collab-
oration (Montecchi, Plangger, and W est 2021).
Blockchain’ s secure architecture transforms the focal 
firm’ s sensing capability from a limited, first-tier perspec-
tive to a multi-tier collaborative intelligence network. 
Previously, as Supplier B, Head of Supply Chain Europe

‘increasing protectionism of different commercial blocks’

tor), severely constrained information flow and collabo-

Through blockchain adoption, this barrier dissolves. 
The enhanced sensing capability that emerges is charac-

combination of the two. If firms are constantly reconfig-

the creation of dynamic sensing capabilities. Blockchain, 
through secure and smart contract-driven communica-

potential for the creation of dynamic sensing capabilities 
in the supply chain.
The focal firm understood that they needed to man-

blockchain. T o them there is no 100% safe technology, but

From the perspective of dynamic capability, where

sensing capabilities of firms. Blockchain can provide this 
through the immutability of information and the use of 
smart contracts to alert suppliers to potential issues due 
to disruptions, thereby enhancing a firm’ s lower-order 
capabilities and its capacity to adjust resources efficiently 
(Fainshmidt et al. 2016).
Seizing capability with blockchain
The dynamic capabilities framework underscores the 
necessity for organisations not only to sense environ-
mental changes but also to seize opportunities arising 
from these changes. seizing ability refers to the capacity 
to assess and control the specifications needed to recon-
figure resources to effectively respond to new opportu-
nities (Zhu, Bai, and Sarkis 2022). This involves shar-
ing the information gathered during the sensing stage 
with suppliers to adjust resource allocation (i.e. to miti-
gate a supply disruption). seizing is a fundamental ability 
of a firm to create and use decision-making processes 
to exploit opportunities and create value with existing 
resources (Foerstl et al. 2021). Transparency provided by 
blockchain technology could increase the level of trust 
among the supply chain partners, which is crucial for 
seizing opportunities. When suppliers trust the accuracy 
and authenticity of the information the focal firm makes 
available through the blockchain, they are more likely 
to engage in collaborative efforts to reconfigure supply 
chain operations in response to disruptions and mar-
ket demand fluctuations. The current body of research

The transparency and trust enabled by blockchain

were sensed, the lack of trusted information prevented 
swift collaborative response. Suppliers questioned the

Blockchain’ s role in ensuring data integrity and trust

as blockchain ensure a timely and accurate transfer of 
information that will significantly impact the effective-

current ERP systems (Benstead et al. 2024). Supply chains

ible and adaptable, enabling the integration, construc-
tion, and adjustment of both internal and external skills

Acquiring the skills to effectively respond to shift-
ing market needs and environmental changes is essential 
for effective supply chain management, as demonstrated 
by empirical data. Through greater information shar-
ing, firms increase their capacity for sensing changes 
in the markets and in customer requirements. Through 
enhanced collaboration with stakeholders, they are also 
better equipped to gather business and regulatory intel-
ligence. According to T eece, Pisano, and Shuen (1997), 
this high level of information sharing is essential for sur-

talise on opportunities and enhance value using exist-

be enhanced using common communication protocols.

dynamic capabilities can be disseminated easily through-
out the organisation and can allow better adjustments to 
be made in changing environments. Blockchain can facil-
itate this dissemination of information throughout the 
supply chain, thereby creating a basis for the creation of 
dynamic capabilities.
Transformation with blockchain
The empirical evidence emphasises the ability of a firm 
to integrate, build, and reconfigure its internal and exter-
nal competences to address rapidly changing environ-
ments. In the context of organisational transformation, 
blockchain and other digital technologies, such as AI and 
IoT , can leverage this transformation in the procurement 
process.
The focal firm recognised that future regulatory 
demands, including modern slavery, CO2 emissions, and 
conflict minerals disclosure, would require increasingly 
comprehensive information sharing. As Procurement B 
explained, suppliers would need to ‘disclose information 
of every possible nature you can imagine, ’ yet ‘they all 
agree that they can jeopardize their business if this infor-
mation is used in an inappropriate way. So that’ s why our

governed information sharing (Montecchi, Plangger, and

information to me misused in the process.
Blockchain’ s flexible architecture addresses these bar-

2019; Saberi et al. 2019). Permission-based access con-

tive transparency, sharing regulatory data while protect-
ing proprietary information, resolving the transparency-
confidentiality paradox.

tion capability emerges across three dimensions: First,

ance to anticipatory capability building. As Procurement

right now, compared to the ones 40 years ago, changed

tal organisational change. Second, network orchestration

Theoretical contributions
This study makes four primary theoretical contributions 
to supply chain management and dynamic capabilities 
literature, advancing understanding of how digital tech-
nologies enable organisational capabilities in complex, 
dynamic environments. In a rapidly evolving global envi-
ronment, where supply chain disruptions and regulatory 
pressures increasingly demand adaptability, the strate-
gic use of digital technologies is critical. By integrat-
ing insights from the dynamic capability’ s framework, 
including the contingent value of blockchain under vary-
ing environmental conditions (Schilke 2014) and the 
hierarchical interplay between higher- and lower-order 
capabilities (Fainshmidt et al. 2016), this study offers a 
nuanced understanding of how firms can align technol-
ogy adoption with strategic decision-making to navigate 
complexity and volatility effectively.
Supply chain transparency barriers drive what we 
term ‘capability frictions, ’ a state where informational 
asymmetries, lack of transparency, and weak data gov-
ernance impede the firm’ s ability to effectively deploy 
dynamic capabilities. This friction manifests through 
four mechanisms that directly weaken sensing, seizing, 
and transformation capabilities: information inaccuracy 
prevents reliable environmental scanning; risks associ-
ated with information sharing limit stakeholder collabo-
ration; absent communication protocols hinder resource 
coordination; and compatibility concerns about future 
communications reduce the firm’ s capacity for organisa-
tional adaptation and process reconfiguration.
These transparency barriers create distinct patterns 
of capability deficiencies across three dimensions. First, 
when information flows are characterised by inaccuracy 
and delays (information asymmetries, lack of communi-
cations protocols), firms experience diminished capac-
ity for regulatory monitoring, environmental scanning, 
and business knowledge creation (Hallikas, Immonen, 
and Brax 2021; T eece 2007), causing managers to adjust 
slowly to rapid changes in their operating environments 
(Eisenhardt and Martin 2000). Second, lack of trans-
parency capability friction undermines seizing capabil-
ities by constraining stakeholder collaboration, limiting 
resource realignment, and compromising risk manage-
ment effectiveness (Foerstl et al. 2021; Zhu, Bai, and 
Sarkis 2022). The reluctance to share information (risk 
of information sharing), stemming from fears of com-
promising competitive advantage or experiencing data 
breaches, intensifies these challenges, restricting the flow 
of crucial data across the supply chain (Sharma et al. 
2021; Sodhi and Tang 2019; Y adav et al. 2020). This is 
particularly problematic given that alliance management

capabilities create competitive advantages most effec-
tively under moderate environmental dynamism (Schilke 
2014), precisely when information sharing becomes crit-
ical. Third, inadequate data governance constrains trans-
formation capabilities by limiting organisational adapta-
tion (future communications compatibility) and process 
reconfiguration potential (Hallikas, Immonen, and Brax 
2021; T eece 2007). Without visibility into partner opera-
tions and reliable data, firms cannot effectively redesign 
supply chain networks or implement the business model 
innovations necessary for sustained competitive advan-
tage (T eece 2018).
Blockchain technology serves as a digital enabler that 
systematically addresses these weakened dynamic capa-
bilities by mitigating the underlying transparency barri-
ers. Through its distinctive properties of immutability, 
transparency, and decentralised consensus (Treiblmaier 
2018; Y aga et al. 2019), blockchain establishes a tech-
nological foundation for enhanced sensing, seizing, and 
transformation capabilities across supply chain networks. 
Its capacity for secure data sharing, permanent transac-
tion recording, and process automation through smart 
contracts (Rejeb, Keogh, and Treiblmaier 2019; Saberi 
et al. 2019) enables firms to overcome the informational 
asymmetries and governance weaknesses that generate 
capability frictions.
Blockchain adoption facilitates transformation from 
weakened to enhanced dynamic capabilities across 
three interconnected dimensions. First, firms develop 
improved environmental scanning capabilities that facil-
itate real-time monitoring of regulatory changes, mar-
ket conditions, and supply chain operations, thereby 
enhancing their ability to sense disruptions early through 
superior visibility of accurate and timely information 
(Pattanayak, Arputham, et al. 2024). Second, enhanced 
opportunity alignment emerges as blockchain facili-
tates coordinated responses among supply chain part-
ners through standardised communication protocols and 
secure information-sharing mechanisms (Benstead et al. 
2024), enabling firms to seize market opportunities 
rapidly through synchronised resource deployment and 
collaborative decision-making processes (Foerstl et al. 
2021; Zhu, Bai, and Sarkis 2022). Third, agile transfor-
mation capacity develops as firms acquire the capabil-
ity to reconfigure supply chain networks, adapt business 
processes, and respond to environmental volatility with 
greater speed and precision (Centobelli et al. 2022). These 
enhanced capabilities represent a fundamental shift from 
reactive, friction-constrained operations towards proac-
tive, seamlessly coordinated supply chain management.
T echnologies create contingent value across differ-
ent levels of the supply chain due to varying envi-
ronmental volatilities. As the focal firm implemented 
blockchain to comply with new EU regulations rather 
than in response to crisis, the resulting capabilities 
become potentially more valuable, enabling proactive 
resource adaptation. Expanding on Schilke’ s (2014) 
research showing that alliance management capabili-
ties create competitive advantage under moderate envi-
ronmental dynamism, blockchain technology mitigates 
transparency barriers and creates more collaboration 
among supply chain stakeholders, thereby enhancing 
value creation and jointly reducing risks. From a strate-
gic decision-making perspective, firms that understand 
the contingent value created through blockchain-enabled 
dynamic capabilities can allocate resources more effec-
tively and choose strategies best suited to the current 
business environment.
By addressing capability frictions at both hierarchi-
cal levels, blockchain adoption supports both higher-
order dynamic capabilities (designing adaptive supply 
chain networks) and lower-order dynamic capabilities 
(executing real-time transparency solutions). Blockchain 
enhances lower-order capabilities by facilitating access 
to accurate information, enabling trust building among 
partners, and supporting cooperative decision-making 
processes related to production scheduling and inven-
tory management (Bai and Sarkis 2020). As Fainsh-
midt et al. (2016) observe, these lower-order dynamic 
capabilities can be disseminated readily throughout 
the organisation, enabling more effective adjustments 
in response to changing environmental conditions. By 
transforming capability friction into seamless coordi-
nation through decentralised and immutable record-
keeping (Addo-T enkorang and Helo 2016; Choi and 
Siqin 2022), blockchain technologies reduce the need 
for human intervention in processes, allowing firms to 
expand operations without the risk of inefficiencies or 
human error (W olf, Louw, and Palm 2024).
Lastly, this study contributes to understanding how 
firms should collaborate with suppliers to increase trans-
parency and overcome both transparency barriers and 
resulting capability frictions, a research opportunity 
discussed in Sodhi and Tang (2019). For successful 
blockchain deployment, focal firms must lead in design-
ing and implementing networks across the supply chain 
to ensure benefits for all stakeholders (W ang, Chen, 
and Zghari-Sales 2021). Blockchain governance relies 
on automated, code-based regulations rather than legal 
enforcement (contractual governance) or future relation-
ship value (relational governance) (Lumineau, W ang, and 
Schilke 2021). The empirical evidence demonstrates how 
focal firms can leverage both relational mechanisms with 
Tier 1 suppliers and blockchain’ s built-in protocols to 
govern suppliers where contracts or trusted relationships

are absent, providing the governance mechanism neces-
sary for transparency across all supply chain tiers.
Practical implications
The results yielded by our study also have several practi-
cal implications. Our capability frictions framework and 
empirical findings offer actionable guidance for man-
agers and policymakers seeking to enhance supply chain 
transparency and resilience through blockchain adop-
tion. Rather than viewing blockchain as a generic tech-
nological solution, practitioners should understand it as 
a digital enabler that systematically addresses capability 
deficiencies by reducing frictions across sensing, seiz-
ing, and transformation dimensions. First, blockchain 
shows promise as a solution capable of overcoming bar-
riers to transparency in the supply chain. By providing 
an accurate, secure, and immutable record of transac-
tions, blockchain technology can foster a collaborative 
trust-based environment, prompting the supply chain 
partners to more readily share the pertinent information 
needed for collaborative planning and demand forecast-
ing with other stakeholders. Blockchain’ s use in cryp-
tocurrency applications for secure data transfers has been 
extensively studied (Agrawal et al. 2021; Rejeb, Keogh, 
and Treiblmaier 2019; Saberi et al. 2019; Y aga et al. 
2019); this study expands knowledge of how managers 
can leverage blockchain technology to enhance strategic 
decision-making capabilities in the automotive manu-
facturing industry. Companies that can recognise and 
seize possibilities in their external environment (e.g. sup-
ply chains) by leveraging blockchain technology improve 
their potential to generate value from business prospects 
(Zhu, Bai, and Sarkis 2022). T o achieve these advan-
tages, companies must consistently gather and analyse 
information from their professional networks (i.e. sup-
ply chain partners), while perceiving shifts in customer 
demands and market dynamics, and making business 
decisions that adapt to this evolving operational land-
scape (Hallikas, Immonen, and Brax 2021). Given the 
dynamic nature of global supply chains, which require 
agility and flexibility from managers, blockchain tech-
nology can serve as a tool for achieving these objectives. 
Blockchain solutions ensure data immutability, enhanc-
ing trust in information reliability and promoting effi-
cient information sharing. They also empower firms to 
rapidly adapt to market changes and sustain their sensing 
and seizing skills.
Existing supply chains, particularly in the automotive 
industry, are built on long-term relationships with key 
suppliers, working together to effectively respond to sup-
ply disruptions and demand fluctuations. This research 
has shown that such trust-based relationships are impor-
tant for firms planning to implement digital technolo-
gies, such as blockchain, to increase transparency and 
information sharing. By leveraging these trusted supplier 
relationships, managers can more effectively implement 
blockchain into their supply chains, highlighting the ben-
efits of increased information sharing. Additionally, these 
supplier partnerships will be of paramount importance 
for expanding the adoption of blockchain farther up the 
supply chain, which was the goal of the focal company in 
this study. From this perspective, blockchain is primarily 
seen as a trusted, secure, and common communication 
channel for information sharing, rather than as a way of 
decreasing stakeholder opportunism. Accordingly, man-
agers can use their existing relationships with suppliers 
to build a blockchain system that works for everyone 
involved, allowing all stakeholders to contribute to a 
more secure and trusted collaborative ecosystem.
From a policy perspective, government regulators 
could help firms to implement blockchain technology 
through more financial incentives and knowledge cre-
ation. The focal firm opted to finance the rollout of 
blockchain internally to all suppliers in their supply 
chain. This was driven by new regulations that require 
more detailed material data sheets for manufactured 
equipment to facilitate afterlife recycling. This additional 
cost could be an undue burden for other firms in sup-
ply chains affected by similar government regulations. 
More incentive programmes and education through gov-
ernment subsidised programmes could lead to more 
widespread adoption of blockchain, as well as other dig-
ital technologies. More funding opportunities, similar to 
the EU blockchain funding and investment programme 
(European Commission 2024), could ease the barrier of 
cost to blockchain implementation. Additionally, edu-
cation programmes discussing privacy issues that hin-
der organisations from adopting blockchain technologies 
could be beneficial.
Conclusion and future research
This study demonstrates that supply chain transparency 
barriers function as capability frictions that system-
atically weaken firms’ dynamic capabilities, and that 
blockchain adoption serves as a digital enabler that trans-
forms these frictions into enhanced sensing, seizing, and 
transformation capabilities. Through an in-depth case 
study of a Swedish OEM and its suppliers, four trans-
parency barriers were identified – information inaccu-
racy, risks of information sharing, lack of communica-
tion protocols, and future communications compatibil-
ity – demonstrating how each creates specific friction 
22 
 E. RAGAN AND M. H. ESLAMI
patterns that impair organisational capabilities. The pri-
mary theoretical contribution is the capability frictions 
framework, which reconceptualises transparency barri-
ers not merely as operational challenges but rather as 
systematic capability deficiencies that prevent firms from 
effectively deploying dynamic capabilities in supply chain 
environments.
The five propositions developed through this inves-
tigation theorise the mechanisms through which block
chain’ s distinctive properties – immutability, trans-
parency, smart contracts, and standardised protocols 
– systematically reduce capability frictions, thereby 
enabling cascading capability enhancement across sens-
ing, seizing, and transformation dimensions. Through 
blockchain integration, firms can overcome inefficien-
cies caused by incomplete or inaccurate data, foster 
trust through secure information sharing, and facil-
itate improved decision-making and resource align-
ment through real-time, accurate data across the supply 
chain. This enhanced transparency strengthens collab-
oration with supply chain partners while positioning 
firms to meet evolving regulatory requirements and con-
sumer demands for greater accountability. Furthermore, 
blockchain’ s potential to transform procurement pro-
cesses and supplier relationships underscores its role as a 
catalyst for organisational change and sustained compet-
itive advantage in dynamic supply chain environments.
The findings emphasise the strategic importance of 
blockchain as a foundational technology for modern sup-
ply chain management. As global supply chains become 
increasingly interconnected and complex, blockchain 
adoption will play a pivotal role in enabling firms to 
remain competitive, comply with regulatory demands, 
and respond effectively to market dynamics and disrup-
tions. Future research should explore how blockchain can 
be further integrated with emerging technologies such 
as artificial intelligence and Internet of Things sensors 
to optimise supply chain transparency and collaborative 
capabilities.
When interpreting these findings, however, several 
limitations must be considered. The findings are based 
on analysis of a single OEM in the Swedish manufac-
turing industry sector operating within a specific regu-
latory context (EU product passport compliance), thus 
limiting generalisability across industries and regulatory 
environments. Additionally, the focal firm’ s blockchain 
adoption was motivated by proactive compliance rather 
than crisis response, which may affect transferability 
to reactive implementation scenarios. Future research 
should explore blockchain implementation under vary-
ing conditions of environmental dynamism, such as post-
disruption recovery or when tracking responsible labour 
practices and carbon emission compliance. Longitudi-
nal studies examining how capability frictions evolve 
as blockchain technology matures would provide valu-
able insights into the temporal dynamics of capability 
transformation. Lastly, complementary theoretical per-
spectives beyond dynamic capabilities warrant investiga-
tion. Agency theory and network theory could illuminate 
mechanisms preventing transparency and information 
sharing, while institutional theory might explain how 
regulatory and normative pressures shape blockchain 
adoption patterns across different organisational and 
institutional contexts.
