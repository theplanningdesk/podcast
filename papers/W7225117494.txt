ABSTRACT
Experiences from real-world supply chains as well as exhaustive research show that the lack of
coordinationwithinsupplychainsleadstobullwhipeffect,inwhichthemagnitudeoffluctuations
inordersincreasesintheupstreamdirectionfromretailertothesupplier.Onepotentialsolution
for addressing this phenomenon is information sharing between supply chain actors that can
reducetheimpactoffluctuationsincustomerdemandonthesupplychain.However,sharingtoo
much information might lead to higher vulnerability of stakeholders in negotiations with their
partners or to an unwanted leakage of information to competitors. Therefore, relevant actors are
often reluctant to share the necessary information.
To overcome these problems, we propose a horizontal information sharing model where
retailers share demand information in form of average values between each other without
revealing the exact values of each individual partner. This could be achieved by, e.g., applying
costly cryptographic methods. Therefore, we evaluate the potential effects of this type of
informationsharingonthewholesupplychainbydevelopingasimulationmodelrepresentinga
4-echelonsupplychain,whichconsistsofmultiplecustomers,eachservedbytheirownretailer,
one wholesaler, one manufacturer and one supplier. We investigate the effect of horizontal
information sharing of average customer demand among the retailers on the bullwhip effect
and show its effects on the different supply chain actors. In addition, the influences of demand
variability and demand correlations between customers, different forecasting methods, safety
stock approaches, stochastic lead times and the number of retailers sharing the information on
the bullwhip effect are analyzed.
1. Introduction
Supply chains (SCs) are complex structures that connect multiple companies responsible for delivering products
and services to end customers. Effective coordination and information sharing across these actors are widely seen as
keyenablersofresponsiveandresilientSCs.Yet,firmsareoftenreluctanttoshareoperationaldata,especiallydetailed
demand information, because they fear losing bargaining power, exposing cost structures, or violating confidentiality
constraints (Sahai, Singh and Dayama, 2020). These frictions can contribute to well-known SC distortions, most
prominently the bullwhip effect (Dejonckheere, Disney, Lambrecht and Towill, 2004).
Lee,PadmanabhanandWhang(1997)describethebullwhipeffectasthephenomenonwhereorderstothesupplier
exhibit larger variance than sales to the buyer and this variance amplification propagates upstream. One operational
driveristhatretailersandupstreampartnersrelyonforecastsformedfromnoisylocaldemandsignals.Whenaretailer
experiences a temporary demand surge, she may interpret it as a lasting trend, adjust her forecast upward, and place
a large order; if other echelons do not see the underlying customer demand, the resulting order variability can be
amplified as it travels upstream Lee et al. (1997).
Anaturalresponseistoenrichtheinformationavailableforforecasting.Classicalverticalinformationsharing,such
as passing point-of-sale (POS) data or inventory positions upstream, has been shown to mitigate the bullwhip effect
in many settings (Cannella and Ciancimino, 2010; Croson and Donohue, 2003; Chen, Drezner, Ryan and Simchi-
Levi, 2000a). At the same time, modern SCs often involve multiple retailers operating at the same echelon who face
related, but not identical, demand processes and who may compete in overlapping markets. In such multi-retailer
environments, there is potential value in exploiting horizontal information sharing at the retailer level: retailers might
improve their forecasts by conditioning not only on their own customer demand, but also on an aggregate signal that
reflects broader market conditions. Such horizontal information sharing between multiple participants at the same SC
echelon was partially investigated especially in game-theoretic contexts, where the positive effects of such strategies
ontheparticipatingactorscouldbeshown(Shi,Zhou,Wang,XuandXiong,2014;Lei,Liu,Deng,HuangandLeong,
2014).However,competitiveconcernsandregulatoryconstraints(e.g.,antitrustrulesonsensitivedataexchange)make

By answering these questions, we make three main contributions. First, we quantify the operational impact of

2. Literature review
Our work builds on two streams of literature: (i) studies of the bullwhip effect and information sharing in SCs and
(ii) models of multi-echelon SCs with multiple retailers and partial information sharing.
The bullwhip effect has been extensively studied in operations and supply chain management. Lee et al. (1997)
formalise the phenomenon of variance amplification as orders propagate upstream and identify operational drivers
such as demand-signal processing, order batching, price fluctuations, and rationing–gaming. Subsequent analytical
and simulation studies have quantified how forecasting methods, lead times, and information availability shape the
bullwhip effect in serial SCs. Chen et al. (2000a) and Chen, Ryan and Simchi-Levi (2000b) analyse the impact of
moving-average and exponential-smoothing forecasts; Dejonckheere et al. (2004) explore information enrichment
from a control-theoretic perspective; Chatfield, Kim, Harrison and Hayya (2004) examine stochastic lead times and
information quality.
A large part of literature deals with vertical information sharing, especially sharing downstream POS data or
inventory information with upstream echelons. Experimental work by Croson and Donohue (2003) highlights that
POS data sharing can reduce order oscillations and improve performance in the Beer game setting. Simulation and
analyticalstudies(e.g.,CannellaandCiancimino(2010),Constantino,DiGravio,ShabanandTronci(2014))showthat

coordinated inventory policies and richer demand visibility can significantly mitigate bullwhip and improve service
levels in multi-echelon SCs.
Real-world SCs often exhibit network structures with multiple retailers sourcing from shared upstream facilities.
Several studies analysed how this structure interacts with demand pooling, correlation, and information sharing. For
multi-retailer SCs, Cachon (1999) proposes a model with one supplier and multiple retailers with stochastic demands
and finds that reducing supplier demand variance with scheduled ordering policies can lower SC costs. Cachon and
Fisher(2000)lookattheoptionsofsharingdemandandinventorydatabetweenretailersandsupplierandcompareitto

thosecomingfromcuttingleadtimesandbatchsizes.Lau,HuangandMak(2004)investigatetheimpactofinformation
sharingondifferentechelonsina3-echelonSCandconcludethatsharinginformationbetweendownstreamactors(i.e.
retailers and distributors) has a higher positive impact than sharing information on upstream echelons.
The interaction of risk pooling and bullwhip effect in multi-retailer settings has also received attention. Khos-
roshahi, Husseini and Marjani (2016) discuss the bullwhip effect for a 3-echelon SC varying the number of retailers.
Sucky (2009) study risk pooling in a 3-echelon SC with two retailers, correlated demand and zero lead time. They
show that both order and demand variance of the wholesaler decrease as the correlation coefficient decreases.
Raghunathan (2003) analyses the impact of demand correlation across retailers on manufacturer surplus in a setting
withmultipleretailersandonemanufacturer.Heconcludesthatverticaldemandinformationsharingbetweenretailers
and manufacturer has the highest positive effect in case of negatively correlated or uncorrelated demand. Yin (2021)
discussestheimpactofcorrelationbetweenmultipleretailersmodeledviadifferentcopulassourcingfromonesupplier
on the bullwhip effect through simulation. They conclude that the selection of copula distribution has an influence on
the bullwhip effect.
Several papers consider partial or selective information sharing in multi-retailer environments. Huang and Iravani
(2005)discussasettingwithonecapacitatedsupplierandtworetailersunderselective-informationandfull-information
sharingwherethesupplierreceivesinformationfromonlyoneorbothretailersandintroducestheinformationpooling
effect. Dominguez, Cannella, Barbosa-Póvoa and Framinan (2018a) show that the reduction of the bullwhip effect
based on partial information sharing, i.e. only some retailers share information with the wholesaler, depends on
retailers’ operational factors in a 4-echelon SC with multiple heterogeneous retailers. An order variance prioritization
strategy for the reduction of the bullwhip effect in such SC is proposed in Dominguez, Cannella, Barbosa-Póvoa and
Framinan (2018b). Whereas all mentioned studies show the positive effects of information sharing, they are mostly
concerned with vertical information sharing between SC echelons.
Horizontal sharing of information between actors at the same SC echelon, which is studied in this paper, achieved
limitedattentionintheliteratureandthestudiesassumedisclosureoffullinformationbetweenallactors.Nagarajaand
McElroy (2018) examine a multivariate bullwhip effect in a single-retailer, multi-product setting, where forecasts for
differentproductsarelinked;theyinterpretthisasaformofhorizontalinformationsharingacrossproductsratherthan
across firms. Since the multiple products belong to a single retailer, the information is fully available and there is no
need to share information with external firms. Shi et al. (2014) study a game-theoretic setting with one manufacturer
andtwocompetingsupplierswheresupplierssharecostinformationbetweenthem.Thepapershowsthatbuildingsuch
coalition has positive effects on the suppliers whereas the manufacturer is worse off. Lei et al. (2014) study demand
information sharing in a dual-channel SC with multiple retailers and conclude that horizontal information sharing is
beneficial for retailers and the profit of the manufacturer is not affected. For vertical demand information sharing they
show that the profits of the retailers actually decrease in this setting. All mentioned studies only consider a 2-echelon
SC without investigating the influence of the horizontal information sharing on further upstream echelons.
Overall, this stream shows that both vertical and horizontal information sharing can significantly affect bullwhip
effect, inventory variability, and service performance in multi-echelon, multi-retailer SCs. However, full horizontal
informationsharingbetweenindependentretailersmightbeproblematicduetoprivacyandcompetitionconcerns.Still,
sharingatleastpartialinformationaboutaveragemarketdemandamongtheretailersmighthavesimilareffectsonthe
whole SC. This partial sharing could be enabled using, e.g. cryptographic techniques, such as SMPC or HE, which
providewaysforcomputingaggregatevaluesfromprivateinputswithoutrevealingthoseinputs.InanSMPCprotocol,
multiple actors can jointly compute a function (e.g., a sum or average) in a distributed computing environment while
their own data stay private and only the final result is revealed to each party (Cramer et al., 2015). HE schemes allow
arithmetic operations to be performed directly on data encrypted by public key so that the result equals the function
appliedtotheplaintexts,butcanbeonlydecryptedbytheauthorizeduserposessingtheprivatekey(Acaretal.,2018).
A detailed overview about these techniques can be found in reviews prepared by Zhao, Zhao, Zhao, Chen, Gao, Li

and Tan (2019) or Hellwig and Huchzermeier (2022). Although these techniques enable privacy-preserving sharing
of aggregated indicators—such as average demand or shared key performance indicators—while keeping firm-level
or customer-level data confidential, their implementation can be costly in terms of computation, communication, and
organisational complexity. Therefore, this paper focuses on the potential operational benefits that horizontal sharing
of average demand information between retailers could bring in order to justify the potential use of these techniques
in SC information sharing. However, the exact implementation of these systems is out of scope of this paper.
The literature reviewed above establishes that (i) information enrichment and vertical information sharing can
mitigate the bullwhip effect in serial and multi-echelon SCs; (ii) networked structures with multiple retailers exhibit
complex interactions between demand correlation, forecasting, and information sharing; and (iii) horizontal privacy-
preserving information sharing of partial information could be enabled using cryptographic techniques.
Yet, there is limited evidence on the operational impact of horizontal information sharing of aggregated demand
signals among independent retailers in a multi-echelon SC when privacy concerns prevent full demand disclosure.
Existing multi-retailer and multi-echelon studies either assume full vertical sharing with upstream stages or focus
on information pooling within a firm (e.g., across products) rather than across competing retailers. To close this
gap, this paper analyses a 4-echelon SC with multiple retailers in which retailers can access a privacy-preserving
average customer-demand signal. We quantify how this horizontal information sharing mechanism affects bullwhip
effect, inventory variance, fill rate and costs across echelons under different demand conditions, forecasting methods,
safety-stock approaches, lead time uncertainty, and participation levels. In doing so, we provide a bridge between
the bullwhip and information-sharing literature and emerging work on cryptographic enablers, offering a basis for
subsequent economic evaluations of privacy-preserving data sharing in multi-retailer SCs.

5. Managerial implications
The extensive computational study investigating the impact of horizontal information sharing of average demand
amongmultipleretailersin4-echelonSCunderconsiderationofmultipleinfluencingfactorsshowedthepositiveeffects
as well as potential challenges of this approach that can be summarized in the following insights:
• Horizontal information sharing of average demands is beneficial for all retailers participating in this process.
This is valid for all considered influencing factors. Even in settings where no changes in costs can be directly
recorded,BWEandIVRperformancemeasuresshowthattheinventoryandordervariancescanbedecreasedas
theaveragedemandinformationsmoothensthevariabilityinownforecasts.However,duetoincreasedbullwhip
effect caused by increased covariances of retailers’ orders, the AFR of retailers can be reduced in case of high
demand variabilities combined with SES forecasting or SL safety stock approach. This is due to the worsened
performanceofthewholesalerandotherupstreamechelonscausedbyinventoryandordervariabilitieswhoare
not able to deliver sufficient amounts of goods to the retailers in time to satisfy the demand of the customers.
• Participation in information sharing leads to benefits for the participating retailer whereas abstaining from
participation brings no significant additional costs for the non-participating retailer. However, the effect of
benefitsoneachparticipatingretaileraswellasthewholeSCincreaseswiththenumberofparticipatingretailers.
Therefore, in ideal case all retailers should be involved in establishing and use of such mechanism.
• Whereas retailers benefit from information sharing, all other echelons face higher bullwhip effect due to higher
covariances of retailers’ orders, resulting in increased order and inventory variances and costs. The highest
increases have to be borne by the wholesaler, who is directly connected to the retailers. There are only few
exceptions from this increase, namely the cases of negative demand correlations, SES forecasting methods and
SL safety stock approach, where all SC echelons can achieve savings due to horizontal information sharing.

6. Conclusion
SharingdemandinformationamongthedifferentSCactorshasadampeningimpactonthebullwhipeffect,which
helps the companies to reduce the variation in orders and thus also save costs on inventories and backlogs. Despite
those positive effects, sharing full information is still not an option for many companies. Therefore, in this paper we
investigatedwhethersharingpartialinformationhorizontally,i.e.averagedemandofallcustomerstoeachretailer,can
contribute to a reduction of the bullwhip effect without the need to share the full information. This could be achieved
using cryptographic methods like SMPC or HE.
Using a simulation model of a 4-echelon SC and multiple retailers with OUT policies, we compared a no-
sharing baseline to a mechanism where retailers receive the average of customer demand and blend it with their own
observations.WeevaluatedperformancewithBWE,IVR,AFRaswellasinventoryandbacklogcosts,andwestressed
robustness by varying demand variability and correlation, forecasting methods, safety stock approaches, lead time
uncertainty and the extent of retailers’ participation.
Three core conclusions emerge. (i) Across wide parameter ranges, the horizontal average serves as a stabilizing
signalthatreducesretailers’orderandinventoryvariabilityandgenerallyimprovesservice.Thesegainsarestrongestat
higherdemand,leadtimeandinventoryvariabilityandundernegativedemandcorrelationacrossretailers.(ii)Because
retailersreactmoresimilarlyaftersharinganaveragesignal,theirorderstendtoriseandfallatthesametime.Thiscan
increasethevariabilityseenbythewholesalerand,byextension,themanufacturerandsupplier—especiallyunderhigh
demandvariabilityorstrongpositivecorrelation.Largersafetystocksupstreamhelp,butdonotremovethiseffect.(iii)
Partial participation (i.e. only some retailers sharing information), giving less weight to the shared average, or using
smoother forecasting can keep retailer benefits without pushing too much volatility upstream.
Our experiments assume unlimited production and transport capacities, and stylized demand processes. Future
work could incorporate capacity constraints and congestion, non-stationary and seasonal demand, richer contractual
forms(e.g.,optionorservice-levelagreements),andstrategicbehavior.Moreover,interactionsbetweentheinfluencing
factorscouldbeinvestigatedandacomparisonbetweentheeffectsofhorizontalandverticalinformationsharingcould
show which approach leads to higher benefits. Finally, field tests with real data and alternative aggregation schemes
(e.g.,mediansorrobustaverages)couldshowhowtokeepretailergainswhilereducingsynchronizedswingsupstream.
