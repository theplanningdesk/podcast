 A B S T R A C T
Forecast revisions contain information about emerging demand changes that is typically discarded in inventory-
control policies. Our two main contributions are (i) a POMDP state augmentation that pairs the history 
of forecast revisions with Bayesian change-point and forecast-uncertainty signals, and (ii) FR-PPO (Forecast 
Revision Proximal Policy Optimization), a two-level recurrent architecture that encodes this revision history 
through a GRU inside a RecurrentPPO controller centred on an uncertainty-adjusted base-stock reference. We 
evaluated FR-PPO against two classical and six reinforcement learning benchmarks in five synthetic demand 
scenarios and seven regime-active M5 SKUs. FR-PPO is the best PPO-family policy in all five synthetic scenarios 
and achieves the lowest overall cost on three of them, reducing cost by 72.2% relative to adaptive BaseStock 
under seasonal demand while reducing adaptation lag by 63%. The method is not uniformly dominant: off-
policy SAC baselines retain an advantage under sparse, intermittent demand, delimiting where the revision 
signal is most valuable. On real M5 data, FR-PPO reduces cost relative to adaptive BaseStock on all seven 
SKUs (4.2%–23.0%). These findings identify forecast-revision histories as useful, low-cost decision information 
for replenishment under demand non-stationarity.
1. Introduction
1.1. Motivation
Inventory management under non-stationary demand remains a 
central problem in supply chain management. Classical inventory the-
ory base-stock policies, (𝑠, 𝑆) rules and the newsvendor formula delivers 
near-optimal solutions when demand is stationary and Normal [1,
2]. Real demand, however, is neither. It exhibits abrupt level shifts 
driven by promotions, seasonality, product introductions, and macroe-
conomic shocks; gradual trend reversals; and intermittent bursts. The 
practitioner’s default response is to widen safety stocks and accept 
chronic over- or under-stocking as a cost of robustness. This volatil-
ity has also motivated a growing organisational literature on supply 
chain resilience and ambidexterity, that is the firm-level capability to 
balance efficiency with adaptability under environmental uncertainty 
[3,4]. That literature addresses strategic and organisational-capability 
questions, while this paper instead addresses the operational and al-
gorithmic question of how a single replenishment policy should react, 
period by period, to demand non-stationarity once it occurs.

Recent advances in deep reinforcement learning (RL) offer a com-
plementary approach: training a policy directly from simulated in-
ventory dynamics so that the neural network learns the mapping 
from observed state to order quantity without closed-form assumptions 
[5–7]. These methods have demonstrated significant cost reductions 
over classical policies on stationary and mildly non-stationary demand. 
However, they share a critical limitation: they treat the forecasting 
system as a black box, consuming its point forecast ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 but discarding 
the forecast revision, i.e., the update 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 = ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 − ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡−1 that the 
forecaster makes when new demand arrives.
Forecast revisions are not merely noise. Under exponential smooth-

1.2. Related work
1.2.1. Reinforcement learning for inventory control
The application of deep reinforcement learning to inventory control 
has matured rapidly since 2020. Gijsbrechts et al. [5] establish the first 
systematic benchmark evidence, demonstrating that DRL is effective 
for stationary inventory problems including lost-sales, dual-sourcing, 
and multi-echelon structures, while concluding that performance ad-
vantages over classical heuristics are most pronounced when no ana-
lytically tractable policy structure is available. Vanvuchelen et al. [7] 
demonstrate that Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) can learn near-
optimal joint replenishment policies in multi-item settings, establishing 
PPO as a strong benchmark algorithm for inventory RL. The conceptual 
roadmap of Boute et al. [6] synthesises the design choices such as 
state representation, action parameterisation, reward specification, and 
simulation design that determine whether DRL succeeds in inventory 
control.
The systematic review of Bergsma et al. [8], covering 122 machine 
learning papers on inventory control, identifies non-stationary demand 
with structural breaks as the most underexplored area in the DRL 
inventory literature. The review notes that few papers explicitly model 
demand regime shifts or measure the policy’s adaptation speed follow-
ing a structural change. This gap motivates the experimental design 
of the present paper, which introduces adaptation lag as a primary 
performance metric alongside total cost and service level.
1.2.2. Forecast signals and change-point detection
A key architectural decision in RL-based replenishment is the role of 
demand forecasts in the policy’s information structure. Dehaybe et al. 
[9] introduce 𝐻-step rolling forecast windows as explicit state com-
ponents for a PPO policy trained on non-stationary lot-sizing problems, 
enabling the policy to condition replenishment decisions on anticipated 
future demand. However, Dehaybe et al. treat the forecast as a point-
in-time prediction at each period, without modelling the sequence of 
revisions to that prediction. The dynamics through which a forecast 
changes from one period to the next are therefore discarded.
This paper proposes a revision as a signal approach. Rather than 
treating the forecast as a fixed snapshot, we model the full sequence 
of forecast updates and extract revision embeddings as explicit policy 
inputs. The revision 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 = ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 − ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡−1 reflects the forecaster’s 
posterior update upon observing demand 𝐷𝑡. Under ETS, this revision 
is analytically proportional to the current forecast error 𝜀𝑡, with mag-
nitude increasing in the forecast horizon ℎ. A large positive revision 
indicates that observed demand exceeded the prior forecast as evidence 
of upward demand pressure or regime transition. We are not aware of 
a prior RL paper for inventory replenishment that explicitly constructs 
and exploits the temporal revision history as a separate encoded signal.
Kou et al. [10] augment the state with a Bayesian Online Change-
Point Detection (BOCPD) signal 𝑍𝑡 ∈ [0 , 1], a continuous posterior 
probability that a regime change has recently occurred, but discard 
the revision itself. We combine these complementary signals: a GRU 
encodes the rolling revision history into a compact embedding, while 𝑍𝑡
modulates exploration via adaptive entropy scheduling. The distinction 
from Dehaybe et al. [9] is that we condition on {𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡} in addition to 
{ ̂𝑑𝑡}; the distinction from Kou et al. [10] is that we build a structured 
revision embedding rather than using 𝑍𝑡 as the sole regime signal.
The bullwhip literature [11] has long studied forecast revision 
propagation as a source of order amplification. Graves [12] derives 
the optimal policy under demand signal processing. Our contribution 
is to operationalise the revision signal as an RL state representation, 
turning a source of amplification into a tool for early regime detection 
(see Table 1). 
1.3. Research gap, research questions, and contributions
Despite the centrality of forecast revisions to supply chain practice, 
the RL for inventory literature has not yet systematically conditioned 
policies on the history of forecast revisions as a structured signal. 
The closest prior work is Dehaybe et al. [9], who pass the difference 
between two consecutive forecasts as a scalar to a PPO policy, and Kou 
et al. [10], who augment the state with a change-point detection signal 
𝑍𝑡 but discard the revision itself. Neither paper encodes the temporal 
pattern of revisions, the shape of the update sequence over the past 𝑘
periods, which is the signal that distinguishes a transient spike from 
a sustained regime change. Without this structured history, the policy 
can only react to shifts already reflected in the forecast level; it cannot 
anticipate them.
This gap motivates two research questions. RQ1: does conditioning 
a replenishment policy on the history of forecast revisions, rather 
than only the current forecast level or a contemporaneous change-
point signal, reduce adaptation lag and cost under regime-switching 
demand? RQ2: under what demand and cost conditions does a revision-
conditioned policy outperform strong off-policy alternatives, and where 
does this advantage fail to hold? We show that encoding the revision 
pattern reduces adaptation lag by 63% on seasonal demand and yields 
statistically significant cost reductions against all 8 baselines on that 
scenario, supported by a value-of-information argument (Proposition 
2); we also show that the advantage does not hold uniformly, delimiting 
the answer to RQ2.
This paper introduces Forecast-Revision Proximal Policy Optimiza-
tion (FR-PPO), a recurrent RL policy for inventory replenishment under 
non-stationary demand. We formulate the inventory problem as a 
partially observable Markov decision process with state

1. State representation (Section 2). We propose a POMDP state

2. Model
2.1. System description
We consider a single-echelon, periodic-review inventory system 
operating over a finite planning horizon of 𝑇 periods. In each period 
𝑡 ∈ {1, … , 𝑇 }, the decision-maker observes the current system state and 
places a non-negative replenishment order 𝑎𝑡 ≥ 0. Replenishment orders 
placed at period 𝑡 arrive at the beginning of period 𝑡+𝐿, where 𝐿 ≥ 1 is 
a fixed, deterministic lead time. The decision-maker does not observe 
the latent demand regime directly; it must be inferred from observable 
signals.
2.2. Demand model
Demand is generated by a regime-switching process with 𝐾 latent 
regimes. Let 𝜉𝑡 ∈ {1 , … , 𝐾} denote the active regime at period 𝑡, 
governed by a time-homogeneous Markov chain with transition matrix 
𝛱 ∈ [0 , 1]𝐾×𝐾 . The agent never observes 𝜉𝑡; it is a latent variable 
throughout.
Within regime 𝑘, period-𝑡 demand is drawn from: 
𝐷𝑡 ∣ 𝜉𝑡 = 𝑘 ∼ 𝐹𝑘(𝜇𝑘, 𝜎𝑘), (1)
where 𝐹𝑘 is a non-negative distribution with regime-specific mean 𝜇𝑘 >
0 and standard deviation 𝜎𝑘 > 0. In baseline experiments, 𝐹𝑘 is taken as 
a Normal distribution truncated at zero, consistent with Dehaybe et al. 
[9]. Extensions to Negative Binomial distributions are evaluated in the 
intermittent demand scenario (Section 4).
Regime transitions generate the structural demand changes the 
framework is designed to detect. Section 4 evaluates five baseline 
demand scenarios: level shift (two-regime), trend change (ramp-and-
plateau), seasonal (periodic regime), promotion shock (spike-and-
return), and intermittent demand (Negative Binomial, alternating
phases).

3.2. Revision signal: Theoretical analysis
Proposition 1 (ETS Revision Decomposition). Under ETS(A,A,N) with 
smoothing parameters 𝛼 ∈ (0 , 1) and 𝛽𝑠 ∈ (0 , 𝛼), the ℎ-step-ahead forecast 
revision at time 𝑡 satisfies: 
𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 ∶= ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 − ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡−1 = 𝛼𝜀𝑡 (1 + ℎ𝛽𝑠 ), ℎ ≥ 1. (24)
Proof. Let 𝓁𝑡, 𝑏𝑡 denote the ETS level and trend at time 𝑡. The standard 
ETS(A,A,N) update gives:
𝓁𝑡 = 𝓁𝑡−1 + 𝑏𝑡−1 + 𝛼𝜀𝑡,
𝑏𝑡 = 𝑏𝑡−1 + 𝛼𝛽𝑠𝜀𝑡.
The ℎ-step-ahead forecast is ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 = 𝓁𝑡 + ℎ ⋅ 𝑏𝑡. The previous forecast 
for the same calendar period was issued at horizon ℎ + 1, so ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡−1 =
𝓁𝑡−1 + (ℎ + 1)𝑏𝑡−1. Therefore:
̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 − ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡−1 = (𝓁𝑡 − 𝓁𝑡−1 − 𝑏𝑡−1) + ℎ(𝑏𝑡 − 𝑏𝑡−1)
= 𝛼𝜀𝑡 + ℎ𝛼𝛽𝑠𝜀𝑡 = 𝛼𝜀𝑡(1 + ℎ𝛽𝑠). □
Corollary 1.1. The entire revision vector 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡 = ( 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡+1|𝑡, … , 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡+𝐻|𝑡)
is determined by the single scalar 𝛼𝜀𝑡, scaled linearly across horizons. 
Consequently, observing 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡 is equivalent to observing 𝜀𝑡 (given 𝛼, 𝛽𝑠), 
justifying inclusion of 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡 in 𝑠𝑡 instead of a raw demand history buffer.

decision rule. If the posterior distribution of the latent regime 
changes enough to alter the cost-minimising order quantity, the 
revision-augmented state supports a strictly lower expected cost 
in regime-transition periods. □
Remark 1 (Scope of Proposition 2). The result holds for the class of 
exact Markov policies over the stated state spaces. In the deployed 
architecture, the policy is approximated by a neural network trained 
via PPO, and the GRU encodes 𝑡 into a finite-dimensional embed-
ding 𝑒𝑡. The proposition motivates including 𝑡 in the state—it is not 
a guarantee that the trained approximate policy achieves the exact 
optimum, nor that it recovers the full informational advantage under 
every demand process. The strict-inequality condition in Proposition 2 
holds only when the revision history changes the optimal action with 
positive probability; whether a PPO-trained approximation realises this 
advantage in practice depends jointly on the demand process and 
the learning architecture, not on the proposition alone. Empirically 
(Section 4), the advantage is realised with statistical significance in 
four of five synthetic scenarios, but not under intermittent, sparse-
reward demand, where an off-policy SAC baseline remains competitive 
or superior—consistent with a conditional, rather than universal, infor-
mational advantage. The empirical confirmation that encoding revision 
history reduces cost and adaptation lag in the scenarios where the 
condition holds provides the primary evidence that the approximation 
preserves the informational advantage in practice.

3.4. Adaptive entropy scheduling
Rather than use a fixed PPO entropy coefficient, FR-PPO modulates 
exploration intensity via an online exponential moving average (EMA) 
of the BOCPD signal 𝑍𝑡. After each rollout of 𝑛steps transitions, the EMA 
is updated as: 
̃𝑍𝜏+1 = (1 − 𝛼𝑍 ) ̃𝑍𝜏 + 𝛼𝑍 ⋅ 𝑍𝜏 , (27)
where 𝜏 indexes rollouts, 𝑍𝜏 = 𝑛−1 steps ∑ 𝑡 𝑍𝑡 is the mean BOCPD prob-
ability over the rollout, and 𝛼𝑍 = 0 .1 is the EMA decay. The entropy 
coefficient is then set to: 
𝛼ent,𝜏+1 = 𝛼min ent + (𝛼max ent − 𝛼min ent ) ⋅ ̃𝑍𝜏+1, (28)
with 𝛼min ent = 0 .005 (stable regime; exploit) and 𝛼max ent = 0 .05 (confirmed 
regime change; explore). When 𝑍𝑡 ≈ 0 throughout the rollout the 
policy converges towards exploitation; when 𝑍𝑡 → 1 (BOCPD detects 
a recent change) the entropy ceiling is reached, concentrating stochas-
tic exploration at regime-transition windows where discovering the 
new optimum is most valuable. The EMA smoothing prevents noisy 
per-rollout jumps from destabilising training.

Rather than amplifying the seasonal peaks into large base-stock swings,
FR-PPO keeps inventory closer to zero while preserving service levels, 
which lowers both holding and backorder costs.
4.3. Ablation study
Table 7 isolates each architectural component by progressively 
augmenting the observation and architecture on the level-shift scenario 
(𝐿 = 4, ℎ = 1, 𝑏 = 9, 20 seeds).
The ablation reveals four noteworthy patterns:
1. Adding CPD and uncertainty to a non-recurrent MLP does 
not improve performance (ForecastUncCPD-PPO: 76.4 vs.
ForecastOnly-PPO: 74.4, a regression). Without recurrence, the 
policy cannot integrate 𝑍𝑡 over time and the noisy uncertainty 
signal introduces variance without providing predictive benefit.
2. Recurrence is the largest single contribution (RNN-PPO: 68.1 
vs. ForecastOnly-PPO: 74.4, −8.5%). The LSTM hidden state 
effectively tracks regime context, achieving most of the available 
gain even without revision signals.
3. Adding the flat revision vector does not improve over re-
currence alone (FlatRevision-PPO: 68.3 vs. RNN-PPO: 68.1, 
−0.2%). Raw revision vectors are near-zero-mean in steady state; 
without encoding their history, the policy cannot extract direc-
tional information from them on the level-shift scenario.
4. The GRU revision encoder and adaptive entropy scheduling 
jointly reduce cost by 6.6% (FR-PPO: 63.8 vs. FlatRevision-
PPO: 68.3). The GRU encoder distils the rolling revision history 
into a compact embedding that enables the policy to track 
directional demand signals; the adaptive entropy mechanism 
concentrates exploration at detected regime-transition windows 
(𝑍𝑡 → 1) and exploits stable periods (𝑍𝑡 ≈ 0 ). The combined 
effect raises fill rate from 87.8% to 90.2% and eliminates the 
instability visible in the flat-revision baseline.

4.5.1. Data and SKU selection
We use the M5 Accuracy Competition dataset [16], comprising 
30,490 daily SKU-level sales across 10 Walmart stores. We aggre-
gate daily data to weekly totals consistent with the periodic-review 
assumption (𝑇 = 200 weeks per episode).
From the full catalogue we retain SKUs satisfying: weekly mean 
demand 𝜇 ∈ [3, 60], at least 200 active weeks, zero-rate below 10%, and 
per-regime coefficient of variation CV𝑘 ∈ [0 .12, 0.55] for both BOCPD-
estimated regimes. We apply two selection criteria derived from the 
sensitivity analysis:
• Rapid regime alternation: minimum persistence probability 
𝑃 min stay ∈ [0 .82, 0.94] with regime separation sep = |𝜇1 − 𝜇0|∕
max(𝜇0, 𝜇1) ≥ 0.30.
• Strong annual periodicity: lag-52 autocorrelation 𝑟52 ≥ 0.35.
The scan identifies 97 SKUs meeting the alternation criterion and 202 
meeting the periodicity criterion; 61 SKUs satisfy both. We select seven 
SKUs using the following prespecified criteria:
1. Overlap SKU (both alternation and periodicity criteria): rank 
by 𝑟52 descending; take the top SKU. This represents the high-
est non-stationarity intensity in the M5 catalogue under both 
criteria.
2. Pure-alternation SKUs (alternation criterion only, 𝑟52 < 0.35): 
filter to sep > 0.44 and 𝑛chg ≥ 6; rank by sep descending; take the 
top 6. These are the SKUs with the sharpest and most frequent 
regime transitions in the alternation candidate set.

4.7.2. When to prefer FR-PPO over an off-policy SAC baseline
The results in this section do not support a uniform ranking between 
revision-conditioned PPO and off-policy SAC; Tables 5 and 11 show FR-
PPO winning outright on three of five synthetic scenarios (level shift, 
trend change, seasonal) while CPD-SAC and ForecastOnly-SAC remain 
competitive or superior on promotion shock and significantly better on 
intermittent demand.
FR-PPO is preferable when the regime signal is inferable from a 
level or trend shift that the ETS forecaster tracks cleanly, and when 
lead time gives the revision signal room to act. The lead-time sensitivity 
sweep (Section 4.4) shows the gap over CPD-SAC widening monoton-
ically with 𝐿 from parity at 𝐿 ≤ 4 to a 13.8% advantage at 𝐿 = 8 , 
because a longer pipeline gives an early revision-based signal more 
periods in which to change the realised order before the corresponding 
demand arrives. The seasonal and level-shift results, where FR-PPO’s 
advantage is largest and statistically significant against all eight base-
lines (Table 11), share this structure: the underlying regime process 
produces a clean, low-noise revision trajectory that the GRU encoder 
can exploit.
SAC’s off-policy, entropy-regularised structure is preferable under 
sparse, high-variance reward landscapes, most clearly intermittent de-
mand. With mean demand near zero for long stretches punctuated by 
backlog spikes, the reward signal is sparse and heavy-tailed; SAC’s 
replay buffer reuses each transition many times and its entropy bonus 
maintains exploration through long quiet periods, whereas FR-PPO’s 
on-policy revision encoder sees a near-zero-mean revision signal for 
most of the episode and has comparatively little gradient signal to 
learn from. This is consistent with FR-PPO remaining the best PPO-
family policy on intermittent demand while still losing significantly to 
both SAC variants (Table 11), and with Proposition 2’s scope restric-
tion (Section 3.2): the informational advantage of revision histories is 
conditional on the demand process producing revisions that are actually 
decision-relevant, a condition that holds less cleanly under intermittent 
demand than under the other four scenarios.
For a practitioner choosing between the two architectures without 
running both, the practical rule implied by these results is to condition 
the choice on the dominant type of non-stationarity in the target SKU: 
revision-conditioned PPO for demand governed by discrete regime 
shifts, trends, or seasonal cycles with moderate-to-long lead times, 
and an off-policy SAC baseline for sparse, intermittent, zero-inflated 
demand. If the SKU population is heterogeneous and the type of non-
stationarity is not known in advance, FlatRevision-PPO (Section 4.3) is 
the more conservative single default: across the five scenarios it ranks 
no worse than 5th of nine policies and its gap to the best policy ranges 
from 4.6% (promotion shock) to 20.0% (seasonal), whereas both FR-
PPO and the SAC baselines have at least one scenario where they are 
significantly outperformed by a wider margin (Table 11). FlatRevision-
PPO trades a larger typical gap to the best policy for the absence of a 
scenario in which it is clearly the worst choice.
5. Conclusion
This paper studied single-item periodic-review replenishment un-
der non-stationary, regime-switching demand, and asked whether the
history of forecast revisions constitutes actionable information for the 
ordering decision. We answered this question both analytically and 
empirically. Analytically, we showed that under ETS the ℎ-step revision 
is a scaled forecast innovation (Proposition 1) and that augmenting 
the policy’s information set with the revision history cannot increase, 
and reduces whenever the revisions carry decision-relevant information 
about the latent regime, the optimal expected cost under regime-
switching demand (Proposition 2). This condition held with statistical 
significance in four of the five synthetic scenarios we studied and was 
most valuable at longer lead times; it did not hold under sparse, inter-
mittent demand, where an off-policy SAC baseline remained preferable, 
delimiting the scope of the theoretical result rather than contradicting 
it. Empirically, we operationalised this signal in FR-PPO, a recurrent 
policy that encodes a rolling revision window through a GRU, condi-
tions a RecurrentPPO controller on the resulting embedding together 
with a Bayesian change-point probability, and centres its action on an 
uncertainty-adjusted base-stock reference.
Across five synthetic regime-switching scenarios and seven regime-
active SKUs from the M5 dataset, conditioning on forecast revisions 
yielded consistent gains. FR-PPO was the best PPO-family policy in all 
five synthetic scenarios and attained the lowest cost overall on three of 
them, including a 72.2% reduction over an adaptive base-stock policy 
on seasonal demand, where it also cut adaptation lag from 26.3 to 
9.8 periods. These gains were statistically significant against all eight 
benchmarks on the seasonal scenario under a Bonferroni-corrected 
Wilcoxon test, and the policy trained without a single divergence 
using 40% fewer environment interactions than the PPO baselines. The 
advantage was not uniform: an off-policy SAC benchmark remained 
competitive under sparse, intermittent demand, delimiting where the 
revision signal is most valuable. On held-out M5 data the policy re-
duced cost relative to the adaptive base-stock benchmark for all seven 
SKUs (4.2%–23.0%) and degraded gracefully on a rapidly alternating 
SKU where the SAC benchmark diverged, indicating that the synthetic 
findings transfer to empirical demand.
From a managerial standpoint, the results reframe the cost of de-
mand non-stationarity: the dominant penalty is less the regime shift 
itself than the lag between the shift and the replenishment system’s 
response, and this lag can be compressed by reading information that 
firms already produce. Because the forecast revision 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 is a by-
product of any re-forecast, the approach requires no additional data col-
lection and integrates with existing ETS-based planning. More broadly, 
the state augmentation (𝑍𝑡, 𝛥 ̂𝑑𝑡, 𝑈𝑡) is modular: the change-point de-
tector, the forecaster, and the revision encoder can each be replaced 
independently, suggesting a deployable pattern in which a standard 
demand-planning module streams revision signals to a lightweight 
learned controller. The same revision dynamics long studied as a driver 
of the bullwhip effect can thus be repurposed downstream to shorten 
adaptation lag rather than to amplify it.
Limitations and future work
Several limitations bound these conclusions and motivate further 
work.
• Scope. The study addresses a single-echelon, single-item system 
with stationary cost parameters and deterministic lead times. 
Extending the framework to multi-echelon networks, joint re-
plenishment, stochastic lead times, and continuous-review (𝑟, 𝑄)
control are natural next steps, the first of which would require 
propagating revision signals across echelons.

• Demand model. The demand model is regime-switching Gaus-
sian with a Normal–Inverse–Gamma change-point prior. Richer 
priors that jointly capture trend, seasonality, and count-valued de-
mand would likely close the remaining gap to off-policy methods 
on intermittent items.
• External validity. The M5 validation is selected for regime activ-
ity rather than catalogue-representative (Section 4.5): the seven 
SKUs sit at the 66th–87th percentile of regime separation among 
the eligible population, and on the more stable majority of a real 
SKU catalogue the method is expected to offer little advantage 
over adaptive BaseStock, consistent with the conditional nature 
of Proposition 2.
• Forecaster dependence. The revision encoder is derived for 
ETS output, for which the innovation decomposition is exact. 
Generalising the revision signal to ARIMA, machine-learning, and 
ensemble forecasters — defined simply as the difference between 
successive forecasts — would broaden applicability, with the ETS 
decomposition serving as a theoretical idealisation rather than a 
requirement.
• Observability. Demand is fully observed in every period of this 
study. Real point-of-sale data is frequently censored during stock-
outs, when realised sales understate true demand; extending the 
revision signal and its ETS decomposition to censored or inter-
mittently observed demand is a natural next step for retail and 
e-commerce deployments, where stockout censoring is common.
A replication package with code and configuration files accompa-
nies the paper to support independent verification and these extensions.
