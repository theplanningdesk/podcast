 A B S T R A C T
Traditional machine learning (ML) techniques are widely used to support operational supply chain decisions, 
but their ‘‘black box’’ nature limits transparency and actionability. This study addresses this challenge by 
exploring the effectiveness of causal discovery methods and large language models (LLMs) in supply chain 
management with a focus on the automotive industry. Six causal discovery algorithms- Peter–Clark (PC), 
Fast Causal Inference (FCI), Greedy Equivalence Search (GES), DirectLiNGAM, NOTEARS, and Regression 
with Subsequent Independence Test (RESIT) are benchmarked against an expert-validated ground-truth graph 
constructed from 18 domain experts with a combined 300+ years of experience, using 12 months of internal 
supplier Key Performance Indicator (KPI) data. RESIT achieves the closest alignment with expert knowledge, 
with a False Discovery Rate of 0.1429, Structural Hamming Distance of 1, precision and recall of 0.8571 and an 
F1-score of 0.85, outperforming all other algorithms on every metric. Robustness of the estimated causal effect 
of Backlog on Escalation is confirmed through three refutation tests-random common cause, random subset 
and placebo treatment returning high p-values of 0.96, 0.76, and 0.98 respectively, indicating insensitivity to 
spurious confounders and random perturbations. Counterfactual simulation shows that reducing Backlog by 
50%–70% consistently lowers escalation levels across suppliers. LLMs contribute by extracting causal factors 
from unstructured external event data, accelerating causal graph construction, and reducing dependence on 
manual expert input. LLM-derived causal graphs are further compared with expert-validated models to ensure 
alignment and reliability, and are operationalized through a Weighted Sum Model risk score linking external 
drivers to internal KPIs for actionable supplier escalation decisions.
1. Introduction
In today’s globalized world, supply chains are becoming increas-
ingly complex as they encompass multiple geographies, stakeholders, 
and technologies involved [1]. Events such as terrorist attacks, wars, 
earthquakes, economic crises, currency devaluations, pandemics, nat-
ural disasters, strikes, and cyberattacks have been shown to disrupt 
supply chains [2]. For example, the COVID-19 pandemic caused se-
vere disruption of global supply-chain operations, with consequences 
spreading across almost every sector of the economy. As countries 
closed their borders and ports experienced long backlogs of orders, 
supply shipments were delayed. This caused demand for products to 
fluctuate drastically. This dynamic resulted in many problems, such as 
consumer hoarding [3,4].
Digitalization and the rise of data analytics have brought both new 
opportunities and challenges for operational supply chains in managing 
the daily flow of goods, services, and information. Modern supply

Traditional machine learning (ML) approaches, while providing

[5–7]. To address these shortcomings, researchers and practitioners 
have begun to take an interest in applying causal machine learning 
(CML). CML does not use the correlation between variables as a basis 
for inferring causal relationships [8,9]. Understanding the true causal 
relationships is critical to planning actions for risk management in the 
supply chain.
Causal discovery and inference are two fundamental elements in 
CML to extract and analyze causal relationships from observed prob-
lems [8]. Causal discovery refers to the process of identifying causal 
relationships between variables from observational data. Causal infer-
ence refers to the process of estimating the effect of an intervention

These models represent causal relationships in data [9]. The main 
output of CML is to determine the impact of changing one variable 
on the change in the outcome variable. This approach often involves 
counterfactual analysis, asking questions such as, ‘‘What if the action 
taken was different from what was done?’’ [10]. For example, how 
would the supplier escalation rate change if the backlog were reduced 
by X%? Decision-makers seek to understand the causes and reasons 
derived from the data to address deficiencies and weaknesses in the 
current system and guide how to make needed improvements.
Causal modeling requires intensive manual effort from domain ex-
perts who understand the cause-and-effect relationships among vari-
ables. These relationships are represented in a causal graph, a directed 
acyclic graph (DAG) [11]. Causal discovery algorithms automatically 
uncover causal relationships from observational data. These algorithms 
can only be applied with structured input data, such as cross-sectional 
or time-series data [12]. In the context of supply chains, such structured 
data is often not available, especially data on external factors such as 
conflicts, strikes, and other disruptions [13]. This limitation is a major 
challenge for modeling cause and effect to analyze the supply chain as 
a whole, which involves many external factors.
Recent work in Supply Chain Analytics highlights (i) how unstruc-
tured online text can be mined to improve supply chain risk visibility 
and decision support and (ii) how generative AI and large language 
models are emerging as a decision layer for supply chain transformation 
[14,15].
Large Language Models (LLMs) can be an effective tool to aid causal 
analysis by extracting cause-and-effect relationships from unstructured 
data, such as news reports, economic forecasts, or supplier communi-
cation documents. This allows companies to comprehensively analyze 
supply chains so that they can act appropriately in managing risks. 
The study by Kıcıman et al. [16] explored the relationship between 
causal inference and LLMs. They found that recent developments in 
LLMs have significantly improved the ability of AI to engage in complex 
tasks. One of the main aspects discussed in their study is the ability 
of LLMs to explain things plausibly. LLMs can even adjust their con-
clusions even when new information is introduced [16]. In addition, 
LLMs can construct counterfactual scenarios, which indicate an implicit 
understanding of causal relationships. This study shows that LLMs can 
be a tool for domain experts to reduce the initial effort to construct 
causal graphs.
Beyond this individual capability of LLMs, recent work in supply 
chain risk management reinforces why pairing them with causal ma-
chine learning matters. Systematic reviews of AI in SCRM show that 
while predictive accuracy has improved, a lack of model transparency 
and of ways to incorporate unstructured external event data continues 
to limit managerial uptake [14,17]. In parallel, recent evidence that 
causal machine learning can move SCM from correlation-based pre-
diction to intervention-planning [18] underscores the case for causal 
approaches, but leaves open how such approaches should be cou-
pled with LLMs to incorporate the external, unstructured drivers that 
dominate real supplier risk. This study addresses that coupling directly.
The overarching objective of this study is therefore to enhance 
decision-making in supply chain management by integrating causal ma-
chine learning (CML) and large language models (LLMs) for discovering 
and analyzing causal relationships, ultimately leading to improved 
risk management and operational efficiency. Specifically, our approach 
addresses three key challenges:
• Validated causal discovery, not abstract benchmarking: Prior 
CML work in SCM is overwhelmingly single-algorithm and lacks 
validation against expert-derived ground truth. We benchmark 
six causal discovery algorithms from four methodological families 
against an 18-expert ground-truth graph on real automotive sup-
plier KPIs, with three formal refutation tests, establishing which 
family of algorithms actually aligns with domain reality.
• Operationalized LLM-based external-factor extraction: LLMs 
have been shown to extract causal relations from text in gen-
eral settings but have not been connected to supplier KPIs in 
an auditable SCM pipeline. We combine LLM-generated causal 
structure with a Node-RED workflow that makes every edge 
inspectable, and align the extracted external factors with internal 
KPIs so that the LLM contribution is verifiable rather than opaque.
• Decision-relevant risk score, not just a causal graph: Causal 
graphs alone do not support managerial action. We translate 
the validated structure into a Weighted Sum Model risk score 
that quantifies how external disruptions propagate into internal 
KPIs, supporting concrete supplier escalation and de-escalation 
decisions.
To address the overarching objective and key challenges, this study 
investigates the following research questions:
1. How much more effectively do different causal discovery algo-
rithms identify causal relationships in operational automotive 
supply chains compared to expert-derived causal graphs?
2. To what extent can LLMs enhance and accelerate causal discov-
ery in supply chains, and how closely do the causal graphs they 
generate align with ground truth data from experts?
3. How can causal relationships identified through LLMs and expert 
interviews improve supplier risk assessment and performance 
simulations for better decision-making in risk escalation and 
mitigation?
Thus, this study contributes to the body of knowledge on CML in 
SCM as follows:
1. To assess the effectiveness of the performance of various causal 
discovery algorithms in identifying causal relationships in op-
erational supply chains by comparing them with causal graphs 
obtained from experts. This study also selects the criteria and 
metrics to evaluate their effectiveness. 
2. To evaluate the potential of LLM in accelerating causal discovery 
in supply chains and measure how closely the causal graph 
obtained from LLM aligns with the ground truth obtained from 
experts. 
3. To apply causal-effect relationships identified from expert inter-
views and LLM to improve supplier risk assessments and conduct 
performance simulations. This will support more accurate and 
actionable decision-making for risk escalation and mitigation in 
supply chains. 
The novelty of this study lies in combining a systematic evaluation 
of multiple causal discovery algorithms with validation against expert-
derived ground truth graphs and extending this with the integration 
of large language models. The use of LLMs to extract and structure 
causal factors from external event data complements the internal KPI 
analysis, creating a comprehensive view of supply chain risks. In line 
with a design science perspective, the study positions this integra-
tion of LLMs with a causal discovery algorithm particularly suited for 
nonlinear, high-dimensional KPI data (RESIT, as highlighted in prior 
literature) as the proposed approach, while the remaining algorithms 
and expert-derived models serve as benchmarks. In addition, embed-
ding robustness checks through refutation tests and linking the causal 
structures directly to decision-relevant KPIs and supplier escalation 
outcomes ensures both methodological rigor and practical applicability. 
This integrated formulation distinguishes the work from more generic 
causal modeling contributions and provides a domain-specific pipeline 
for resilient supply chain decision-making.

2. Literature review
2.1. Related work
Modern supply chains have evolved into global and complex net-
works. These networks are influenced by factors such as globalization, 
stakeholder relationships, technological advancements, and sustainabil-
ity challenges [19]. Global supply chains now span multiple regions 
and markets worldwide, creating logistical, regulatory, and cultural 
challenges. Technology, especially IoT and big data, adds to the com-
plexity. Cybersecurity and data privacy are critical concerns [20,21]. 
In addition, environmental issues [22] and natural disasters such as 
pandemics highlight the vulnerabilities of supply chains, requiring ro-
bust and adaptive management strategies [23,24]. The data generated 
in supply chains has the potential to provide valuable insights, but 
challenges remain in their effective integration, analysis, and use to 
improve supply chain resilience and agility.
Many organizations face challenges in obtaining actionable insights 
from an extensive collection of data because traditional predictive 
analytics produces predictions but not prescriptive guidance [25]. AI 
and ML technologies are valuable for data processing, as well as pre-
dictive and logistics optimization capabilities. They play an essential 
role in improving supply chain performance by enhancing demand 
forecast accuracy, system resilience, and performance management. 
These technologies enable the prediction from hidden patterns in data 
using artificial neural networks to improve supply chain operations per-
formance [26]. They can also optimize production and manufacturing 
operations and supply chain networks, as well as address integration 
and collaboration issues [27]. However, translating AI and ML out-
puts into proactive decision-making is still challenging [28]. Advanced 
decision support systems are needed to address this gap by not only pre-
dicting outcomes but also recommending actions. This enables supply 
chain managers to respond to uncertainty effectively [19].
ML plays an important role in the shift from traditional to proactive 
analytics in the supply chain [29,30], enabling strategies that can 
improve the accuracy of demand forecasting, inventory management, 
and logistics optimization [31]. ML models uncover data patterns, help 
anticipate disruptions, and facilitate real-time responses [32]. ML is 
increasingly essential in helping manage complex supply chains. This is 
shown by the ML market being valued at USD 1.5 billion by 2023 and a 
projected compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 29% by 2032 [28]. 
In supply chain risk management, ML can improve risk prediction and 
mitigation [33,34], and also drive supply chain agility and resilience 
[35,36].
While ML has its advantages in general, traditional ML models often 
act as a ‘‘black box’’. This lack of transparency leads to a lack of 
practically actionable guidance. Many models are predictive in this way 
but not prescriptive [37,38]. As a result, interpretable machine learning 
has attracted significant attention, focusing on prescriptive analytics 
that provide clearer actions and increased model transparency [39,40]. 
Models that provide interpretable results are especially important in 
complex supply chains, where clear, actionable insights are critical for 
decision-making. Table 1 summarizes the limitations of traditional ML 
compared to the needs of modern supply chains.
CML is a step beyond traditional ML. It focuses on cause-and-effect 
relationships rather than correlations [41,42]. This feature is important

2.2. Research gaps
Causal Machine Learning (CML) has been applied across medicine 
[47], molecular genetics [48], sustainability [49], manufacturing [50], 
and the natural sciences [51]. In Supply Chain Management (SCM), 
prior work spans counterfactual disruption analysis [52], mixed CML–
deep learning for interpretability [53], and ‘‘what-if’’ scenario planning 
[18]. Parallel to these, Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently 
shown ability to infer causal relations from unstructured text and 
metadata [16], offering a path to extract external drivers at scale.
Despite these advances, three gaps remain for SCM: (i) a lack of an 
end-to-end, empirically validated workflow that benchmarks multiple 
causal discovery algorithms and grounds them in expert knowledge; (ii) 
limited integration of unstructured external event data using LLMs; and 
(iii) scarce validation of learned graphs against expert-derived ground 
truth. Table 2 summarizes representative approaches and contrasts 
them with our design-science framework that integrates CML with LLM-
based external factor extraction and expert validation, and links the 
resulting structures to KPI-based supplier risk scoring (cf. Research 
Objectives 1, 2, 3).
Beyond the novelty of the integrated pipeline, each component 
also advances the state of the art within its own stream. On the 
CML side, prior SCM applications [18,52,53] typically commit to a 
single causal discovery method and do not benchmark alternatives, 
validate against expert-derived ground truth, or apply formal refuta-
tion tests. Our contribution is a systematic comparison of six causal 
discovery algorithms-spanning constraint-based (PC, FCI), score-based 
(GES), linear non-Gaussian (DirectLiNGAM), and nonlinear additive-
noise approaches (NOTEARS, RESIT)— evaluated against an expert 
graph constructed from 18 domain experts and subjected to three 
refutation tests (random common cause, random subset, placebo) in the 
automotive supply-chain context. This lifts CML in SCM from single-
algorithm case studies to a benchmarked, empirically validated design. 
On the LLM side, Kıcıman et al. [16] established that LLMs can extract 
causal relations from unstructured text in general settings, but did 
not translate this capability into an operational SCM pipeline. Our 
contribution is to (i) apply LLM-based causal extraction specifically 
to automotive supplier risk using external event data (GDELT), (ii) 
embed the LLM in a Node-RED workflow that renders every generated

3. Methods and materials
Building on the extensive insights from related work and identified 
research gaps discussed in Section 2.2, we now present our empirical 
approach.
3.1. Proposed approach
The following section details a robust methodology that integrates 
causal machine learning (CML) and large language models (LLMs) to 
tackle supply chain risk management challenges. We begin by dis-
cussing the selected data preprocessing techniques and causal discovery 
algorithms, then by exploring evaluation metrics for assessing causal 
discovery outcomes. Additionally, we cover key aspects of causal esti-
mand identification, causal effect estimation, and refutation techniques 
to ensure the robustness of our findings. Recognizing the dynamic 
nature of supply chains, we also include methods for analyzing external 
factors that may influence risk and decision-making. Beyond detailing 
the mechanics of these algorithms and techniques, this section empha-
sizes the rationale behind their selection—highlighting why they are 
particularly suited for tackling complex, real-world supply chain risks.
In our research, we developed a comprehensive methodology to 
integrate causal machine learning (CML) and large language models 
(LLMs) for supply chain risk management. The methodology covers all 
steps from data collection to simulation and risk score development 
with the help of LLM. The methodology is illustrated in the flowchart 
for the analysis depicted in Fig. 1. The flow is divided into two main 
parts: Internal KPI analysis flow (cf. blue rectangle in Fig. 1) and exter-
nal (cf. green rectangle in Fig. 1). Internal KPI analysis uses CML, while 
the analysis of external factors influencing internal KPIs combines LLM 
and CML. Solid arrows indicate the relationships between steps and 
dashed arrows indicate the techniques applied at each step. The outputs 
of LLM-based external factor extraction and expert-derived ground 
truth are compared at the Graph Comparison & Evaluation Interface (cf. 
purple rectangle, Step 12, Fig. 1), which represents the design-science 
artefact integrating the CML and LLM pipelines before proceeding to 
the Causal NLP and risk score development stages.
The process in the developed methodology begins with collecting 
data from internal and external sources. Internal data is supplier KPI 
data from a leading automotive company. Meanwhile, external data is 
obtained from GDELT. More details about data collection are presented 
in Section 3.2. The internal KPI analysis aims to understand and mea-
sure the factors influencing supplier performance and identify causal 
relationships between various performance indicators using CML. Each 
step of this internal analysis is described in Section 3.3. In contrast, 
analyzing external factors aims to evaluate and understand their im-
pact on supplier performance and overall supply chain operations by 
utilizing LLMs. The steps for analyzing external KPIs are described in 
Section 3.4

For the internal dataset, which covers 12 months, missing values

These preprocessing steps enhance the scalability and robustness 
of causal discovery, particularly when leveraging LLMs for extracting 
insights from text. They also address limitations of traditional methods, 
such as the PC algorithm, by ensuring that the input data is both clean 
and representative of real-world conditions.
3.2.2. Data exploration
The second step of the process is data exploration (cf. Fig. 1, step 2) 
that aims to analyze the relationships between various supplier perfor-
mance KPIs in the internal data using correlation analysis. In this study, 
we calculate the Pearson Correlation Coefficient, which measures the 
linear relationship between pairs of variables. The choice of Pearson 
over rank-based alternatives (Spearman, Kendall) or nonlinear mea-
sures (e.g., distance correlation, mutual information) is deliberate and 
reflects the narrow role the correlation step plays here: it is used only 
as an exploratory screen to show that pairwise linear associations are 
weak or missing for some of the pairs of interest (notably Wrong_De-
liveries and Backlog), motivating the shift to causal discovery. 
Rank-based metrics such as Spearman and Kendall would have added 
monotonicity robustness but would not have changed this conclusion, 
and nonlinear association measures would have blurred the specific 
point being made — namely, that a linear screen is insufficient for this 
data and a structural causal-discovery step is needed, which is precisely 
why RESIT (a nonlinear additive-noise method) was selected for the 
subsequent analysis (Section 3.3.1). The Pearson correlation coefficient

3.3.2. Ground truth graph construction
This step aims to create a causal graph representing the ground truth 
to be compared with causal graphs generated by causal discovery algo-
rithms. A combination of literature analysis, surveys, and interviews 
was conducted to construct the causal graph for the external factors 
influencing the KPIs (cf. Fig. 1, step 10) as well as the internal KPIs (cf. 
Fig. 1, step 4). Table 6 lists ground truth causal relationships among 
variables in the internal KPI dataset based on literature, whereas Table 
7 shows ground truth causal relationships among external factors and 
internal KPIs.
The causal relationships identified from the literature were val-
idated through expert interviews to ensure that the literature-based 
ground truth accurately reflects real-world conditions. Twenty experts 
were interviewed; eighteen took part and completed the questionnaire. 
These experts, with a combined professional experience exceeding 300 
years, represented diverse domains such as logistics, quality manage-
ment, material planning, supply chain operations, and data science. 
Table 8 provides more details regarding the expertise of the experts 
and their corresponding titles. This diversity in expertise was critical 
to capturing the multifaceted nature of supply chain challenges and 
ensuring a robust causal model. For instance, material planners offered 
deep insights into how seasonal leaves and hiring temporary workers 
contribute to human errors, while logistics managers refined causal 
connections between strikes, roadblocks, and their cascading impact on 
backlogs.
Moreover, experts were deliberately selected based on their exten-
sive industry experience and proven expertise in critical supply chain 
domains. The selection process was designed to cover all key areas of 
supply chain operations, ensuring that sectors such as procurement, 
logistics, material planning, quality management, and data analytics 
were thoroughly represented. This comprehensive approach provided a 
well-rounded perspective that enriched the construction of the ground 
truth causal graph and reinforced the robustness of our model.
The importance of this diversity in expert selection aligns with 
findings by Sezer et al. [96], who emphasize that knowledge-based 
supply chains enriched by expert inputs play a critical role in enhancing 
resilience and sustainability. Their study demonstrates that engaging 
experts from varied domains contributes significantly to building com-
prehensive and reliable causal models by incorporating a wide range of 
perspectives and domain-specific knowledge. Following this principle, 
our approach ensured that each expert’s unique perspective enriched 
the causal diagram construction process, minimizing potential biases 
and improving the model’s validity.
To minimize bias introduced by human judgment, the ground-truth 
construction followed a documented protocol with three elements. (1)
Standardized instrument : every expert received the same questionnaire 
and interview script, so that differences in responses reflect differences 
in judgment rather than in how the question was asked. (2) Independent 
review before aggregation: experts completed the questionnaire individ-
ually, without seeing each other’s responses, so that consensus (when 
it arose) was not an artefact of social conformity or the first-speaker 
effect common in group elicitation. (3) Multi-expert agreement threshold: 
a causal link was retained in the ground-truth graph only when en-
dorsed by multiple experts; links supported by a single respondent were 
flagged for discussion rather than accepted. Functional diversity across 
logistics, material planning, quality management, procurement, and 
supply chain analytics (cf. Table 8) further reduced the risk that a single 
operational lens would dominate the resulting structure. This protocol 
aligns with the best practices outlined by Sezer et al. [96] for engaging 
diverse expert knowledge in supply chain causal modeling, and is 
consistent with the broader emphasis on structured expert elicitation 
and human bias as an explicit evaluation dimension applied in our prior 
work on the same automotive supply-chain domain [97].
We note one residual source of bias that the above protocol does 
not fully eliminate: all 18 experts are employed by the same automo-
tive company that provided the internal KPI data. Within-organization 
functional diversity mitigates single-function groupthink, but cross-
organizational perspectives — for example from tier-2 suppliers, logis-
tics providers, or other OEMs are not represented in the ground-truth 
graph. This limitation is acknowledged again in Section 6.1, where we 
identify cross-organizational expert elicitation as a direction for future 
work.

3.3.4. Causal estimand identification
Having completed the causal discovery process to identify relation-
ships between variables, we now move to causal inference methods to 
estimate and validate causal effects.
Causal estimand identification (cf. Fig. 1, step 6) defines which vari-
ables and paths to adjust for isolating the causal effect of a treatment 
(cause) on an outcome (effect) [105]. This process ensures accurate 
estimation of the causal impact of an intervention by using criteria 
based on causal assumptions. Key criteria to identify a causal estimand 
include:
1. Back-door criterion adjusts for confounders to block non-causal 
paths between treatment and outcome, isolating the direct causal 
effect [106].
2. Front-door criterion uses mediators to capture the causal path-
way when direct confounder adjustment is not feasible [107].
3. Instrumental Variables (IV) employs external variables that 
influence the treatment without affecting the outcome directly, 
useful when unobserved confounders are present [108].
4. Mediation (Direct and indirect effect identification) decom-
poses the total causal effect into direct and indirect effects, 
where intermediaries mediate the influence on the outcome 
[109].
In this study, the back-door criterion is applied. The choice is 
deliberate: the front-door criterion requires a fully mediating variable 
not confounded with the outcome, which our KPI set does not cleanly 
provide; instrumental variables require an external variable affecting 
the treatment but not the outcome, which is difficult to justify in 
our operational supplier data; and mediation decomposition targets 
direct-versus-indirect effect separation, which is not the quantity of 
interest here. With the main confounders of Backlog on Escalation 
(Missing Parts, Wrong Deliveries, Special Transport) observable in the 
internal KPI dataset, the back-door criterion is the appropriate and 
minimally assumption-laden choice. This approach blocks non-causal 
paths, isolating the true causal effect. For a set of variables 𝑍 satisfying 
the back-door criterion, the causal effect of treatment 𝑇 on outcome 𝑌
is represented in Eq. (7).

3.3.7. Counterfactual scenario - supplier performance simulations
Causal estimation quantifies the effect of a treatment (intervention) 
on an outcome, while counterfactual inference simulates alternative 
scenarios to predict what might have happened had the treatment 
been different. Together, these methodologies provide a robust un-
derstanding of both observed and hypothetical causal dynamics [42,
115]. Counterfactual inference, as a natural extension of causal effect 
estimation, plays a critical role in simulating hypothetical outcomes 
based on causal models. Building on the causal estimates derived in 
Sections 3.3.4 and 3.3.5, counterfactual scenarios allow exploration of 
alternative realities by altering treatment variables (T) and assessing 
their impact on outcome variables (Y).
The goal of a counterfactual scenario (cf. Fig. 1, step 9) is to 
simulate alternative outcomes by altering certain variables (interven-
tions) within the system to understand their potential impact on the 
observed data. This process helps predict how changes in supplier 
performance metrics would affect the overall system, providing insights 
for decision-making and strategic planning.
In this research, we employ the DoWhy-GCM4 package that has 
a whatif module for counterfactual analysis. It computes counter-
factuals and forecasts the consequences of interventions [44]. The 
input of the counterfactual analysis is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) 
showing the causal links among the variables in the system, together 
with tabular observational data for these variables, to use DoWhy-
GCM. What-if simulations and causal queries can be done, including 
assessments of complex interventions and counterfactuals at the point 
or population level, once a graphical causal model (GCM) has been 
fitted.
counterfactual_samples function in the DoWhy-GCM pack-
age is used to develop the counterfactual scenario. It explores ‘‘what-if’’ 
scenarios by predicting what would have happened if certain conditions 
in a system were different. It allows us to simulate changes (inter-
ventions) in a variable and observe how that change would impact 
the outcome, all based on an established causal model. For instance, 
it can help answer questions like ‘‘What if a supply chain delay had 
not occurred?’’ and estimate the resulting impact on other performance 
metrics. Mathematically, a counterfactual scenario can be represented 
in Eq. (13). 
𝑌𝑋=𝑥 = 𝑓 (𝑋 = 𝑥, 𝑈) (13)
where 𝑌𝑋=𝑥 is the outcome (𝑌 ) that would happen if the intervention 𝑋
were set to a specific value 𝑥, 𝑓 is the function (causal model or causal 
graph) that describes how variables interact, and 𝑈 represents the other 
factors or random influences that stay the same in the counterfactual 
scenario.
The result of the function is the estimated counterfactual data. 
Using the counterfactual_samples function, we can compute 
counterfactuals in three steps, as described by [116].
• Step 1 (Abduction): Inferring the latent variables or unobserved 
factors (𝑈 ) from the observed data.
• Step 2 (Action): Simulating the effect of an intervention (e.g., set-
ting 𝑋 = 𝑥) by altering the values in the structural equations.
• Step 3 (Prediction): Make predictions about the future (𝑌 ) based

To refine the LLM’s causal inference capability, we tested a se-

model to identify causal links, but results were often vague or incom-

chain-of-thought approach.
Next, we enforced structured output — clearly defined ‘‘Cause →
Effect’’ pairs — to reduce ambiguity and increase interpretability. Later, 
we added contextual examples to guide the model with templates 
of correct causal inferences. This iterative refinement improved the 
accuracy, clarity, and completeness of generated causal graphs.
Each prompt adjustment led to measurable gains in result quality. 
The shift from open-ended prompts to structured reasoning helped the 
model better capture variable relationships, reducing omissions and 
logical errors. The explicit format enabled easier validation of causal 
claims.
The ‘‘A causes B’’ structure made outputs more precise and readable. 
Including contextual examples reduced common errors like confusing 
correlation with causation. These refinements addressed limitations of 
earlier approaches, yielding causal diagrams that were more logical and 
aligned with real-world dynamics.
This process aims to auto-generate a comprehensive causal network, 
incorporating known and novel causal links, particularly from external 
factors. Using Node-RED and GPT-3.5, we systematically explored how 
external disruptions (e.g., strikes, seasonal changes) impact supplier 
KPIs (e.g., backlogs, delivery delays), producing an updated causal 
model. This enhances understanding of external–internal supply chain 
interactions, supporting better decisions and interventions.

to guide models effectively applies to causal diagram generation as 
well. In the context of supply chain risk management, HPT can be 
used to design prompts that reflect hierarchical dependencies among 
disruptions and key performance indicators. For example, a top-level 
prompt could represent external disruptions such as strikes leading

3.4.1.5. Reconciling LLM use with the interpretability goal. A reasonable 
concern arises from embedding an LLM inside a pipeline whose purpose 
is to improve interpretability over black-box ML: LLMs are themselves 
opaque, layered models, and their internal reasoning is not directly 
inspectable [121]. Our design mitigates this tension at three levels 
rather than by treating the LLM itself as interpretable.
First, the LLM is not used as the decision model. Its role is confined 
to proposing causal structure from unstructured text; the resulting graph 
is a symbolic, fully inspectable DAG that is then subjected to the 
same expert validation, refutation testing, and counterfactual analysis 
as the CML-derived graphs (cf. Sections 3.3.6 and 3.3.7). The decision-
relevant artefact — the graph and the risk score built on it — is 
transparent even when its upstream source is not.
Second, the LLM output is structurally constrained and externally 
auditable. Prompts require a fixed [A] --> [B] syntax
(Section 3.4.1.1), and the Node-RED workflow renders every generated 
edge as a visible node in a flow that a user can inspect, edit, or 
reject. This converts free-form LLM generation into a step that produces 
traceable, diff-able artefacts rather than opaque embeddings or scores.
Third, we triangulate LLM output against expert ground truth 
both structurally (edge-level overlap, Section 4.2.1) and qualitatively 
(domain-expert review of interpretability and actionability,
Section 4.2.1). Where the LLM and experts disagree, the expert struc-
ture is retained—the LLM accelerates construction but does not over-
ride domain validation.
This layered approach aligns with recent work on LLM interpretabil-
ity, which argues that for high-stakes applications the practical route 
is not to make LLMs internally transparent but to constrain their 
outputs to auditable forms and validate them externally [121,122]. Our 
pipeline instantiates that principle: the LLM is a generative proposer, 
the DAG is the interpretable object, and the experts remain the final 
arbiters. With these safeguards in place, the validated LLM-derived 
structure can be operationalized in downstream decision artefacts such 
as the risk score described next.

score.

3.4.3. Weighted sum model
Our research determines the risk score using the Weighted Sum 
Model (WSM) (cf. Fig. 1, step 14). WSM was selected over more elabo-
rate multi-criteria decision-making methods such as AHP or TOPSIS, 
including recent hybrid ML+MCDM designs for explainable supplier 
evaluation in Supply Chain Analytics [123], for three reasons: (i) our cri-
teria weights are derived from causal ATE values rather than pairwise 
expert comparisons, so the pairwise-comparison machinery central to 
AHP is not required and would introduce an additional elicitation step 
not otherwise needed; (ii) WSM produces a transparent, additive score 
that is directly interpretable by supply-chain managers as ‘‘weighted 
causal impact’’, in keeping with the interpretability goal of the overall 
pipeline; and (iii) because WSM is linear in the weights, any change 
in an ATE or a weight propagates into the score in a traceable way, 
which is essential for the auditable risk-score use case. The assumption 
WSM carries is that the criteria contribute additively and independently 
to the overall risk score; given that the causal DAG already separates 
direct from indirect paths, this assumption is consistent with how the 
ATE values enter the score. The score for each alternative (labeled as 
𝑊 𝑆𝑖) is calculated by adding together the values of different criteria 
(labeled as 𝑎𝑖𝑗 ), where each criteria value is multiplied by its respective 
weight (labeled as 𝑤𝑗 ), reflecting its importance [124].
The WSM-score is defined in Eq. (19).

In our research, 𝑎𝑖𝑗 represents the Average Treatment Effect (ATE),

4.2.2. Risk score application
In addition, we developed an application (cf. Fig. 7) that integrated 
external textual-data from GDELT with causal graphs generated from 
GPT-3.5. This application utilized a Weighted Sum Model (WSM) to 
compute a risk score (cf. Section 3.4.3). The key to this approach 
was assigning weights to the variables based on Average Treatment 
Effects (ATE), which were determined from the GDELT data using 
the CausalNLP package (cf. Section 3.4.2). The combination of these 
causal graphs and real-world data allowed us to create a risk score that 
captured the underlying causal relationships between the variables, 
offering a data-driven measure of risk.
4.3. Interactive risk score application
The developed application (cf. Fig. 7) corresponds to the risk evalua-
tion stage in Fig. 1 (steps 13–14), demonstrating how the outputs from 
the design-science artefact (Step 12) are operationalized for decision 
support. These include supplier-specific risks like financial instability 
(e.g., bankruptcy) and operational disruptions (e.g., shutdowns), as 
well as external risks such as natural disasters, pandemics, and labor 
shortages. Each edge in the causal graph reflects a dependency, making 
complex relationships clearer.
Users can explore these links by selecting internal KPIs and cor-
responding external factors via dropdown menus and adjusting edge 
strengths using a slider (cf. Fig. 7). The selected weight is assigned 
to the WSM formula as 𝑤𝑗 (cf. Eq. (19)). For example, modifying 
the weight between bankruptcy and shutdown enables users to assess 
how one factor impacts another, offering a dynamic view of cascading 
effects across the supply chain. Risk scores are updated in real-time. If 
risk score is less, the graph highlights the score and edge in green (cf. 
Fig. 7(a)). Scores trigger red highlights to alert users of elevated risk 
(cf. Fig. 7(b)).
This interface enables what-if simulations, allowing users to test 
how different risk intensities affect the supply chain’s overall vulner-
ability. By facilitating proactive analysis, the tool supports data-driven 
decisions that enhance supply chain resilience.
The contribution of this application to the literature is twofold. First, 
it is, to the best of our knowledge, the first supply-chain risk-scoring 
tool that derives its variable set and causal structure from an LLM-
generated graph aligned against an expert ground truth, rather than 
from a fixed predefined taxonomy of risk factors. This makes the set of 
factors the score responds to extensible without re-modeling. Second, 
the score is not a black-box output: because it is built on an auditable 
DAG with explicit ATE-based weights, managers can trace any change 
in the score back to a specific causal path and a specific external driver, 
addressing the transparency gap repeatedly flagged in recent SCM-AI 
reviews [17,35].
Although preliminary, this risk score prototype demonstrates how 
LLM-derived causal graphs and external data can inform risk evalua-
tion. The approach remains adaptable and can be refined for domain-
specific applications. Future implementations may integrate more nu-
anced factors or revised weight schemes to suit different industries.
In supply chain contexts, the score aids in forecasting and mitigating 
risks tied to unpredictable external drivers, such as geopolitical shifts or 
economic volatility. By providing causal insights, the tool helps organi-
zations preempt disruptions and operate more effectively in uncertain 
environments (cf. Research Objective 3).
5. Discussion
5.1. Summary of findings and interpretation
In this study, various causal discovery algorithms (PC, GES, FCI, 
DirectLiNGAM, RESIT, and NOTEARS) have been explored. The results 
show that RESIT performed the best due to its ability to discover 
nonlinear and additive causal relationships. This makes sense since 
our data represents complex and nonlinear supply chain performance 
metrics and interactions. The performance of each algorithm varied, but 
FDR and SHD metrics support the superiority of RESIT. Additionally, 
the LLM-generated causal graph indicated significant overlap with the 
expert-derived ground truth. Thus, it validates the potential of LLMs 
in causal discovery for supply chains. In addition to structural align-
ment with expert graphs, experts reported that LLM-augmented outputs 
required fewer refinements during validation and were consistently 
described as more interpretable and actionable. These process efficien-
cies represent quantifiable advantages of LLM support, complementing 
traditional accuracy metrics such as FDR or SHD.
Incorporating external data (GDELT) with internal KPIs demon-
strates that while external factors influence supply chain operations, 
they are not always evident through traditional methods. By applying 
causal NLP and the Weighted Sum Model, we calculated risk scores 
that help quantify the influence of these factors on internal KPIs such 
as backlog and escalation. This integration of CML and LLMs provided 
more comprehensive insights into supply chain risk management. This 
cannot be achieved using traditional machine learning models [26,33,
35,36].
Furthermore, both direct and indirect causal relationships could 
be identified by integrating CML and LLMs. The traditional ML and

5.2. Key considerations
Our findings, though encouraging, require careful interpretation in 
light of several critical considerations, including model uncertainty, 
the evolving nature of large language models (LLMs), data quality, 
reproducibility, and practical implementation challenges. Model un-
certainty remains a core issue in predictive analytics. Understanding 
the confidence levels associated with model outputs is fundamental for 
scientific rigor and decision-making [128]. By acknowledging forecast 
uncertainty, we align our methodology with established best practices 
in model validation. In real-world applications, predictions should be 
contextualized using confidence intervals or predictive ranges to inform 
actions, preventing overreliance on deterministic outputs. Such trans-
parency is crucial for building trust in AI-driven systems, particularly 
where erroneous decisions may carry high operational or financial costs 
[129].
The integration of LLMs adds both capabilities and concerns. These 
models offer advanced language comprehension and generation, sig-
nificantly enriching analysis. However, they are prone to limitations 
including absorption of data biases and generation of hallucinated 
content [130,131]. This necessitates rigorous human oversight. In our 
study, all LLM-generated insights were reviewed by domain experts and 
subjected to structured verification protocols. We recommend caution 
when interpreting surprising outputs from LLMs and suggest corrobo-
rating them through additional data or expert consultation.
Data quality is another foundational pillar [7]. Our pipeline in-
cluded extensive preprocessing noise filtering, deduplication, and stan-
dardization to ensure consistency and reduce bias propagation. Nonethe-
less, potential issues like hidden confounders or representational gaps 
could impact findings. Hence, future adopters should emphasize data 
governance through continuous validation, cleaning, and representa-
tiveness assessments.
Due to confidentiality and policy constraints, we have withheld 
certain experimental details, including exact time spans, heteroge-
neous data fusion processes, and proprietary code. While we provide 
transparency in terms of preprocessing steps and evaluation criteria, 
replication may be partially limited. Future research should prioritize 
open science practices to improve reproducibility and foster robust, 
generalizable AI systems.
A further consideration concerns computational cost and scalability. 
The pipeline reported here runs on a standard laptop without spe-
cialized hardware: the six causal discovery algorithms, the refutation 
tests, and the counterfactual simulations all operate on a small number 
of KPIs over 12 months of internal data, and none of these steps is 
the bottleneck. The two components most sensitive to scale are the 
LLM call and the expert validation loop. The LLM is accessed as an 
external service, so local compute is negligible, but wall-clock time 
and cost grow linearly with prompt volume, and API-level rate limits 
become the binding constraint when the framework is extended to 
many suppliers or refreshed at high frequency. The expert validation 
step scales even less gracefully: moving from 18 experts to a larger or 
cross-organizational panel multiplies elicitation effort roughly linearly 
in the number of experts and quadratically in the number of candidate 
causal links under review. Real-time deployment with a larger KPI set 
or a broader supplier portfolio would therefore require three additions 
not tested here: batched or cached LLM calls to manage API throughput, 
nonlinear causal discovery algorithms (RESIT in particular) run on a 
server rather than a laptop as variable count grows, and an incre-
mental validation workflow so that experts re-review only changed 
edges rather than the full graph. These are engineering steps rather 
than methodological barriers, but we flag them explicitly because the 
results in this paper establish feasibility at the scale reported, not at 
operational scale.
5.3. Theoretical, managerial, and policy implications
5.3.1. Theoretical implications
This study contributes to the supply-chain analytics literature in 
three ways. First, it extends causal machine learning in SCM from 
single-algorithm case studies to a systematically benchmarked, expert-
validated design, answering a gap flagged by recent SCM-CML work 
[18,53]. Second, it shows that the expressive power of LLMs can 
be combined with the structural discipline of causal DAGs without 
surrendering interpretability, provided the LLM is used as a proposer 
of structure rather than as a decision model (Section 3.4.1.5). This has 
implications beyond SCM for any domain where unstructured exter-
nal signals must enter a causal decision pipeline. Third, by showing 
that RESIT aligns best with expert-derived ground truth on nonlinear 
operational KPI data, the study offers empirical evidence for method-
selection guidance in applied causal discovery — an area that has been 
largely theoretical.
5.3.2. Managerial implications
For supply-chain managers, the findings translate into a concrete set 
of practice changes. First, the pipeline produces lever-level recommen-
dations (e.g., reducing Backlog by 50%–70% lowers escalation), which 
is a different decision artefact from the accuracy-focused outputs of 
traditional ML and is usable directly in supplier-escalation reviews. Sec-
ond, the LLM step compresses the causal-graph construction cycle from 
weeks of expert interviews to hours of review, which enables risk struc-
tures to be refreshed as external conditions change rather than being 
fixed at a point in time [16,132]. Third, adoption should be treated as a 
strategic transformation rather than a technical upgrade: a phased roll-
out with pilot studies, modular cloud-hosted components, and domain 
experts kept in the validation loop is recommended, and staff should be 
trained to interpret probabilistic outputs rather than read them as de-
terministic predictions [133,134]. Finally, economic planning around 
cost–benefit analyzes, milestone-based rollouts, and predefined success 
metrics is essential to convert the pipeline’s analytical advantages into 
durable value [135].
5.3.3. Policy recommendations
The study surfaces three issues that are better addressed at the 
policy level than by individual firms. First, expert-grounded valida-
tion of causal AI in operational contexts requires cross-organizational 
knowledge sharing (tier-2 suppliers, logistics providers, OEMs), yet 
current data-handling norms treat supplier data as strictly bilateral; 
policy frameworks that enable structured, privacy-preserving knowl-
edge pooling — building on established data-governance standards 
such as GDPR, CCPA, and ISO 27001 — would materially improve the 
robustness of industry-wide causal models [136,137]. Second, because 
LLM-assisted pipelines depend on external event data (e.g., GDELT), 
continued public investment in open event-data infrastructure is a 
precondition for the external-factor branch of such pipelines to remain 
viable. Third, as causal AI moves into supplier-escalation decisions, 
regulators should consider minimum standards for auditability of AI-
driven decisions: requiring that any AI-assisted supplier action be trace-
able to an inspectable causal path, as our pipeline is designed to 
support, would align emerging AI-governance expectations with the 
operational reality of SCM adoption.

6. Conclusions
This study set out to enhance operational decision-making in supply-
chain risk management by integrating causal machine learning (CML) 
with large language models (LLMs), validated against expert-derived 
ground truth in the automotive sector. The findings are presented point 
by point below, together with the significance of each.
1. RESIT is the best-aligned causal-discovery algorithm for 
operational supplier KPIs. Among the six algorithms bench-
marked (PC, FCI, GES, DirectLiNGAM, NOTEARS, RESIT), RESIT 
achieved the closest alignment with the expert-derived ground-
truth graph, with a False Discovery Rate of 0.1429, Structural 
Hamming Distance of 1, precision and recall of 0.8571, and an 
F1 score of 0.85 (Section 3.3.3). Significance: this provides the 
first empirically anchored guidance in SCM on which family of 
causal-discovery algorithms to prefer when KPIs are nonlinear 
and mixed-type, shifting CML in SCM from single-algorithm case 
studies to method-selection evidence.
2. The estimated causal effect of Backlog on Escalation is ro-
bust. Three refutation tests — random common cause, random 
subset, and placebo — returned p-values of 0.96, 0.76, and 
0.98 respectively (Section 4.1.6), indicating that the estimate is 
insensitive to synthetic confounders, data subsampling, and spu-
rious signal. Significance: the causal quantity on which supplier-
escalation decisions would be based survives standard sensitivity 
perturbations, which is the minimum bar for operational use.
3. Counterfactual reduction of Backlog by 50%–70% consis-
tently lowers escalation across suppliers. The counterfactual 
analysis (Section 3.3.7) produces lever-level recommendations 
rather than accuracy-focused predictions. Significance: this is a 
qualitatively different decision artefact from traditional ML out-
puts and directly supports the escalation/de-escalation choices 
supply-chain managers actually have to make.
4. LLM-generated causal graphs align structurally with the 
expert ground truth and accelerate construction. The GPT-
3.5-generated graph shows strong structural overlap with the 
18-expert reference graph, and was produced in minutes rather 
than the hours of interviews required for the expert baseline 
(Section 4.2.1). Significance: LLMs can compress the causal-graph 
construction cycle enough to make periodic refreshes feasible as 
external conditions change, which is a prerequisite for keeping 
risk models current.
5. Interpretability is preserved when the LLM is used as a 
proposer rather than a decision model. By confining the 
LLM’s role to proposing causal structure and routing its output 
through an auditable Node-RED DAG validated by experts (Sec-
tion 3.4.1.5), the pipeline retains the transparency advantage 
of CML despite incorporating an opaque upstream component.
Significance: this resolves the apparent tension between CML 
(transparent) and LLMs (opaque) and offers a reusable design 
principle beyond SCM.
6. LLM-derived external factors linked to internal KPIs through 
a Weighted Sum Model produce an auditable, causally
grounded risk score. The interactive application (Section 4.3) 
lets managers trace any change in the risk score back to a specific 
external driver and causal path. Significance: to the best of our 
knowledge, this is the first SCM risk-scoring tool whose variable 
set and causal structure are derived from an LLM-generated 
graph validated against an expert ground truth, rather than from 
a fixed predefined risk taxonomy.
Taken together, these six findings constitute the contribution of the 
paper: a CML + LLM pipeline that is benchmarked, expert-validated, 
sensitivity-tested, and operationalized in a decision-relevant risk score 
for the automotive supply chain, with each design choice anchored to 
a specific problem in prior work. Despite these contributions, certain 
limitations must be acknowledged.
6.1. Limitations
Firstly, the proposed methodology is validated within a specific 
industry context using select models and datasets. Broader applicability 
should be explored by testing across domains and varying data condi-
tions. Relatedly, the expert panel used to construct the ground-truth 
graph, while functionally diverse, was drawn from a single automotive 
company; extending elicitation to experts from other organizations 
(tier-2 suppliers, logistics providers, other OEMs) would further re-
duce organizational bias in the reference graph. Secondly, although 
model uncertainty was discussed, formal quantification (e.g., through 
predictive intervals) was not performed. Additionally, we did not assess 
stability metrics for LLM-generated graphs.
Temporal and domain limitations in LLM training data may con-
strain relevance in rapidly evolving contexts. Furthermore, while par-
tial code and methods are shared, full reproducibility may be hindered 
by proprietary elements and system-specific configurations.
We also acknowledge that external datasets like GDELT may not 
capture all factors influencing supply chains, particularly qualitative or 
latent variables. While LLM-generated graphs showed alignment with 
expert-derived structures, they require refinement in high-stakes or 
domain-sensitive cases [134]. Hallucinations remain a known issue in 
LLM outputs [138], necessitating mechanisms such as fact-checking, 
ensemble modeling, or secondary validation layers. Building on these 
limitations, future research can extend this work in several directions.
6.2. Future directions
Several concrete lines of work would extend this study.
Cross-organizational expert validation. The ground-truth graph in 
this paper was built from 18 experts within a single automotive com-
pany. A natural next step is to replicate the elicitation with experts from 
tier-2 suppliers, logistics providers, and other OEMs, to test whether 
the expert-derived structure is stable across organizational perspec-
tives or whether systematic disagreements emerge at organizational 
boundaries. A successful outcome would be a cross-organizational ref-
erence graph whose high-confidence edges are retained across panels, 
and whose disagreements identify where firm-specific causal structure 
begins.
Comparative benchmarking of LLM-based versus traditional causal 
inference. Future studies should conduct head-to-head evaluations of 
LLM-proposed and algorithm-derived causal structures using outcome-
relevant metrics — ATE bias against an agreed reference, intervention-
success rate in controlled settings, and implementation cost — rather 
than structural-overlap metrics alone. Given the known sensitivity 
of LLM outputs to prompt wording [139], developing standardized 
prompt templates for causal-graph elicitation, together with reporting 
protocols that disclose the exact prompt and model version, is a 
prerequisite for replicability.
Stability metrics for LLM-generated causal graphs. The internal-
KPI results in this paper include refutation-based sensitivity analysis of 
the causal effect, but the stability of LLM-generated graphs themselves 
was not quantified. Future work should measure LLM graph stability 
under prompt perturbation, model version, and temperature setting 
using metrics such as Jaccard similarity on edge sets and earth-mover 
distance on adjacency distributions. Combined with uncertainty esti-
mation (e.g., Bayesian networks or Monte Carlo dropout [140]), this 
would allow risk scores to carry explicit confidence bands rather than 
point values.
Scalability testing beyond a single firm and a single time win-
dow. The pipeline has been validated on one company’s data over 
twelve months. Extending it across industries (e.g., aerospace, phar-
maceuticals, consumer electronics) and across longer horizons would 
test whether the method-selection finding (RESIT aligning best with
